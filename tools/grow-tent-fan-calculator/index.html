What Fan Size That Means

Inline fans are sold by duct diameter, and the sensible reading of your result is "which size, run gently" rather than a precise CFM shopping target. These are the typical ranges for common consumer inline fans:

The row your result lands in is highlighted. Ratings vary by brand; check the manufacturer’s own figure rather than the category.

What to actually buy. The standard answer for a 4×4 is a 6-inch inline fan and carbon filter kit, because a 6-inch fan at 40% is quieter than a 4-inch at 100% and costs maybe twenty dollars more. Buy the filter matched to the fan’s duct size — a 4-inch filter bolted to a 6-inch fan re-creates the restriction you paid to avoid. If you are near the top of a size band, go up rather than buying the biggest fan in the smaller size.

Two Requirements, Not One

Most fan calculators do one sum: tent volume divided by an exchange interval. That is the right starting point and it is wrong on its own, because it is blind to how much heat you are putting into the box. A 2×4 tent with 600 W in it needs far more air than its 40-odd cubic feet suggest. So this calculator does both sums and takes the larger.

1. Air exchange

Tent volume in cubic feet divided by how many minutes you will allow per full air change. At a one-minute exchange the arithmetic is pleasingly circular: cubic feet is your CFM. This is the requirement that handles CO₂ replenishment and humidity removal.

2. Heat removal

The standard sensible-heat airflow equation from HVAC:

CFM = BTU/hr ÷ (1.08 × ΔT°F)
where BTU/hr = watts × 3.412

The 1.08 is not arbitrary: it is 60 minutes/hour × 0.075 lb/ft³ air density × 0.24 BTU/lb·°F specific heat, for standard air. Every watt you put into a sealed tent comes back out as heat, so wall wattage is a good proxy for the heat load — slightly conservative, since a little energy leaves as light through the tent walls and a lot of it leaves as latent heat in evaporated water, which this equation does not count.

The two requirements answer different questions and the honest thing is to satisfy both. If the heat number is the larger one, no amount of "exchange every three minutes" advice will keep your tent cool.

Where the Airflow Actually Goes

A fan rated 400 CFM is rated at zero static pressure — free air, nothing attached. Everything you connect to it takes a cut:

  • The carbon filter: 25% here. The single biggest deduction, and the one most people forget entirely. Worth knowing how soft this figure is: Can-Filters publishes a 10–25% airflow drop and a pressure drop of about 0.7 in. w.g. at rated flow, while AC Infinity publishes 60% for the filter. The default here sits between them. A loaded or undersized filter costs more either way, and a pre-filter sleeve keeps it near the low end for longer.
  • Every 90° bend: about 5%, and nobody really knows. This is the softest number on the page. Can-Filters puts a 90° bend at 1–4%; AC Infinity puts it at 60%. Those differ by a factor of fifteen to sixty, which is the tell that neither is a measurement. The actual engineering method is equivalent length rather than a percentage — a 6-inch smooth 90° elbow is worth about 1 ft 9 in of straight duct, while a 4-inch mitered one is worth 5 ft — so a typical tent elbow costs you a couple of equivalent feet, not a fixed share of your airflow. Two bends is normal; six means rethink the routing before buying a bigger fan.
  • Duct length: about 1% per foot of flexible duct. The corrugations are the problem — smooth rigid duct is roughly half as costly per foot. Also: pull the flexible duct taut. A concertina’d run left half-compressed behind the tent can cost more than the filter.
  • An inline silencer: about 10%. Also vendor lore — AC Infinity says 20% and publishes no pressure drop for its own silencers, and nothing in this market is rated to the standard that would settle it. Usually worth fitting anyway: quiet gear gets run at the speed it should be run at, rather than the speed you can stand.
  • Speed headroom: whatever you choose. Not a loss at all — a deliberate purchase of reserve. Sizing so the fan sits at 60–75% buys you a quieter tent, a longer-lived motor, and somewhere to go when July arrives.

One thing you will see on other calculators and will not see here is a derate for a hot or humid room. That one is simply backwards. A fan is a constant-volume machine: air density changes the pressure it develops and the power it draws, but not the volume of air it moves. What actually degrades in a hot room is how much heat each cubic foot can carry away, which is why the heat-removal cross-check above takes the room temperature into account through ΔT rather than by pretending the fan got weaker.

One deduction this calculator cannot model is a starved intake. If your passive intake openings do not total roughly three to four times the cross-sectional area of the exhaust duct, the fan spends its life pulling against a vacuum and delivers far less than any of the numbers above. A 6-inch duct is about 28 in², so it wants somewhere near 100 in² of open intake — which is more than the little mesh window on the front of most tents, and the reason experienced growers unzip a lower flap.

How This Calculator Works

The derates are applied as efficiency multipliers, not subtracted percentages, which matters once you stack several of them:

needed = max( volume ÷ interval , watts × 3.412 ÷ (1.08 × ΔT) )
rated  = needed ÷ (filter × bends × duct × silencer × speed)

Stacking a 25% filter loss and a 10% duct loss does not give 65% of rated flow, it gives 0.75 × 0.90 = 67.5%. Small difference here, larger once four or five factors pile up, and multiplying is the correct way round.

References. Air exchange rate: Bartok, J., Ventilation for Greenhouses, UMass Extension — "one volume air exchange per minute to a height of 8 feet for summer ventilation", dropping to about a quarter of that in winter. This is the basis for the one-minute default, and matches our own ventilation guide. Heat removal: the sensible-heat airflow relationship q = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT, rearranged; 1.08 = 60 min/h × 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 BTU/lb·°F for standard air, and 1 W = 3.412 BTU/hr. Worth noting that the current standard covering sealed indoor plant environments, ANSI/ASABE/ASHRAE EP653 (2021), sizes ventilation by heat and moisture load rather than by volume exchange — which is the same approach as the cross-check on this page, and the reason the cross-check exists. ANSI/ASAE EP406.4, still widely quoted for greenhouse airflow, has been withdrawn; its own §7.1.10 recommends specifying airflow at a stated static pressure (0.060 in. w.g.) rather than subtracting percentages, which is the technically correct approach and one that consumer inline fans make impossible because they publish single-point specs and no pressure curves. Derates: carbon filter from Can-Filters (10–25% airflow drop, ~0.7 in. w.g.) and AC Infinity (60%, self-disclaimed as "not fixed and may vary"); per-bend from the same two sources, which disagree by a factor of fifteen to sixty; equivalent-length figures for elbows from IRC Table M1502.4.5.1. These are vendor rules of thumb, not measured constants, which is why every one of them is an editable input. Constant volume: Twin City Fan engineering letter FE-1600 — "fan air volume (CFM) is not affected by the air density", the reason this calculator has no temperature derate. Fan ratings are quoted at zero static pressure by convention.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size fan do I need for a 4x4 grow tent?

A 4×4×6.5 ft tent is about 104 cubic feet, so exchanging the air once a minute needs 104 CFM at the tent. Once you account for a carbon filter, a couple of ducting bends and the headroom to run the fan below full speed, the fan you buy should be rated around 250–300 CFM — which in practice means a 6-inch inline fan rather than a 4-inch one.

How much CFM does a carbon filter cost me?

Roughly 25–30% of the fan’s rated airflow for a clean filter matched to the fan’s duct size. An undersized filter, or one that has loaded up with dust because it was run without a pre-filter, costs considerably more. The rating on the box is measured with nothing attached to the fan at all.

Should I buy a bigger fan and run it slower?

Almost always yes. A larger fan loafing at 40–60% is quieter, lasts longer, draws less power and leaves you headroom for summer. A small fan screaming at 100% is loud, has no reserve, and has nowhere to go when the room warms up. This is why the calculator divides by a target speed rather than sizing the fan to run flat out.

Do I need an intake fan as well?

Usually not. Passive intakes are simpler and produce the slight negative pressure that stops odour leaking out of the tent seams. What matters is that the passive intake openings total roughly three to four times the cross-sectional area of the exhaust duct, otherwise the fan is fighting for air and its real-world output drops well below anything this calculator predicts.

How often should the air in a grow tent be exchanged?

Once every one to three minutes is the standard range. Once a minute is the usual starting point and is what this calculator defaults to; a cool room with modest lighting can stretch to two or three minutes, while a hot room or a high-wattage fixture needs the faster end plus the heat-removal check.
