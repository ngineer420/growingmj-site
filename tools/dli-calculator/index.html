A caveat on that last row. There is no published biological optimum for cannabis DLI. The largest controlled trial to date tested a range from 5 to 78 mol/m²/day and found yield still rising linearly at the top of it, with no saturation point reached — the authors explicitly hand the decision to economics rather than physiology. So 30–45 here is "where the electricity, the heat load and the yield curve usually cross", not "where the plant stops responding". If you have the cooling and the CO₂, more appears to keep working; whether it pays is a different question from whether it grows.

What DLI Is, and Why Watts Are Not It

PPFD tells you how bright it is right now. DLI tells you how much light the plant actually received today. Photosynthesis works on photons accumulated over a day, not on instantaneous brightness, which is why two setups with identical PPFD can produce completely different plants if one runs 12 hours and the other runs 18.

DLI is measured in moles of photosynthetically active photons per square metre per day. A mole is 6.02 × 10²³ photons, which is an absurd-sounding unit until you realise a bright summer day outdoors delivers 40 to 60 of them and a north-facing windowsill in December delivers about 1.

Notice what is not in that definition: watts. A fixture’s wattage tells you what it costs to run, not what it delivers — two 200 W lights can differ by 60% in photon output, and the same fixture at two different hanging heights differs by more than that at canopy level. Any lighting decision made on watts, "equivalent watts", or lumens is a decision made with the wrong instrument. Lumens are weighted for the human eye, which is most sensitive to green — exactly the colour plants reflect.

Getting a PPFD Number You Can Trust

The calculation above is trivially correct. The input usually is not, and there are three common ways to feed it a bad number:

  • Using the manufacturer’s centre-point figure. PPFD charts quote the value directly under the middle of the fixture. Your plants are not all standing there. Take an average across the actual footprint — the corners of a 4×4 tent commonly read 40–60% of the centre.
  • Confusing PPF with PPFD. PPF (µmol/s) is the whole fixture’s output. PPFD (µmol/m²/s) is what lands on a square metre of your canopy. They are different numbers with similar names, and the bigger one is always the one on the box.
  • Trusting a phone app. Phone light meters read lux through a camera sensor with an unknown spectral response. Converting lux to PPFD requires a conversion factor specific to the light source, and getting it wrong by 30% is easy. A cheap dedicated quantum sensor is the only way to actually know.

If you have no meter at all, work the calculator backwards instead: pick the DLI your crop wants, set your photoperiod, and read off the PPFD you need. Then compare that against the fixture’s published PPFD map at your hanging height. It is not a measurement, but it is a much better guess than buying by wattage.

Trading Hours Against Intensity

Because DLI is a product, the same daily total can be reached several ways: 300 µmol/m²/s for 16 hours and 400 for 12 both land near 17 mol/m²/day. Longer and dimmer is often the cheaper route, since running LEDs at lower drive current is more efficient per photon and produces less heat for your exhaust fan to remove.

Two limits stop this from being a free trade. Photoperiod-sensitive plants read day length as a flowering signal independently of intensity, so you cannot extend hours on a short-day crop in flower without consequences — that is the whole subject of grow light schedules. And most crops have a saturation point where extra intensity simply stops converting into growth, which is why 45 mol/m²/day into a lettuce crop buys tipburn rather than lettuce. "Most", not "all" — see the caveat under the crop table.

How This Calculator Works

DLI (mol/m²/day) = PPFD (µmol/m²/s) × hours × 3600 ÷ 1,000,000
                 = PPFD × hours × 0.0036

The 3600 converts hours into seconds, because PPFD is a per-second rate. The 1,000,000 converts micromoles into moles. That is the entire derivation — there is no fudge factor and no proprietary adjustment, and any DLI calculator that produces a different answer from the same inputs is simply wrong.

The reverse modes rearrange the same equation: hours = DLI ÷ (PPFD × 0.0036), and PPFD = DLI ÷ (hours × 0.0036).

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate DLI from PPFD?

DLI (mol/m²/day) = PPFD (µmol/m²/s) × photoperiod (hours) × 3600 ÷ 1,000,000. The 3600 converts hours to seconds and the million converts micromoles to moles, so the whole thing collapses to PPFD × hours × 0.0036.

What DLI does lettuce need?

Lettuce and most leafy greens do well between about 12 and 17 mol/m²/day, with 17 the usual target where there is good vertical airflow. Below roughly 10 the heads stay loose and light. Tipburn risk starts climbing around 15, and without airflow to move air through the canopy the practical ceiling drops to about 12.

Is more DLI always better?

For most crops, no: they reach a saturation point past which extra light produces no extra growth and then starts producing damage — bleaching, tipburn and heat stress — while you pay full price for the electricity. Lettuce is the clearest example. But it is not universal. The largest published cannabis trial pushed DLI from 5 to 78 mol/m²/day and yield was still rising linearly at the top, with no saturation reached, so for that crop the limit found so far is economic rather than biological.

Can I get the same DLI with fewer hours and a brighter light?

Within limits, yes — DLI is a product, so 300 PPFD for 16 hours and 400 PPFD for 12 hours both give about 17 mol/m²/day. The limits are that photoperiod-sensitive plants use day length as a flowering signal regardless of intensity, and that very high instantaneous PPFD can saturate or bleach a crop even when the daily total is reasonable.

What is the difference between PPF and PPFD?

PPF is the total photon output of the fixture in µmol/s, measured in an integrating sphere. PPFD is how many of those photons land on a square metre of your canopy, in µmol/m²/s, and it depends on hanging height, reflectivity and where under the light you measure. DLI is built from PPFD, so use a canopy-level average and not the single brightest spot on the manufacturer’s chart.
