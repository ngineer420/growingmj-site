Why Are My Leaves Yellow?

A symptom-first identifier that answers with a shortlist rather than a diagnosis — because the honest answer to “which nutrient is it” is usually several, and often it is not a nutrient at all. Every claim below is tied to a published source, listed at the bottom.

What you can see

1. Which leaves went wrong first?
2. What does the affected tissue look like?
3. Anything else true of the plant right now?

Leave blank if you have not measured it. That is itself an answer, and the tool will say so.

Only useful if you know which extraction method your reading came from — the same substrate reads differently by PourThru, 1:2 and saturated media extract, so a bare number means little. Why that matters.

What This Tool Is Actually Doing

It applies two published rules and then refuses to pretend it knows more than they support.

The first rule is position. Nutrients the plant can move around inside itself get pulled out of old leaves to feed new growth when supply runs short, so their deficiencies show at the bottom of the plant. Nutrients it cannot move stay where they were deposited, so their deficiencies show at the top. Colorado State states the mechanism plainly for potassium — “Older leaves are affected first, because potassium is highly mobile within plants” — and inverts it for iron: “Because iron is not readily moved within plants once incorporated, symptoms appear on younger leaves and on new growth.”[6]

The second rule is pH before nutrients, which is gated ahead of everything else below. The rest is a symptom-to-cause mapping taken from the sources in the reference list, and a deliberate refusal to output a single confident answer.

Position First, Because Position Is the Cheap Half

Interveinal chlorosis — yellow between green veins — is the clearest example of why the location question comes before the appearance question. The same pattern means magnesium at the bottom of the plant and iron at the top, and NC State says exactly that in a single sentence: “Lower leaf interveinal chlorosis is the most commonly observed initial magnesium deficiency symptom. (An upper foliage interveinal chlorosis situation would initially point to an iron deficiency.)”[8]

So a photograph of one leaf, which is what most people bring to this problem, is missing the single most informative thing about it. Where the leaf was standing on the plant carries more diagnostic weight than what it looks like. Our deficiency guide covers the mobile-versus-immobile split in more depth.

Why pH Is Gated Ahead of Everything Else

Because a plant fed correctly can still starve. Iron is the textbook case: Colorado State reports that “for every unit of increase in pH, the solubility of iron decreases by a factor of 1,000,”[6] and that in high-pH soils iron “is not deficient in an absolute sense, rather, it is unavailable for plant uptake due to the soil’s high pH”[6] — and, in its companion note on woody plants, that “soil applications of iron alone are ineffective.”[7]

In containers the same thing happens faster. Ohio State describes iron going insoluble as pH climbs and notes the practical consequence: “iron deficiency symptoms frequently develop in floriculture crops that are being fertilized correctly, particularly when pH drifts above the crop’s target range,” and warns that “correcting chlorosis without verifying pH often leads to repeated iron applications with uneven results because the underlying chemistry has not changed.”[5]

This is why the tool leads with pH rather than mentioning it in a footnote. It is also the published diagnostic order, not an opinion of ours: NC State’s procedure for a suspected nutritional disorder begins “Step 1. … the first step is to test the substrate pH and electrical conductivity (EC),”[1] with substrate and tissue analysis as steps two and three. Symptoms are what tells you to measure. They are not the measurement.

Above the window, iron and the other micronutrients go out of reach first. NC State puts the working threshold at “elevated substrate pH-induced iron (Fe) deficiencies when the pH is above 6.5,”[24] and Michigan State recommends holding 5.5–5.8 in young plants specifically to prevent iron, manganese and boron deficiency.[9]

Below the window, the popular framing is wrong in an interesting way. Low pH in a soilless substrate does not mainly cause micronutrient deficiency — it causes micronutrient toxicity. NC State: “When the pH drops too low, the availability of micro-nutrients, especially iron and manganese, increase. If a plant is efficient at taking up these elements in excess, then the leaves develop symptoms.”[23] NC State documents it in Christmas cactus, where the first reading was “an iron (Fe) deficiency or magnesium deficiency”. Testing found substrate pH well below target — 4.4–4.7 in a 2015 case and 5.4 in a 2025 one — with leaf iron somewhere between about two and five times the sufficiency range, depending on which published standard you measure it against.[24] Either way it was iron poisoning, and dosing iron would have made it worse. What low pH genuinely does starve the plant of is calcium and magnesium.

A note on coco, because the usual explanation does not hold up

You will read everywhere that coco coir has a high cation exchange capacity that binds calcium and magnesium and causes lockout. We went looking for a published source for that and could not find one, and the primary literature points the other way: Handreck’s 1993 study measured coir’s cation exchange capacity at 21–30 mmol(+)/L against 78 for peat, and concluded this “indicates a low ability to retain cations and to buffer against pH change.”[27]

What is documented is compositional, and it lands in the same place for practical purposes without inventing a mechanism. Coir arrives loaded with potassium, sodium and chloride and nearly devoid of calcium, so Purdue advises that “coir-based substrates require more Ca, sulfur (S), Cu, and Fe; they also require less K,”[3] and Handreck found that with coir-based media “it was necessary to amend the media with Ca, S, Cu and Fe.”[27] So: yes, add calcium in coco. No, not because the coir is holding it hostage — because there was very little there to begin with. And no, we could not find any published source giving coco a pH target different from a peat mix, so this tool does not invent one.

While we are here: that nutrient availability chart

The banded pH-versus-availability chart reproduced on every grow site is older and shakier than its confident appearance suggests. It originates with N.A. Pettinger’s 1935 Virginia Agricultural Experiment Station Bulletin 136, sold at the time as a lecture poster.[25] Pettinger himself listed the limits on page 5: the differing widths of the bands “have no significance when compared with each other”; the chart illustrates “only the changes which take place in well-drained mineral soils of the humid regions” with “no claims … for its applications … to swampy or highly organic soils”; and the relationship “should be referred to as one of association rather than as one of cause and effect.”[25]

That second caveat is the one that matters for anyone growing in peat or coir, because it excludes exactly the media indoor growers use — and it is the author’s own caveat, not a later critic’s. The diagram has been revisited in the modern soil-science literature, and Hartemink and Barrow’s 2023 note in Plant and Soil[26] is where to go next — but it is paywalled, we could not read it, and we are not going to tell you what a paper says on the strength of its title. The 1935 caveats above are freely readable and sufficient on their own. This tool therefore uses target ranges published for container substrates, not the chart.

Why the Answer Is a Shortlist, and Why It Says “Likely” Rather Than a Percentage

Five questions cannot support a percentage. A figure like 87% implies a model calibrated against outcomes, and there is no such model here — only a published symptom-to-cause mapping and the position rule. Emitting a number would be dressing up the same guess in better clothes.

Worse, several of these genuinely cannot be separated by eye, and the sources say so about their own subject matter:

  • UMass, on iron: “In all but the most extreme cases, iron deficiency can be easily mistaken for nitrogen or magnesium deficiency … a soil or tissue test is necessary to confirm a suspected case of iron deficiency.”[10]
  • Colorado State, on phosphorus: “Phosphorus deficiency is difficult to diagnose because other growth factors will give similar symptoms.” And on potassium: “difficult to diagnose because other growth factors, including nitrogen deficiency, will cause similar symptoms.”[6]
  • Michigan State, on calcium versus boron: both are immobile, both hit new growth, “symptoms can be similar”, and leaf distortion “can be caused by other factors such as pesticide phytotoxicity, or damage by mites, aphids, and thrips” — so they recommend “a tissue analysis by a horticulture laboratory to help diagnose the issue.”[9]
  • Cornell, on root rots: “It is difficult to tell different root rots apart visually.”[17]

Given that, three bands are what the evidence carries. Likely means it fits both the position and the pattern you described. Possible means it fits, but something else fits at least as well, or the sources warn it cannot be told apart by eye. Ruled out means the position contradicts it — and the tool always shows you that list with its reason, because knowing what you have eliminated is half of a diagnosis.

One more thing worth knowing about patterns, from Cornell: a biotic problem tends to be “scattered and not uniformly occurring” across a crop while a nutritional one shows “a more uniform or consistent pattern”, and on a single leaf “a nutritional deficiency will generally appear more symmetrical.”[28] If one plant in six is affected and the rest are fine, look at pests and pathogens before nutrition.

What this tool deliberately leaves out

Six nutrients, not the full set. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium on the mobile side; iron (with manganese) and calcium on the immobile side. Everything else was cut, and for the same reason each time — a candidate that cannot be sourced properly does not become more accurate by being ranked, it just makes the ranking invented.

  • Zinc appears in our own chart and is not here. We could not find extension or peer-reviewed sourcing for the symptom description that would let the tool separate it from calcium and boron on new growth, and a seventh candidate we cannot tell apart from the ones already listed adds noise, not resolution. The chart still covers it; this tool will not guess at it.
  • Sulfur and boron were never in scope. Nothing on this site establishes a symptom mapping for either, so including them would have meant inventing one.
  • Manganese is not given its own card. It is grouped with iron because the two overlap where it counts — both are immobile, both give interveinal chlorosis on new growth, and both move with the same cause: NC State names iron and manganese together as the micronutrients whose availability shifts with pH,[23] and Michigan State recommends holding 5.5–5.8 in young plants to prevent iron and manganese deficiency in one move.[9] Published descriptions of the two do differ in detail, so this is not a claim that they are indistinguishable; it is that identifying either one by eye alone is unreliable, and that the extension literature answers the question the same way in both cases — check substrate pH and EC, then run a tissue test.[1][10] Splitting them would imply this tool can make a distinction that the sources settle with a laboratory rather than a look.
  • Pests and pathogens beyond root disease are out of scope, which is why the Cornell scatter-versus-uniform rule above matters: it is the cheapest way to tell that you are in the wrong tool entirely.

The Non-Nutrient Causes Are the Most Useful Part of the Output

Four things routinely produce symptoms that get treated as deficiencies, and a bottle of anything makes all four worse. They stay on the shortlist rather than being relegated to a closing paragraph.

Overwatering, which is a root-oxygen problem

It is not that the plant has too much water; it is that the roots have too little air. Maryland Extension: “Excess water reduces oxygen in the soil, which damages fine roots and renders the plant unable to take up water,” with “wilting or yellowing of lower and inner leaves” as the primary symptom.[12] The direct link to this page’s subject is stated by e-GRO: “When plants are over-watered for a period of time they may begin to show symptoms of nutrient deficiencies,” illustrated with plants displaying nitrogen, phosphorus and iron deficiency symptoms.[11] So overwatering does not merely resemble a deficiency. It manufactures one.

The trap is that Maryland’s own diagnostic table lists wilting under “overwatering or lack of water”[13] — the same symptom, opposite treatments. Judge by the weight of the pot, not by the leaves.

Root disease, which you cannot diagnose from above

Above ground it is a convincing impersonation. UConn lists “stunting, wilting, yellowing of leaves” as the general symptoms of crown and root rots;[15] UC IPM notes that leaves affected by Phytophthora “appear drought stressed” and “may turn dull green, yellow, or in some cases red or purplish”[16] — which covers both the nitrogen picture and the phosphorus one.

The diagnostic step is to take the plant out of the pot. UMass: “Healthy roots are generally white and firm; decayed roots may be water-soaked in appearance and/or darkened and easily macerated between the fingers.”[14] UConn adds the classic sign: “The outer root easily sloughs off when pulled with fingertips, leaving the inner strand or cortex of the root (rat-tail symptom).”[15] Two honest caveats: brown mushy roots do not prove disease, since “high soluble salts, ammonium toxicity, excessive moisture and lack of oxygen” damage roots too,[15] and as noted above, the different root rots are hard to separate by eye.[17]

Feeding too much, which looks like feeding too little

UMass puts it plainly: soluble salts above the normal range for a prolonged period “may cause root injury, leaf chlorosis, marginal burn and sometimes wilting.”[18] Margins go first for a mechanical reason — as Georgia puts it, “as the leaves transpire, more water is lost at the margins, hence more nutrients are brought to that area from the xylem.”[19]

And here is the trap, from the same Georgia publication: marginal burn is also listed there under nutrient deficiencies — calcium in poinsettia bracts, potassium in hydroponics.[19] A burnt leaf edge tells you the fertility is wrong. It does not tell you which direction. Only a number does that, which is what the EC to PPM converter is for.

Why your EC number needs its method attached

UMass gives the illustration that settles this: a reading of 2.6 in soilless media is extreme by the 1:2 method, normal by saturated media extract, and low by leachate PourThru — “always use the interpretative data that match the test you made, otherwise you could make an incorrect interpretation of the results.”[18] A USDA-ARS review quantifies the offset: pour-through EC runs roughly 1.4–1.6 times higher than saturated media extract.[29] A useful action threshold, tied to its method: NC State reports that “values greater than 6.0 mS/cm based on the PourThru sampling method can be problematic for many plants.”[20] Note that pH does not have this problem — the three extraction methods agree on pH and disagree only on EC.[1] Purdue’s HO-237-W walks through how each of the three readings is actually taken, if you want to know which one your meter is giving you.[2]

Light and heat, which get blamed on each other

Maryland’s table is direct: “Bleached or whitened leaves — Excessive light or sunburn.”[13] The distinguishing feature is that the damage is sharply bounded by exposure: “only certain parts of the leaf are scalded (these are the areas that had direct sunlight on them for an extended period) and the tissue next to the scalded area is still bright green.”[22] A nutrient problem does not respect the edge of a shadow.

The reason this one is worth having on the list at all is a case Michigan State published: geranium baskets with white, bleached and chlorotic foliage that the specialists first read as “a severe iron (Fe) or sulfur (S) deficiency.” The cause was a heater twelve feet away; bleaching decreased bow by bow and there were no symptoms at twenty-eight feet.[21] If your symptom map matches the geometry of a lamp or a heater rather than the biology of the plant, that is your answer.

Being straight about the limits here: we could not find a published source for several claims this section could easily have made — that light burn shows on the upper leaves nearest the lamp, that it curls margins upward, or that it bronzes tissue. The one sourced “margins curl” symptom we did find belongs to over-fertilisation,[19] so publishing it under light burn would have recreated the exact confusion this page exists to reduce. The tool therefore only claims what the sources say.

What to Do With the Shortlist

  1. Measure pH and EC before you buy anything. It is published Step 1,[1] and it is cheaper than the supplement you would otherwise guess at. Our guide covers what the two meters do.
  2. Get the EC onto a scale you can interpret, with the extraction method attached, using the EC to PPM converter. The three PPM scales disagree by 40%, so a number without a scale is not a number.
  3. Take the plant out of the pot and look at the roots. White and firm, or dark and sloughing.[14] This single step resolves two of the four impostors.
  4. If calcium came up, check transpiration rather than calcium. Calcium moves with the transpiration stream, and Michigan State lists the conditions that starve it: low light, high humidity — explicitly “low vapor pressure deficits (VPD)” — overmisting, cold substrate and “little to no air movement.”[9] The VPD calculator is the relevant instrument, not a cal-mag bottle.
  5. Fix one thing at a time, and judge it on new growth. Damaged leaves do not recover their colour; the next set of leaves is the readout.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are my plant’s leaves turning yellow?

Yellowing has more causes than it has appearances, which is why a photo rarely settles it. The three questions that narrow it fastest are where the yellowing started (oldest leaves or newest growth), whether the veins stayed green, and what the root-zone pH is. Uniform yellowing beginning on the oldest leaves is the classic nitrogen picture, but overwatering produces the same look by starving roots of oxygen, and a root-zone pH above about 6.5 produces iron chlorosis in plants that are being fed correctly. Measure pH before buying a supplement.

Should I check pH before adding a nutrient supplement?

Yes, and extension guidance says so explicitly. NC State’s e-GRO Alert 5.15 lists testing substrate pH and EC as Step 1 when a nutritional disorder is suspected, with substrate and tissue analysis as steps 2 and 3. Ohio State’s e-GRO Alert 15.09 puts it more bluntly: correcting chlorosis without verifying pH often leads to repeated iron applications with uneven results, because the underlying chemistry has not changed.

How accurate is diagnosing a nutrient deficiency from symptoms alone?

Not accurate enough to act on alone, and the published sources say so themselves. Colorado State’s GardenNotes 231 says phosphorus deficiency is difficult to diagnose because other growth factors give similar symptoms, and says the same of potassium. UMass Extension notes that in all but the most extreme cases iron deficiency can be easily mistaken for nitrogen or magnesium deficiency, and that a soil or tissue test is necessary to confirm it. Symptoms are a trigger for measurement, not a substitute for it.

What is the difference between a nutrient deficiency and nutrient lockout?

In a true deficiency the nutrient is not there. In lockout it is there but chemically unavailable at the current pH, so the plant starves in the middle of plenty. The leaf cannot tell you which one you have, because the plant’s internal shortage is identical either way. Colorado State’s GardenNotes 223 makes the point for iron: soils can be abundant in iron while the plant is chlorotic, because above pH 7 iron is fixed into insoluble forms and soil applications of iron alone are ineffective.

Why does this tool say likely and possible instead of giving a percentage?

Because five questions cannot support a percentage. A number like 87% implies a calibrated model behind it, and there is no such model here, only a published symptom-to-cause mapping and the mobile-versus-immobile position rule. Bands say what the evidence can actually carry: this one fits what you described, these are still open, and these are contradicted by where the symptom appeared.

Can a plant have more than one problem at the same time?

Routinely, and the combinations are the reason single answers mislead. Overwatering damages roots and then produces nitrogen, phosphorus and iron deficiency symptoms on top of the root damage. High soluble salts injure roots, which then look like root disease. A shortlist is the honest output because the underlying situation is frequently a shortlist too.
