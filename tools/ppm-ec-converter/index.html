Why Three Scales Exist

A TDS or PPM meter does not measure parts per million. It cannot — measuring dissolved solids properly means evaporating a known volume and weighing what is left. What the pen actually measures is electrical conductivity, in millisiemens per centimetre, and then multiplies by a factor to estimate PPM.

That factor depends on which salt you assume the solution is made of, because different salts conduct differently per unit mass. Three assumptions became conventions:

ScaleReferenceTypical meters
500 (sometimes written ×0.5, or "TDS")Sodium chlorideHanna, most inexpensive US pens, many aquarium meters
640A mixed-salt referenceEutech and some European instruments
700 (sometimes written ×0.7)Potassium chlorideBluelab, the Bluelab Truncheon, much Australian and NZ equipment

So a solution at 2.0 mS/cm is 1000 PPM, 1280 PPM or 1400 PPM, and all three readings are correct. The 40% spread between the ends is why a feed chart quoted only in PPM is close to useless, and why two growers can have a long argument about nutrient strength while running identical reservoirs.

The practical fix: use EC. It is the number the meter actually measured, it needs no conversion factor, and it is what every serious nutrient manufacturer publishes alongside their PPM figures. If a chart gives you PPM without naming a scale, assume 500 if it is American and 700 if it is Australian, then confirm by converting to EC and checking the result is sane for the growth stage.

Three Things EC Will Not Tell You

  • Which nutrients are in there. EC is a measure of total dissolved salt, blind to composition. A reservoir that has been stripped of nitrogen while accumulating calcium reads the same as a balanced one at the same strength. This is the single most important limitation and the one that catches people out: EC tells you how strong, never how correct.
  • What your source water was already carrying. Hard tap water can start at 0.3–0.6 mS/cm of calcium and magnesium carbonate before you add anything. Measure it, and treat feed-chart numbers as what you add on top. This is the main argument for RO water: not purity for its own sake, but knowing what is in the tank.
  • Anything, if the solution is cold. Conductivity rises about 2% per °C, so a meter without automatic temperature compensation will read a chilly reservoir low and tempt you into overfeeding. Meters with ATC normalise to 25 °C.

The related trap is rising EC in a running reservoir. Plants drink water faster than they eat salt, so the EC of a topped-up reservoir climbs over a week even though the plants are being underfed. Rising EC usually means top up with plain water, not with more nutrient — the opposite of what the number seems to be asking for.

How This Converter Works

PPM = EC (mS/cm) × factor    factor ∈ { 500, 640, 700 }
EC (mS/cm) = PPM ÷ factor
1 mS/cm = 1 dS/m = 1000 µS/cm

Everything you enter is converted to EC in mS/cm first, and every other figure is derived from that single value — which is also the honest way to convert between two PPM scales: 1200 PPM on the 500 scale is 1200 ÷ 500 = 2.4 mS/cm, which is 2.4 × 700 = 1680 PPM on the 700 scale. There is no direct PPM-to-PPM shortcut that does not pass through EC.

References. The three conversion factors are the manufacturers’ own: the 500 (NaCl) scale as published by Hanna Instruments for its TDS meters, the 700 (KCl) scale as published by Bluelab for the Truncheon and its conductivity pens, and the 640 factor as used by Eutech and several European instruments. The 2%-per-°C temperature coefficient and the 25 °C reference are the standard conductivity conventions those same manufacturers calibrate to. Stage EC bands follow published hydroponic nutrient-solution guidance for greenhouse and controlled-environment crops.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between the 500, 640 and 700 PPM scales?

They are three different conversion factors between electrical conductivity and parts per million, each calibrated against a different reference salt. PPM = EC in mS/cm multiplied by the factor. The 500 scale (also called the NaCl or TDS scale) is used by Hanna and most US meters, the 700 scale (the KCl scale) by Bluelab and the Truncheon, and 640 by some European and Eutech meters. The same nutrient solution reads 1000, 1280 or 1400 PPM depending only on which scale the meter uses.

How do I know which PPM scale my meter uses?

Check the manual, or test it: measure a solution, then read its EC. If PPM divided by EC is about 500 you are on the 500 scale, about 700 means the KCl scale, about 640 the Eutech scale. If your meter only shows PPM and cannot show EC, the brand is the strongest clue — Hanna and most inexpensive US pens are 500, Bluelab is 700.

Should I use EC or PPM?

EC, whenever you have the choice. EC in mS/cm is the actual measurement the meter makes and it is unambiguous. PPM is EC multiplied by a conversion factor that nobody agrees on, which is why a feed chart quoted in PPM is useless unless it also names the scale.

Does an EC meter tell me which nutrients are in my reservoir?

No. EC measures how well the solution conducts electricity, which depends on the total quantity of dissolved salts and not on what they are. A reservoir that has run out of nitrogen but accumulated calcium can read exactly the same EC as a perfectly balanced one. EC tells you how strong the solution is, never how correct it is.

Do I need to subtract my tap water EC?

Yes, if you are following a feed chart. Hard tap water can start at 0.3–0.6 mS/cm before any nutrient is added, and that background is mostly calcium and magnesium carbonates rather than the fertiliser you are dosing. Measure your source water first and treat the feed chart figure as the amount you add on top.

Does temperature affect an EC reading?

Strongly — conductivity rises roughly 2% per degree Celsius. Any meter worth using has automatic temperature compensation and reports EC normalised to 25 °C. If yours does not, a cold reservoir will read low and you will over-feed trying to correct it.
