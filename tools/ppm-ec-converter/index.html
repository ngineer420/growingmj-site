Why Three Scales Exist

A TDS or PPM meter does not measure parts per million. It cannot — measuring dissolved solids properly means evaporating a known volume and weighing what is left. What the pen actually measures is electrical conductivity, in millisiemens per centimetre, and then multiplies by a factor to estimate PPM.

That factor depends on what you assume the solution is made of, because different salts conduct differently per unit mass. Three assumptions became conventions — and two of the three are routinely misdescribed, including by the shops that sell the meters:

ScaleReferenceTypical meters
500 (sometimes written ×0.5, or "TDS")Sodium chloride — genuinelyHanna, most inexpensive US pens, many aquarium meters
640Irrigation agronomy. Published by the FAO for agricultural water quality, not by a meter makerAgricultural and irrigation work; a few meters offer it
700 (sometimes written ×0.7)A proprietary mixed-salt standard — not potassium chlorideBluelab, the Bluelab Truncheon, most hydroponic equipment

Two corrections worth making, because the wrong version is repeated almost everywhere: the 700 scale is not the KCl scale. A genuine potassium chloride factor is about 0.50–0.53, which is nearly the 500 scale. The 0.7 traces instead to a proprietary mixed-salt reference standard — Myron L’s "442 Natural Water", which reads 1000 ppm at 1417 µS/cm, giving 0.706. And the 640 scale is not a meter brand’s house factor. It comes from irrigation agronomy, where the FAO publishes it for agricultural water quality; the meters usually blamed for it ship defaulted to 0.5.

None of the three is a standard in the sense that ASTM or ISO issues standards. The standards bodies specify how to measure conductivity and stop there — the conversion to PPM is left alone precisely because no single number is right. The USGS puts the real-world ratio for natural waters at 0.55 to 0.75, which brackets all three of these and is a fair summary of how much anyone actually knows.

So a solution at 2.0 mS/cm is 1000 PPM, 1280 PPM or 1400 PPM, and all three readings are equally defensible. The 40% spread between the ends is why a feed chart quoted only in PPM is close to useless, and why two growers can have a long argument about nutrient strength while running identical reservoirs.

The practical fix: use EC. It is the number the meter actually measured, it needs no conversion factor, and it is what every serious nutrient manufacturer publishes alongside their PPM figures. If a chart gives you PPM without naming a scale, assume 500 if it is American and 700 if it is Australian, then confirm by converting to EC and checking the result is sane for the growth stage.

Three Things EC Will Not Tell You

  • Which nutrients are in there. EC is a measure of total dissolved salt, blind to composition. A reservoir that has been stripped of nitrogen while accumulating calcium reads the same as a balanced one at the same strength. This is the single most important limitation and the one that catches people out: EC tells you how strong, never how correct.
  • What your source water was already carrying. Hard tap water can start at 0.3–0.6 mS/cm of calcium and magnesium carbonate before you add anything. Measure it, and treat feed-chart numbers as what you add on top. This is the main argument for RO water: not purity for its own sake, but knowing what is in the tank.
  • Anything, if the solution is cold. Conductivity rises about 2% per °C, so a meter without automatic temperature compensation will read a chilly reservoir low and tempt you into overfeeding. Meters with ATC normalise to 25 °C.

The related trap is rising EC in a running reservoir. Plants drink water faster than they eat salt, so the EC of a topped-up reservoir climbs over a week even though the plants are being underfed. Rising EC usually means top up with plain water, not with more nutrient — the opposite of what the number seems to be asking for.

How This Converter Works

PPM = EC (mS/cm) × factor    factor ∈ { 500, 640, 700 }
EC (mS/cm) = PPM ÷ factor
1 mS/cm = 1 dS/m = 1000 µS/cm

Everything you enter is converted to EC in mS/cm first, and every other figure is derived from that single value — which is also the honest way to convert between two PPM scales: 1200 PPM on the 500 scale is 1200 ÷ 500 = 2.4 mS/cm, which is 2.4 × 700 = 1680 PPM on the 700 scale. There is no direct PPM-to-PPM shortcut that does not pass through EC.

References. 500 scale: Hanna Instruments HI5321 manual — "TDS = Factor × EC₂₅… The default value is 0.50", genuinely an NaCl reference. 700 scale: Bluelab’s own conductivity page ("PPM = EC × 700"); the 0.7 originates with Myron L’s proprietary "442 Natural Water" standard (1000 ppm at 1417 µS/cm), and Myron L themselves state that no single multiplier can be used. It is not a potassium chloride factor, and this page previously said it was. 640 scale: FAO Plant Production and Protection Paper 217 (2013), p. 176 — "EC in mS/cm is multiplied by 640"; the unit identity 1 dS/m = 1 mS/cm = 1000 µS/cm is from p. 175 of the same paper. Ratio range 0.55–0.75: USGS Water-Supply Paper 2254 (Hem), p. 164. ASTM D1125 and D5391 standardise the conductivity measurement only and endorse no conversion factor, which is why the PPM figure on any meter is a derived estimate rather than a measurement — Hanna’s own position as well. Stage EC bands follow FAO Paper 217 ch. 12 and the Cornell CEA lettuce handbook (1150–1250 µS/cm above source water, pH 5.6–6.0).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between the 500, 640 and 700 PPM scales?

They are three different conversion factors between electrical conductivity and parts per million, each based on a different assumption about what is dissolved. PPM = EC in mS/cm multiplied by the factor. The 500 scale (the NaCl or TDS scale) is used by Hanna and most US meters and really is calibrated to sodium chloride. The 700 scale is used by Bluelab and the Truncheon, and despite being universally called the KCl scale it is not one — real potassium chloride is about 0.5. The 640 factor comes from irrigation agronomy, where the FAO publishes it, rather than from any meter brand. The same nutrient solution reads 1000, 1280 or 1400 PPM depending only on which scale the meter uses.

How do I know which PPM scale my meter uses?

Check the manual, or test it: measure a solution, then read its EC. If PPM divided by EC is about 500 you are on the 500 scale, about 700 the hydroponic one, about 640 the irrigation one. If your meter only shows PPM and cannot show EC, the brand is the strongest clue — Hanna and most inexpensive US pens are 500, Bluelab is 700.

Should I use EC or PPM?

EC, whenever you have the choice. EC in mS/cm is the actual measurement the meter makes and it is unambiguous. PPM is EC multiplied by a conversion factor that nobody agrees on, which is why a feed chart quoted in PPM is useless unless it also names the scale.

Does an EC meter tell me which nutrients are in my reservoir?

No. EC measures how well the solution conducts electricity, which depends on the total quantity of dissolved salts and not on what they are. A reservoir that has run out of nitrogen but accumulated calcium can read exactly the same EC as a perfectly balanced one. EC tells you how strong the solution is, never how correct it is.

Do I need to subtract my tap water EC?

Yes, if you are following a feed chart. Hard tap water can start at 0.3–0.6 mS/cm before any nutrient is added, and that background is mostly calcium and magnesium carbonates rather than the fertiliser you are dosing. Measure your source water first and treat the feed chart figure as the amount you add on top.

Does temperature affect an EC reading?

Strongly — conductivity rises roughly 2% per degree Celsius. Any meter worth using has automatic temperature compensation and reports EC normalised to 25 °C. If yours does not, a cold reservoir will read low and you will over-feed trying to correct it.
