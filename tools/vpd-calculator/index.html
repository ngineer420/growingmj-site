What VPD Actually Is

Vapour pressure deficit is the gap between how much water vapour the air is holding and how much it could hold at the temperature of the leaf. It is the pull on the plant — the force that drags water up through the roots, through the stem and out of the stomata, carrying calcium and every other immobile nutrient along for the ride.

That is why VPD does a job that temperature and humidity cannot do separately. 75 °F at 80% humidity and 85 °F at 80% humidity read as the same humidity problem on a hygrometer and are different environments for a plant: the first is a sluggish 0.59 kPa, the second a working 0.82 kPa. Two numbers you can control, one number that decides what happens.

When VPD is too low, transpiration slows to a crawl. The plant stops pulling, calcium stops arriving at new growth, leaves stay wet longer, and you get the classic humid-tent trio of soft growth, powdery mildew and bud rot. When VPD is too high, the plant defends itself by closing its stomata — and a closed stoma is not taking in CO₂ either, so photosynthesis stops along with the water loss. That is the part growers miss: a too-dry tent does not just wilt plants, it switches off the factory.

Leaf Temperature Is Not Air Temperature

This is the single thing most VPD calculators get wrong, and it is worth being precise about because the correction is large enough to move you a whole band.

VPD is a difference between two vapour pressures. The one inside the leaf is saturated at leaf temperature. The one in the air is whatever the air is holding, which is saturation at air temperature multiplied by relative humidity. If you use air temperature for both halves you get "air VPD" or "atmospheric VPD" — a real quantity, but not the one the plant experiences.

So far, so standard. Here is the part almost every grow site gets wrong, this one included until we went and checked.

The number repeated everywhere is that a transpiring leaf under LEDs sits about 2 °C below air temperature, and that published VPD charts assume it. Neither half of that is true. We could not find a single published source prescribing a −2 °C offset, and the best measurements available point the other way: Nelson and Bugbee (2015) measured leaf-to-air differences under a range of light sources and found them under 2 °C in every scenario short of the extremes, ordered HPS > greenhouse sun > LED > clear sky, with indoor leaves frequently running warmer than the air rather than cooler, because a leaf indoors has no cold sky to radiate to. Their conclusion is blunt: the effect of LED technology on leaf temperature is smaller than is often assumed.

And the charts do not assume an offset either. We reproduced the whole of the widely-circulated Argus VPD table to within 0.08 mb using air temperature and no leaf offset at all — so a chart printed off a forum and a calculator applying −2 °C are not describing the same quantity, and the bands printed on that chart belong to the first one.

This matters because the correction is not small. A −2 °C offset moves VPD by 0.34–0.42 kPa at ordinary tent conditions — a third to a half of an entire target band, in the direction of telling you a dry tent is fine. So the offset here defaults to 0, which is what the published bands were built on, and the field is there for the case that actually justifies it: you pointed a thermometer at a leaf and know your real number.

Measuring it yourself. A measured offset is worth having precisely because the assumed one is not. Point an infrared thermometer at a big healthy fan leaf from about six inches away, lights on, and subtract the reading from your air temperature. Average three leaves. Expect something small, and do not be surprised by a negative result — a leaf running warmer than the air is a normal indoor outcome, and entering a negative offset here is a perfectly valid thing to do. Worth re-checking once per grow, because a canopy that has filled out moves air differently than one that has not.

Target VPD by Growth Stage

These are air-VPD bands, matching the published sources they come from — which is also why the offset above defaults to 0. They are broad on purpose: VPD is a range to stay inside, not a set point to chase, and chasing 0.05 kPa with a humidifier that cycles every ten minutes causes more problems than it solves.

Note that the top of this table stops at 1.4 kPa. You will see 1.5–1.6 kPa quoted for late flower all over grow forums, and it exceeds every published greenhouse source we could find — the widely-cited Argus application note puts the optimal band at 0.5–1.2 kPa and calls 1.37 the edge of marginal.

Fixing a VPD That Is Out of Range

There are only two levers, and the difference between them is not really about strength.

  • Per step they are comparable. Around a typical 78 °F / 60% tent, moving relative humidity by 5 percentage points moves VPD by about 0.16 kPa, and moving air temperature by 5 °F moves it by about 0.18 kPa. Anyone telling you humidity is ten times the lever has not run the numbers.
  • The steps are not comparable. Five points of humidity is a nudge on the exhaust fan. Five degrees is a large change to a tent you are already holding inside a narrow band for photosynthesis, for the lights and for the plants’ own heat tolerance. Using temperature to chase VPD usually means trading a humidity problem for a heat problem.

So the practical order is: fix humidity first with extraction or a dehumidifier, only then touch temperature, and only then reach for a humidifier. In most tents the exhaust fan is already the humidity control — running it harder is free and instant, which is one more reason to size the fan with some headroom instead of buying exactly enough.

One caveat worth stating plainly: VPD is a target for a healthy plant with a working root system. A plant that is wilting, root-bound, sitting in cold wet medium or already sick will not transpire properly at any VPD, and pushing the environment harder to make it drink is a way to kill it faster. Fix the roots, then aim the air.

How This Calculator Works

Saturation vapour pressure comes from the Buck (1996) equation for vapour over liquid water, with temperature in °C and the result in kPa:

SVP(T) = 0.61121 × exp[ (18.678 − T/234.5) × (T / (257.14 + T)) ]

VPD is then the leaf-to-air deficit:

VPD = SVP(Tleaf) − SVP(Tair) × RH/100
where Tleaf = Tair − offset

Buck rather than the older Tetens equation simply because it is the more recent formulation. It is not a meaningful accuracy improvement here: across 10–30 °C the two agree to better than 0.05%, which is roughly a hundred times finer than the ±3% accuracy of a typical tent hygrometer. Your sensor, not the equation, is the limit on this number — and the Buck manual itself quotes no accuracy figure for water, so claims you may have read about its precision are not from the primary document.

With the offset at its default of 0 this reduces to air VPD, which is the quantity the published target bands were derived from. Enter a nonzero offset and you get leaf-to-air VPD, which is the physically correct quantity but has to be compared against bands derived the same way.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good VPD for growing?

Roughly 0.4–0.8 kPa for clones and seedlings, 0.8–1.2 kPa through vegetative growth, and 1.2–1.4 kPa in flower. Those are air-VPD figures, which is how the published sources they come from define them. Below about 0.4 kPa transpiration nearly stops and disease risk climbs; above about 1.4 kPa plants start closing stomata and growth slows. The 1.5–1.6 kPa flowering figure common on grow forums exceeds every published greenhouse source.

Why does a VPD calculator ask for a leaf temperature offset?

Because VPD is the difference between the vapour pressure inside the leaf and the vapour pressure of the air, and the inside of the leaf is at leaf temperature, not air temperature. What is worth knowing is that the 2 °C offset repeated on almost every grow site is not sourced: measurements by Nelson and Bugbee (2015) put leaf-to-air differences under 2 °C in every ordinary scenario, with indoor leaves often running warmer than the air rather than cooler. The offset here therefore defaults to 0, matching the published charts and target bands, and exists as a field so you can enter a leaf temperature you have actually measured.

How do I measure leaf temperature?

With an infrared thermometer pointed at a large, healthy fan leaf in the canopy from about 15 cm away, taken with the lights on and at the same time of day. Take three leaves and average them. Subtract that reading from your air temperature and use the difference as your offset.

Is VPD measured in kPa or millibars?

Horticulture almost always uses kilopascals (kPa). Some older equipment reports millibars or hectopascals: 1 kPa = 10 mbar = 10 hPa, so a 1.2 kPa target is 12 mbar.

My VPD is too low. Should I raise the temperature or drop the humidity?

Drop the humidity first. Humidity moves VPD far more per unit than temperature does, and raising tent temperature to fix humidity usually creates a heat problem instead. In a sealed tent that means more extraction or a dehumidifier; in a dry climate it can mean simply running the exhaust harder.

Which saturation vapour pressure equation does this calculator use?

The Buck (1996) equation for saturation vapour pressure over liquid water. Over the 10–30 °C range that matters indoors it agrees with the FAO-56 Tetens equation to better than 0.05%, which is far smaller than the error in your hygrometer, so the choice is effectively arbitrary.
