# Cockson et al. (2019) — nutrient disorders of Cannabis sativa

Working digest of the single most directly applicable source for this site's nutrient content.

**Cockson, P.; Landis, H.; Smith, T.; Hicks, K.; Whipker, B.E. (2019). "Characterization of Nutrient Disorders of Cannabis sativa." _Applied Sciences_ 9(20):4432.** doi:10.3390/app9204432. Open access, CC-BY. Senior author Brian Whipker (NC State Horticultural Science) is the same group behind the NC State floriculture nutrient-disorder literature this site already cites.

Why it matters: every other source this site leans on — NC State's handbook, UF/IFAS, e-GRO, UMass — describes deficiency in other crops and is extrapolated here. This one induces and photographs each disorder in *Cannabis sativa* under controlled conditions, so symptom descriptions are direct rather than transferred.

Access note: MDPI returns 403 to every automated route including the DOI resolver, so this cannot be re-fetched by an agent. The PDF was pulled by hand (`applsci-09-04432-v2.pdf`). If a claim below needs checking against the figures, the PDF has to be opened manually.

### Boron Toxicity
Symptoms of B toxicity appeared 7 weeks after treatment. Initially, the plants developed a marginal yellowing of the lower leaves. This yellowing intensified along the leaf margin and moved inward toward the midrib of the leaflets. In advanced symptoms, the leaf margin turned brown and eventually became necrotic. Foliar B concentrations were >10 fold higher in the B toxicity plants than in the controls. Toxicity treated plants contained 671.75 mg·kg−1 B, while the control plants contained 64.60 mg·kg−1 (Table 4). Foliar B concentrations in control plants were within the published survey range of 56–105 mg·kg−1 B (Bryson et al., [9]). There were no statistically significant differences in the dry weights of the control plant and the B toxicity plants (Table 3).

### Iron
Symptoms of upper leaf interveinal chlorosis developed on Fe-deficient plants after 9 weeks of iron deficiency treatments. The leaves were lighter in appearance compared to control plants, and symptoms spread throughout the upper half of the foliage, especially around the growing tip and newly expanding leaves. Symptoms of Fe stress began as a slight marginal yellowing of the leaflets, especially around the base of the leaf. ### Molybdenum
Visual symptoms did not develop on Mo-deficient plants after 9 weeks of deficiency treatments. Despite a lack of visual symptoms, foliar Mo concentrations were 96% lower in plants subjected to Mo-deficient conditions when compared to the controls. Deficiency treated plants contained 0.06 mg·kg−1 Mo, while the control plants contained 1.46 mg·kg−1 (Table 4). Foliar Mo concentrations in control plants were within the published survey range of 0.5–1.5 mg·kg−1 Mo (Bryson et al., [9]). Despite the statistical significance of the tissue concentrations, there was no statistical difference in dry weights of the Mo deficient plants when compared to the controls (Table 3).

## What this changes for the diagnoser

Two open questions in `/tools/deficiency-diagnosis/` are answered directly by this source, and both answers differ from what currently ships.

**1. Zinc does not collide with iron.** Zinc was cut from the tool (issue #23) because the draft described it as "pale" new growth, which would have duplicated iron's interveinal signature. That description was wrong for this crop. Cockson describes zinc as *marginal* yellowing on new and expanding foliage, concentrated at the leaf margin and along the toothed edges of the leaflets, progressing to tan irregular necrosis along the margin. Marginal-on-new-growth is a distinct pattern from interveinal-on-new-growth, so zinc is separable from iron after all and can be recovered as a seventh candidate.

**2. Iron and manganese are more separable in this crop than the tool currently claims.** The tool groups them and says visual identification alone is unreliable. In cannabis specifically, this source distinguishes them: manganese presents as a bright yellow *netted* interveinal chlorosis initiating at the midrib and spreading outward, on upper *and central* foliage, later developing small tan necrotic spots in the interveinal regions; iron presents as a generally lighter leaf with interveinal chlorosis concentrated around the growing tip and newly expanding leaves, beginning as marginal yellowing near the leaf base. Onset also differed — manganese at 6 weeks, iron at 9.

That does not automatically mean the grouping should be dissolved. Under controlled single-nutrient starvation the pattern is clean; a real grower sees compounding deficiencies, pH lockout, and no control plant beside it for comparison. But the current wording claims more uncertainty than this source supports, and "netted, from the midrib outward, upper and central" versus "around the growing tip, lighter overall" is a question a five-question tool could plausibly ask.

**Also worth noting:** manganese *toxicity* is described here as marginal yellowing of the *lower* leaves moving inward — a different picture from manganese deficiency, and a reminder that the NC State table's "shows up as Fe deficiency" line sits under toxicity, not deficiency. That misreading has already been corrected once on this site (PR #22).

## Caveats

- One cultivar ('T1'), controlled conditions, single-nutrient starvation. Real-world presentation is messier.
- Onset weeks are experimental, driven by when treatments began. Do not present them to readers as "expect this at week 6".
- Tissue concentration ranges in the paper's tables are the most citable numbers here and have not been transcribed into this digest — go to the PDF for them rather than paraphrasing.
