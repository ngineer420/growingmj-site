LED vs. HPS Grow Lights, Compared

An honest comparison of LED and HPS grow lights for indoor gardening: heat, electricity cost, spectrum, and lifespan.

Lighting Updated August 20, 2026 10 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

Is LED or HPS cheaper overall?

HPS is cheaper upfront, but LED is almost always cheaper over the long run — lower electricity bills typically offset LED's higher purchase price within one to two growing seasons of daily use.

For decades, high pressure sodium (HPS) lights were the default choice for serious indoor growing, and for good reason — they're cheap to buy and produce a lot of usable light. LED grow lights have since caught up and, for most home growers, overtaken HPS as the better all-around option. But "better" depends on what you're optimizing for, so here's a plain comparison across the factors that actually matter day to day.

Upfront Cost

HPS still wins here. A basic HPS fixture with a ballast and bulb is often cheaper than a comparable LED, especially at the low end of the market. If your budget is the primary constraint and you don't mind the tradeoffs below, an HPS kit can get you growing for less money on day one.

Electricity Cost Over Time

This is where LED earns its price. A modern LED fixture turns more of the electricity it draws into photosynthetically usable photons than any HPS lamp does, so it takes fewer watts to put the same light on a canopy. Over a year of daily use that gap is often enough to swallow the difference in purchase price.

Here's the math on a concrete pairing, because "lower running cost" is easy to nod along to and easy to underestimate. Say you're lighting a 4x4 tent with a 450W LED, against the 600W HPS a lot of people are replacing. At 16 hours a day and 17.8¢/kWh — the US residential average for the Rolling 12 months ending May 2026:1

  • 450W LED: 216 kWh/month, about $38.49/month
  • 600W HPS: 288 kWh/month, about $51.32/month — plus, in warm months, whatever your exhaust fan and AC spend removing its heat

That's about $154 a year of direct savings before counting cooling or replacement bulbs, so a $150 gap in sticker price closes inside a season. Check your own rate before trusting the total, though, and note that the national figure has risen every year since 2020. State averages are nothing like uniform: in May 2026 the residential average ran from 12.35¢ in Idaho to 52.00¢ in Hawaii.2

That comparison also flatters the HPS, and it's worth saying so rather than banking the saving twice. The two fixtures are not equal-output. Independently tested LED fixtures reach 2.5 to 2.8 µmol/J, so 450 W buys 1,125 to 1,260 µmol/s of photons.3 The mogul-base single-ended HPS fixtures most home growers own were measured at 1.02 µmol/J, against 1.66 to 1.70 for the 1000 W double-ended fixtures greenhouses use.4 Nobody has published a measured figure for a 600 W single-ended lamp specifically, and we are not going to invent one — but it belongs to the first group, not the second, which puts it closer to 600 µmol/s than to 1,000. The LED is cheaper to run and brighter; the electricity is the smaller half of the argument.

How to Compare Light Output Honestly

Wattage is the number everyone shops by, and it's the least useful one on the box. Watts measure what the fixture pulls from the wall, not what reaches your plants. Two specs actually compare fixtures:

  • PPFD (photosynthetic photon flux density) — how much usable light lands on the canopy, in µmol/m²/s. Decent manufacturers publish a PPFD map showing readings across the coverage area; a map that only shows the hot centre reading is itself a warning sign.
  • Efficacy — µmol of light per joule of electricity, and the one number that lets you compare two fixtures at all. Independently tested LED fixtures reach 2.5 to 2.8 µmol/J, and the DesignLights Consortium now refuses to list a horticultural LED below 2.5.35 Quote HPS with the fixture class attached or the figure means nothing: 1.66 to 1.70 µmol/J is a 1000 W double-ended greenhouse fixture, while the mogul-base single-ended lamps sold into home tents measured 1.02.4 If a fixture doesn't publish efficacy at all, assume there's a reason.

PPFD on its own still isn't the target, because it says nothing about how long the light is on. What a plant responds to is the day's total — the daily light integral, in moles of light per square metre per day — and you get it from PPFD with one multiplication:

DLI = PPFD × hours × 0.0036

So 200 µmol/m²/s for 16 hours is 11.5 mol/m²/day. Against Virginia Tech's crop table that grows seedlings and lettuce comfortably and will not ripen a tomato:

Seedlings and cuttings5-10
Microgreens9-12

One more trap: "1000W equivalent" in a product title means nothing at all. Find the actual draw wattage in the spec sheet and do the photon arithmetic instead. A 150 W fixture at 2.5 to 2.8 µmol/J delivers roughly 375 to 420 µmol/s; the 1000 W double-ended HPS it claims to equal runs about 1,700 µmol/s at its measured efficacy. That is not an equivalent, it's about a quarter — fine over a 2x2 and hopeless over a 4x4.

Heat Output: Relocated, Not Removed

This is the practical difference beginners notice first, and it is also the one most often described wrongly. Nearly all the electrical power a fixture draws ends up as heat in the room — the fraction that leaves as light is absorbed by leaves, walls and floor and becomes heat there too. A 300 W LED and a 300 W HPS put roughly 300 W of heat into a closed tent. What differs is where that heat goes, not how much of it there is.

HPS radiates a large share of it downward onto the canopy; an LED sheds more of its heat by convection off heat sinks and driver, away from the leaf. Nelson and Bugbee measured the consequence: LEDs reduce leaf temperature by about 1.3 °C compared with HPS under typical indoor conditions. In the same paper's near-worst-case model of water stress and low wind, leaves ran about 8 °C above air temperature under LED against 12 °C under HPS.7 A real advantage, then, and a much smaller one than the folklore claims. So:

  • You can usually mount an LED closer to the canopy, because less of its heat arrives at the leaf as radiation.
  • You still have to get the heat out of the space. Fixture wattage sets the exhaust requirement; the technology doesn't change it.
  • A clip fan is not a substitute for exhaust. Stirring air inside a tent is not the same as exchanging it. Ambient outdoor CO2 is around 430 ppm, and Virginia Tech reports levels in enclosed growing facilities reported as low as 200 ppm, because plants draw it down faster than a sealed space replaces it — the more tightly sealed the space and the less it vents, the further it falls.8 A circulating fan fixes neither heat removal nor CO2 replenishment. Size an exhaust path to the space, and run the clip fan for leaf-level air movement on top of it.

Light Spectrum

HPS light is heavily weighted toward the yellow-orange part of the spectrum, which works well but isn't a full match for what plants use across their whole life cycle. Quality LED fixtures, especially full-spectrum "quantum board" style panels, are built to output a broader spread of wavelengths closer to natural daylight, which tends to support healthier, more balanced growth from seedling through mature plant. Shopping for a full-spectrum LED panel, though, the layout matters as much as the spectrum: a bar-style fixture spreads light into the corners of a tent far better than a single dense board of the same wattage hung in the middle, and that is the difference between a canopy that grows evenly and one where only the plants directly underneath do. Ask for a published PPFD map at your tent size, and treat a fixture without one as an unknown quantity.

Lifespan and Maintenance

Both technologies fade rather than fail, and both are routinely described in a way that inverts what the rating means.

HPS lamp life is the number this page has been most wrong about, and the reason is instructive: it depends entirely on which lamp you mean, and almost nobody quoting it says. Ushio's catalogue rates its single-ended sodium lamps at 19,000 to 24,000 hours and its double-ended lamps at 10,000 hours; the single-ended entries are on pages the catalogue marks discontinued.9 Osram is blunter about what the hours are for, recommending replacement of its 1000 W lamp "after 10 000 hours… in order to ensure optimal and continuous growth of the plants," a figure it footnotes as a 95% survival rate rather than a burnout point.10 Both numbers describe output, not failure: an HPS lamp dims steadily long before it stops striking, which is why growers replace on measured output.

So the "12 to 18 months" this page used to attribute to manufacturers turns out to be roughly right by accident, for the wrong reason. Nobody publishes months, because months are not a unit of lamp use. Convert instead: 10,000 hours is about 21 months at 16 hours a day, or 18 months at 18. Run the same lamp 12 hours a day and it is closer to two and a quarter years. The interval is a consequence of your photoperiod, not a property of the bulb, and that is the whole reason a fixed number in months was never going to hold.

LED fixtures are rated by L70 instead, and it is worth knowing what that means, because "tens of thousands of hours before any noticeable drop" — which this page used to say — states it backwards. The Department of Energy defines L70 as "the point when output has dropped to 70% of the original value," usually published as L70-B50: the time at which half a sample is expected to have got there.11 A typical LED fixture L70 is 50,000 h.3 That is the hour output has already fallen by 30%, not the hour decline begins. Output drops from the first hour, faster when the diodes run hot. The practical upshot still favours LED decisively — 50,000 h is about eight and a half years at 16 hours a day against under two for a double-ended HPS lamp, and there is no bulb to swap, no ballast to fail and no glass to break — but a five-year-old panel is dimmer than it was, and hanging it at the same height as on day one is a real mistake.

Noise and Bulk

HPS setups need a separate ballast (the box that regulates power to the bulb), which adds bulk, weight, and sometimes an audible hum. LED fixtures are typically a single flat panel with a built-in driver, mount flush to the tent's top bars, and run silently.

So Which Should You Buy?

For almost every home grower working in a tent-sized space, LED is the better long-term choice: lower running cost, less heat to manage, a longer usable lifespan, and a broader spectrum. The main scenario where HPS still makes sense is a very tight upfront budget where you need light output immediately and can tolerate the added heat, higher power bills, and bulb replacements down the line.

If you're buying your first light: pair a full-spectrum LED fixture with a digital outlet timer so it runs on a consistent daily schedule, and add a small clip fan for gentle air circulation. Budget for an inline exhaust fan and ducting as well, whatever light you buy — the clip fan moves air around inside the tent, and the exhaust fan is what actually removes heat and brings fresh air in. Our ventilation guide covers sizing it.

A Quick Sizing Note

Whichever light you choose, check its recommended coverage area against your tent size (most manufacturers list this directly). A light rated for a 2x2 ft footprint will underperform in a 4x4 tent — you'll get healthy plants directly under the light and weak, stretchy growth toward the edges. When in doubt, size the light to match your tent, not the other way around.

The Middle Options Nobody Markets

The LED-vs-HPS framing skips two lights that are quietly right for certain jobs. T5 fluorescent fixtures are cheap and gentle and still fine for seedlings, cuttings and low-light herbs on a shelf, where a DLI of 5 to 10 is the whole requirement and nothing has to be cooked from a distance. The efficiency argument against them is real, though: the best fluorescent fixture in Nelson and Bugbee's test managed 0.95 µmol/J, which is the worst number in the whole comparison.4

Ceramic metal halide is the more interesting one, and the usual shorthand for it — "more efficient than HPS" — is only half true. The best CMH fixture measured 1.46 µmol/J: clearly ahead of the 1.02 of a mogul-base single-ended HPS, and clearly behind the 1.66 to 1.70 of a double-ended one.4 Which HPS you are comparing it to decides whether the sentence is right. CMH shares HPS's heat and ballast bulk either way, and it had its moment before quantum boards got cheap.

The used market is also full of HPS kits from growers who switched to LED, and if your budget is genuinely tight one of those beats a new bargain-bin LED of unknown provenance. Assume the lamp needs replacing — you have no idea how many of its 10,000 hours are gone — and check that the ballast doesn't buzz loudly enough to hear across a room.

What Dimming Is Actually For

Most quality LEDs now ship with a dimmer knob, and the DLI table above is what it is for. A seedling wants 5 to 10 mol/m²/day and a fruiting tomato wants 20 to 306 — a threefold swing over the life of one crop, in one tent, under one fixture. Dim to the tray you actually have, then work the knob up rather than buying a second light. It also spares you the classic first-week mistake of blasting seedlings with a full-power fixture hung high, which wastes light, or hung low, which bleaches them. Frequently Asked Questions

Is LED or HPS cheaper overall?

HPS is cheaper upfront, but LED is almost always cheaper over the long run — lower electricity bills typically offset LED's higher purchase price within one to two growing seasons of daily use.

Do LED grow lights need extra cooling?

Yes. LEDs relocate heat rather than removing it — nearly all the wattage a fixture draws becomes heat in a closed tent either way — and a clip fan circulates air without exchanging it, so it addresses neither heat removal nor CO2 replenishment. Size an inline exhaust fan to the space and use the clip fan for leaf-level air movement on top of it.

How close can I put an LED light to my plants?

It depends on the fixture's wattage and the manufacturer's recommended hanging distance — always check that number specifically, since LEDs running cooler doesn't mean distance stops mattering.

How long do HPS bulbs actually last?

It depends which lamp, and almost nobody quoting a figure says. Manufacturers rate double-ended HPS lamps at 10,000 hours; older single-ended lamps were rated to 24,000. Output fades steadily long before the lamp stops striking, so those hours are a replacement point, not a failure point. At 16 hours a day, 10,000 hours is about 21 months — which is where the “12 to 18 months” that circulates online comes from. It is your photoperiod, not a property of the bulb.

Is HPS ever still the better choice?

Mainly if your budget is genuinely tight and you need light output on day one — HPS kits are often cheaper to buy, even though they cost more to run and generate more heat over time.
