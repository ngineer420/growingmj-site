LED vs. HPS Grow Lights, Compared

Lighting

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Photo by Plantlady223, licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0

For decades, high pressure sodium (HPS) lights were the default choice for serious indoor growing, and for good reason — they're cheap to buy and produce a lot of usable light. LED grow lights have since caught up and, for most home growers, overtaken HPS as the better all-around option. But "better" depends on what you're optimizing for, so here's a plain comparison across the factors that actually matter day to day.

Upfront Cost

HPS still wins here. A basic HPS fixture with a ballast and bulb is often cheaper than a comparable LED, especially at the low end of the market. If your budget is the primary constraint and you don't mind the tradeoffs below, an HPS kit can get you growing for less money on day one.

Electricity Cost Over Time

LED wins convincingly here, and it's not close. Modern LED fixtures convert a much higher fraction of the electricity they use into photosynthetically usable photons, which means lower wattage for comparable light output. Over a year of daily use, the difference in your electricity bill between an LED and an equivalent-output HPS setup is often enough to offset the LED's higher purchase price within the first one to two growing seasons.

Here's the math on a concrete example, because "lower running cost" is easy to nod along to and easy to underestimate. Say you're lighting a 4x4 tent. A 450W LED and a 600W HPS deliver roughly comparable usable light to that footprint. At 16 hours a day and the US average rate of about $0.15/kWh:

  • 450W LED: 216 kWh/month, about $32/month
  • 600W HPS: 288 kWh/month, about $43/month — plus, in warm months, whatever your exhaust fan and AC spend removing its heat

That's $130+ a year of direct savings before counting cooling or replacement bulbs. A $150 price gap between fixtures closes in about a season, exactly as the rule of thumb says.

How to Compare Light Output Honestly

Wattage is the number everyone shops by, and it's the least useful one on the box. Watts measure what the fixture pulls from the wall, not what reaches your plants. Two specs actually compare fixtures:

  • PPFD (photosynthetic photon flux density) — how much usable light lands on the canopy, in µmol/m²/s. As rough targets: seedlings and cuttings want 100-300, leafy greens and herbs 200-400, fruiting vegetables 400-700+. Decent manufacturers publish a PPFD map showing readings across the coverage area; a map that only shows the hot center reading is itself a warning sign.
  • Efficacy — µmol of light per joule of electricity. Good modern LEDs run 2.5-2.8 µmol/J. HPS lands around 1.7, and cheap "blurple" Amazon LEDs are often no better than that despite the marketing. If a fixture doesn't publish efficacy at all, assume there's a reason.

One more trap: "1000W equivalent" in a product title means nothing. Look for the actual draw wattage in the spec sheet — those fixtures usually pull 100-150W from the wall, which is fine for a 2x2 and hopeless for a 4x4.

Heat Output: Relocated, Not Removed

This is the practical difference beginners notice first, and it is also the one most often described wrongly. Nearly all the electrical power a fixture draws ends up as heat in the room — the fraction that leaves as light is absorbed by leaves, walls and floor and becomes heat there too. A 300 W LED and a 300 W HPS put roughly 300 W of heat into a closed tent. What differs is where that heat goes, not how much of it there is.

HPS radiates a large share of it downward onto the canopy; an LED sheds more of its heat by convection off heat sinks and driver, away from the leaf. Nelson and Bugbee measured the consequence: LEDs decrease leaf temperature by about 1.3 °C compared with HPS under typical indoor conditions. That is a genuine advantage and a much smaller one than the folklore claims. In the same paper's near-worst-case model of water stress and low wind, leaves ran about 8 °C above air temperature under LED against 12 °C under HPS. So:

  • You can usually mount an LED closer to the canopy, because less of its heat arrives at the leaf as radiation.
  • You still have to get the heat out of the space. Fixture wattage sets the exhaust requirement; the technology doesn't change it.
  • A clip fan is not a substitute for exhaust. Stirring air inside a tent is not the same as exchanging it. Virginia Tech reports CO2 in enclosed growing spaces measured as low as 200 ppm against roughly 430 ppm outside, because plants draw it down faster than a sealed space replaces it — and photosynthesis slows or stops as it falls. A circulating fan fixes neither heat removal nor CO2 replenishment. Size an exhaust path to the space, and run the clip fan for leaf-level air movement on top of it.

Light Spectrum

HPS light is heavily weighted toward the yellow-orange part of the spectrum, which works well but isn't a full match for what plants use across their whole life cycle. Quality LED fixtures, especially full-spectrum "quantum board" style panels, are built to output a broader spread of wavelengths closer to natural daylight, which tends to support healthier, more balanced growth from seedling through mature plant. This broader spectrum is the main reason growers switch to a quantum board style LED panel — look for one with genuine name-brand diodes and a bar-style layout that spreads light more evenly to the corners of the tent than a single dense panel at the same wattage (the Spider Farmer SF-4000 quantum board light is a popular pick for a 4x4 footprint that fits this description), which matters if you want the whole canopy to grow evenly instead of just the plants directly underneath.

Lifespan and Maintenance

HPS bulbs degrade in light output well before they actually burn out, and manufacturers generally recommend replacing them every 12 to 18 months of regular use even though the bulb still technically "works." LED fixtures commonly run for tens of thousands of hours — often 5+ years of daily use — before any noticeable drop in output. There's also no bulb to swap, no ballast to fail, and no glass to worry about breaking.

Noise and Bulk

HPS setups need a separate ballast (the box that regulates power to the bulb), which adds bulk, weight, and sometimes an audible hum. LED fixtures are typically a single flat panel with a built-in driver, mount flush to the tent's top bars, and run silently.

So Which Should You Buy?

For almost every home grower working in a tent-sized space, LED is the better long-term choice: lower running cost, less heat to manage, a longer usable lifespan, and a broader spectrum. The main scenario where HPS still makes sense is a very tight upfront budget where you need light output immediately and can tolerate the added heat, higher power bills, and bulb replacements down the line.

If you're buying your first light: pair a quality LED grow light (the Spider Farmer SF-4000 LED grow light is a solid pick) with a digital outlet timer so it runs on a consistent daily schedule (the BN-LINK 7-day digital outlet timer works well here), and a small clip fan for gentle air circulation (the Hurricane Classic clip fan is a good fit). A Quick Sizing Note

Whichever light you choose, check its recommended coverage area against your tent size (most manufacturers list this directly). A light rated for a 2x2 ft footprint will underperform in a 4x4 tent — you'll get healthy plants directly under the light and weak, stretchy growth toward the edges. When in doubt, size the light to match your tent, not the other way around.

The Middle Options Nobody Markets

The LED-vs-HPS framing skips two lights that are quietly right for certain jobs. T5 fluorescent fixtures are cheap, gentle, and still a perfectly good choice for seedlings, cuttings, and low-light herbs on a shelf — a two-bulb T5 costs $40 and won't cook anything you park an inch beneath it. And ceramic metal halide (CMH) sits between the two camps: better spectrum and efficiency than HPS, cheaper than a top-shelf LED, but it shares HPS's heat and ballast bulk. CMH had a real moment before quantum boards got cheap; nowadays it mostly makes sense used.

Speaking of which — the used market is full of lightly-used HPS kits from growers who switched to LED, often at $50-80 for a complete setup. If your budget is genuinely tight, a used HPS kit beats a new bargain-bin LED of unknown provenance. Check the bulb's age (assume it needs replacing, budget $20-30) and that the ballast doesn't buzz loudly enough to hear across a room.

What Dimming Is Actually For

Most quality LEDs now ship with a dimmer knob, and it's more useful than it looks. Run the fixture at 50-75% while plants are young and raise it as they grow — you save electricity, and you avoid the classic first-week mistake of blasting seedlings with a full-power light hung high, which wastes light, or hung low, which bleaches them. It also means you can buy the light your tent will eventually need rather than the one this month's seedlings need, and grow into it with the dimmer instead of buying twice. Frequently Asked Questions

Is LED or HPS cheaper overall?

HPS is cheaper upfront, but LED is almost always cheaper over the long run — lower electricity bills typically offset LED's higher purchase price within one to two growing seasons of daily use.

Do LED grow lights need extra cooling?

Yes. LEDs relocate heat rather than removing it — nearly all the wattage a fixture draws becomes heat in a closed tent either way — and a clip fan circulates air without exchanging it, so it addresses neither heat removal nor CO2 replenishment. Size an inline exhaust fan to the space and use the clip fan for leaf-level air movement on top of it.

How close can I put an LED light to my plants?

It depends on the fixture's wattage and the manufacturer's recommended hanging distance — always check that number specifically, since LEDs running cooler doesn't mean distance stops mattering.

How long do HPS bulbs actually last?

HPS bulbs technically keep working for a long time, but their light output degrades noticeably before they burn out, so manufacturers recommend replacing them every 12 to 18 months of regular use.

Is HPS ever still the better choice?

Mainly if your budget is genuinely tight and you need light output on day one — HPS kits are often cheaper to buy, even though they cost more to run and generate more heat over time.
