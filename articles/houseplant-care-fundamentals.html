Houseplant Care Fundamentals

Light, water, soil, and humidity basics that apply to almost every houseplant, plus how to diagnose common problems.

Houseplants Updated August 20, 2026 9 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

How do I know if my houseplant is getting enough light?

Read the plant rather than the room. If it has stopped growing, if new leaves are coming in smaller than the old ones, or if the gaps between leaves on new growth are stretching, it wants more light. The interior of a well-lighted home is often under 100 foot-candles against more than 10,000 outdoors, which is why "low light tolerant" plants like pothos and snake plant are surviving rather than thriving.

Houseplant advice online tends to get weirdly specific and species-by-species very quickly, which is useful once you have a particular plant with a particular problem, but not very helpful when you're just starting out. Underneath all the species-specific detail, though, almost every houseplant responds to the same four fundamentals: light, water, soil, and humidity. Get those roughly right and most common houseplants take care of themselves.

Light: Match the Plant to the Window, Not the Other Way Around

"Bright indirect light" is the most common care instruction on plant tags, and it trips people up because it's not obvious what it means in a real room. Clemson gives the sentence that puts it in proportion: the interior of a well-lighted home is often less than 100 foot-candles, against more than 10,000 outdoors on a sunny day.1 A hundredth. That gap is why "bright" indoors and "bright" outdoors are not the same word, and why a spot that looks perfectly cheerful to you can be a dim corner to a plant.

That reframes the categories on the tag. They are not three grades of brightness so much as three distances from a window:

  • Direct light — sun rays hitting the leaves directly, usually within a couple feet of a south- or west-facing window.
  • Bright indirect light — a room that's clearly sunlit but the plant itself isn't in the direct sun beam, often a few feet back from a bright window or near an east-facing window. Clemson's medium band, 200 to 500 foot-candles, lands within several feet of a west or south window, or in an eastern exposure.
  • Low light — a room with a window, but the plant is far from it or the window itself is small or north-facing. UGA measured a northern windowsill as low as 200 foot-candles on a clear winter day, which is fine for an African violet and thin for most other things.

You do not need a meter to tell whether you got it wrong, because the plant reports. UGA's list of signs that light is short: the plant stops growing; new internodes are much longer than the old ones; new leaves come in smaller than the old ones; new foliage is a lighter green than old foliage; the oldest leaves die.2 Three of those five are things people usually blame on water.

Rather than forcing a plant that wants bright indirect light to survive in a genuinely dim corner, move it nearer a window or accept a small supplemental grow light. And treat the "low light tolerant" label as exactly that word: pothos, snake plant and ZZ plant tolerate dim corners, they do not prefer them, and all three grow faster and fuller a few feet from a window.

Water: The Finger Test Beats a Fixed Schedule

Watering on a fixed schedule ("every Sunday") ignores that a plant's water needs change with season, light, humidity and pot size. Both UGA and Clemson give the same test in different words: push a finger into the mix about an inch, to the first joint. If it is still moist there, wait. If your fingertip comes out dry, water — and water properly, meaning until it runs out of the bottom of the pot. Clemson's reason for that is the one people miss: a full watering is what carries water to the bottom two-thirds of the pot, where most of the roots are, and it washes accumulated salts out on the way through. Then empty the saucer so nothing sits in it.23

An inch, not the two this page used to say. In a four-inch pot, two inches is halfway down, and a plant whose mix is dry that deep has been thirsty for a while.

If you consistently forget to check, or want a reading rather than a guess, a capacitive soil moisture meter measures water content more directly. Treat whatever it says as a cross-check on your finger rather than a replacement for it — Clemson's verdict on the category is that accuracy varies widely and that both fertilizer and soil type move the reading.3 That is worst on the classic battery-free probes, the ones with two bare prongs and a color-coded dial, which respond to electrical conductivity: fertilizer salts in a fed pot drag them toward "wet" no matter how dry the mix is. For plants you know you will forget, a self-watering planter with a visible reservoir moves the decision from remembering to glancing. How long it buys you depends on the plant, the pot and the season, so read the gauge rather than the calendar.

Soil: Drainage Is the Whole Point

Almost every common houseplant problem traced back to "soil" is really a drainage problem. Dense, compacted soil (or garden soil used in a pot, which is a common mistake) holds water against roots for too long, encouraging rot. A good indoor potting mix — lightweight, with perlite already blended in for aeration — combined with a pot that actually has a drainage hole solves most of this automatically. Read the panel for a peat or coir base and no topsoil, and don't buy a mix on a promise about fungus gnats. Gnats lay in whatever surface stays damp, and Colorado State names consistently moist, peat-heavy media as the substrate egg-laying females find most attractive.4 It is a watering problem wearing an ingredients label. If a favorite pot doesn't have drainage, it's usually better used as a decorative outer cover for a plastic nursery pot with drainage, rather than planting directly into it.

Humidity: Which Plants Actually Care

Plants native to tropical understories (many ferns, calatheas, and similar foliage plants) evolved in consistently humid air and struggle in the dry conditions common in heated or air-conditioned homes — typically showing crispy leaf edges or browning tips. Plants native to arid regions (succulents, cacti, snake plants) are the opposite and actually prefer drier air. Knowing which category a given plant falls into explains a lot of "why does this keep looking unhappy" situations that aren't about water or light at all.

There are two numbers worth carrying. Nebraska Extension puts the preferred range at 40 to 60% relative humidity, and puts injury — leaf drying and curling — below 20%, with succulents excepted. That lower figure is not hypothetical: a heated house in winter reaches it.5

The fixes are not equivalent, and extension is willing to rank them where most articles list them flat. New Hampshire Extension puts a portable humidifier placed near the plants first, and says it will need to run for hours a day to hold a target; grouping plants second, because they raise humidity for each other in a small room with low airflow; and a pebble tray third, in the specific words "a small benefit".6 Which is not nothing — it is just not a substitute for the first one, and it gets recommended as though it were.

Before any of them, a basic hygrometer tells you the actual number in the room instead of letting you guess from how the air feels. For one room, that's all you need.

Reading the Warning Signs

SymptomLikely cause
Yellow, soft, or mushy leavesOverwatering / poor drainage
Crispy brown leaf tips or edgesLow humidity, inconsistent watering, salt build-up, or fluoride — see below
Leggy growth, leaning hard toward a windowNot enough light
Bleached or scorched patches on leavesDirect sun or grow light too close/intense for that species
Slow growth, small new leavesNeeds repotting, or lacking nutrients/light

The crispy-tip row is the one that gets answered wrong most often, because "it needs more humidity" is the first answer everyone reaches for and there are at least four candidates. Two are about water: not enough of it, or not enough of it at once. The third is soluble salts from fertilizer, which UGA describes as burning roots by drawing water back out of them, showing up as leaf margin and tip burn with a whitish crust on the pot rim or the surface of the mix.2

The fourth is fluoride in tap water, which many municipal supplies add at about 1 ppm; it accumulates in the foliage, moves out to the leaf margins in the transpiration stream, and produces exactly the marginal and tip necrosis you are looking at. Dracaena and the spider plant are the classic indicators, which is awkward given how often both get recommended as beginner plants.7

Worth knowing before you change anything: letting tap water stand overnight is a fix for chlorine, which leaves. Fluoride does not — inorganic fluorides stay in solution. If that is your problem the answers are filtration, keeping the mix from going acidic, and avoiding high-phosphate fertilizers, which carry fluoride of their own.

Feeding: Less Than the Bottle Says

Houseplants need far less fertilizer than the bottle implies. UGA puts the starting point at about one-quarter of the label rate, monthly, and — better than any rate — gives a feedback rule for adjusting it: if overall color goes lighter green, move to every two weeks; if new growth is dark green but the leaves are small and the internodes longer than on older growth, you are feeding too hard.2 That second signal is the useful one, because it looks like vigor until you know what it is.

Use a complete liquid feed and let "complete" do the work: turn the bottle over and check the guaranteed analysis lists the micronutrients, not just N-P-K. Feed during the growing season only. Under low winter light a plant has little use for more nutrients, and feeding anyway just accumulates salts — the white crust on the pot rim.

When the crust shows up, leach it out. Nebraska's procedure is more specific than the usual "flush it": let water filter down through the mix and out of the drainage hole, let the mix dry, and then repeat that four or five times. One pass moves some of the salt; the repetitions are the point.5

Winter Is a Different Season Indoors Too

Almost every houseplant question I get between November and February has the same answer: it's winter. Light drops to a fraction of summer levels, heated air takes indoor humidity down — Nebraska says a home can reach the under-20% range where plants start taking damage — and growth slows to a crawl.5 The adjustments are all in the direction of doing less: water less often (the mix dries slower with less growth and light), stop fertilizing, and don't panic over a few yellow leaves on an otherwise stable plant. The exceptions worth acting on: move plants back from cold windows at night, keep leaves off freezing glass, and consider a small cool-mist humidifier for the crispy-leaf tropicals — one you can refill from the tap without inverting the tank, since you'll be doing it every couple of days. Come March, the same plant that sulked all winter often puts out more growth in six weeks than it did in the previous six months.

Repotting: On the Roots, Not the Calendar

This page used to give an interval — every one to two years — and there isn't one. A fast grower may want it annually and a slow one may go three years, and neither of them is reading a calendar. Purdue's test is to take the plant out of its pot and look at the root ball: a few roots showing on the outside means leave it, many roots showing means repot, and the correct pot is the next size larger, so the mix doesn't stay wet too long.8 You can see the same thing from outside the pot when it is further along: roots circling the surface or coming out of the drainage hole, or water running straight through because roots have displaced most of the mix.

Pruning and General Maintenance

Trimming yellowed or dead leaves keeps a plant looking healthy and redirects energy away from dying growth. A pair of micro-tip snips makes clean cuts, which heal faster and look tidier than leaves torn off by hand. Look for bypass blades rather than anvil, and tips narrow enough to reach into a crowded pot without taking three other stems with them.

A Starter Shelf That's Hard to Kill

If you're building a first collection and want wins rather than lessons: pothos (tolerates nearly everything, and tells you it's thirsty by drooping a day before it matters), snake plant (water it monthly, ignore it otherwise), a ZZ plant (same), spider plant (grows babies you can pot up — see the propagation guide), and a heartleaf philodendron. All five turn up on supermarket and hardware-store shelves, which is where to start rather than a specialist nursery. Conspicuously absent: fiddle-leaf figs, calatheas, and anything sold as "rare" — all real plants people successfully keep, and all terrible first plants that mostly teach discouragement.

If you have a cat or a dog, four of those five plants are toxic. The ASPCA toxic plant database lists golden pothos (Epipremnum aureum) and heartleaf philodendron (Philodendron hederaceum) as toxic to dogs and cats — insoluble calcium oxalates, causing oral irritation, burning of the mouth and lips, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing — and snake plant (Sansevieria trifasciata) as toxic to both on saponins, causing nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.9 The botanical names are there for a reason: that database is indexed on them, and several common names in it belong to a different plant than the one you would assume.

ZZ plant is not in the ASPCA database at all, but NC State Extension's plant toolbox records Zamioculcas zamiifolia as a problem for dogs, of medium poison severity, with calcium oxalate as the toxic principle and vomiting and diarrhea if the leaves are eaten in quantity.10 Spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) is the one on the list the ASPCA records as non-toxic to dogs and cats. That doesn't make the other four unkeepable — it makes shelf height, hanging pots and sweeping up dropped leaves worth thinking about. If a pet chews one, call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 or your own vet.

The Real Takeaway

You don't need care instructions for every species you own. You need to know roughly how much light your rooms actually get, to check the mix instead of the calendar, to use something that drains into a pot with a hole in it, and to know which of your plants came from a humid place. Nearly every specific question is one of those four wearing a costume.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my houseplant is getting enough light?

Read the plant rather than the room. If it has stopped growing, if new leaves are coming in smaller than the old ones, or if the gaps between leaves on new growth are stretching, it wants more light. The interior of a well-lighted home is often under 100 foot-candles against more than 10,000 outdoors, which is why "low light tolerant" plants like pothos and snake plant are surviving rather than thriving.

How often should I water my houseplants?

Skip the fixed schedule. Push a finger into the mix about an inch, to the first joint: if it's still moist there, wait; if your fingertip comes out dry, water until it runs out of the bottom of the pot, then empty the saucer.

How often do houseplants need repotting?

There is no interval, only a test. Slide the plant out of its pot and look at the root ball: a few roots showing on the outside means leave it alone, many roots showing means move it up one pot size. Jumping several sizes at once surrounds the root ball with wet, unrooted mix and causes the problem it was meant to prevent.

Why are my houseplant's leaf tips turning brown and crispy?

There are four usual causes, not one: low humidity, watering too little or too shallowly, salt build-up from fertilizer, and fluoride in tap water — spider plant and Dracaena are the classic fluoride indicators. Standing water overnight releases chlorine but not fluoride, so work through the other three before blaming the air.

Does every houseplant need high humidity?

No — plants from arid regions (succulents, cacti, snake plants) actually prefer drier air. Match the plant's native habitat to its humidity needs rather than assuming all houseplants want the same conditions.
