Choosing a Grow Medium: Soil vs. Coco Coir vs. Rockwool

An honest comparison of soil, coco coir, and rockwool as growing mediums, and which one fits your indoor setup and experience level best.

Grow Tents & Setups Updated August 20, 2026 8 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

Which grow medium is easiest for beginners?

A bagged soilless potting mix. Its peat or coir base carries real cation exchange capacity, so it holds nutrients between feedings and absorbs a mistake instead of passing it straight to the roots. Rockwool is the opposite — it holds nothing, which is exactly why growers who want control choose it.

The medium decides how often you water, whether you have to feed every time, and how much a mistake costs you. It is worth choosing deliberately rather than by whatever the tent kit came with. What separates the three is one property with an unglamorous name — cation exchange capacity, the substrate's ability to hold positively charged nutrient ions against the pull of water — and that number runs from high in a peat or coir mix to approximately zero in stone wool.

Soil: The Forgiving Default

A bagged soilless potting mix is where almost everyone starts, and it earns the position. Peat and coir both sit in Purdue's high-CEC list, so the mix holds onto what you feed it and releases it gradually; that slack is the whole of what "forgiving" means.

  • What you get: plain water works for a while, the pH moves slowly rather than instantly, and a skipped feed is not an event.
  • What you give up: weight, mess, slower drainage if you overwater, and a permanently damp surface that indoor pests find attractive.

Reach for a bagged indoor potting mix and read the ingredient panel before you buy: a peat or coir base with perlite already blended in, and no garden soil or topsoil in the list. What to ignore is any claim about keeping fungus gnats away. Colorado State: "moist-growing media containing high amounts of peat moss are particularly attractive to adult females," and the control is letting the top 1 to 2 inches dry between waterings, because dry medium kills the eggs and larvae in it and makes the surface less attractive to lay in.1 Gnats are a moisture problem. No bag fixes that.

Coco Coir, and the Buffer Myth

Processed coconut husk, behaving a bit like peat but draining faster and holding more air. This page used to tell you that coco "holds almost no nutrients on its own, so there's no buffer the way there is with soil," and that it "naturally binds calcium and magnesium." Both are wrong, and the second is the shape of invented mechanism this site has had to retract before.

Purdue puts coconut fibre in the high-CEC group, alongside clay, peat moss and vermiculite — and puts rock wool and perlite in the low group. Coir holds nutrients. What is actually true, and more useful, is compositional: "coir-based substrates require more Ca, sulfur (S), Cu, and Fe; they also require less K."2 That is a real difference and it explains the calcium and magnesium supplements coco growers reach for, without pretending the substrate is chemically grabbing them.

The other genuine coir problem is what it arrives carrying. Purdue again: the electrical conductivity of coir substrates "can exceed the recommended levels for container-grown plants" because coir that "was not properly processed (soaked and rinsed)" may hold excessive potassium, sodium and chloride.2 So check the bag says buffered or pre-rinsed, or rinse it yourself before it goes anywhere near a plant. Buy a brick that blends coir fibre with coco chips rather than straight fibre — the chips hold air open as the fibre settles. Rehydrate outside the final container, and read the expanded volume printed on your own brick rather than trusting a rule of thumb; vendor figures for how far a brick expands differ by a factor of nearly three, so there is no single number to give you.

On sustainability, the honest answer is that it is contested and neither peat nor coir is cleanly ahead. What is documented for coir is land-use change during production, water pollution during processing, deforestation in coconut-growing regions "particularly evident in places like the Pacific Islands," and a high CO2 footprint from shipping a bulky material out of Asia.3 Two claims that circulate alongside those — that salt-washing consumes large volumes of fresh water, and that coir production involves poor labour conditions — are not established by that paper, and we could not find a source for either, so this page no longer implies them.

Rockwool: The Conditioning Step Is the Trick

Spun mineral fibre, sold as small cubes for propagation and larger blocks for mature plants. Its appeal is uniformity: every cube in the sheet is the same density and the same size, so they all dry at the same rate and one seedling isn't sitting soaked while its neighbour goes dry. Grodan describes its own product as inert with minimal cation exchange capacity, and as sterilized of pathogens.4 Take that as a manufacturer's claim about its own material, which is what it is — but it is also the honest description of the trade: nothing in it means nothing working for you, and nothing working against you either.

Everything people find hard about rockwool is really the conditioning soak, and the numbers for it are published. Grodan specifies a balanced nutrient solution at EC 1.5 to 3.0 mS/cm, pH adjusted to 5.5 to 6.5, at about 68 °F, with the blocks held under until the bubbling stops. It also carries a warning worth more than the recipe: do not go below pH 5.2, because "a lower pH used during initial saturation and at any time in the cultivation process will dissolve the stone wool fibres resulting in a loss of structural integrity."4 That is a failure mode nobody warns beginners about, and it comes from over-correcting with pH-down.

Note what we are not giving you: a starting pH for dry stone wool. That number appears all over the internet and we could not find it in any non-blog source — Grodan itself never publishes one, and calls the material inert rather than alkaline or pH-neutral. The conditioning target above is the actionable figure and the only one we can stand behind. Pre-soak your rockwool starter cubes to it.

One property explains most rockwool watering mistakes. After a slab drains freely it typically holds about 80% nutrient solution and 15% air by volume, and that water is not evenly distributed: the base sits near saturation while the upper layer is much drier. Growers who don't know about the gradient feel a dry top, decide the block needs water, and drown the roots underneath.5

Handling stone wool, without the scare story. Fresh stone wool sheds coarse fibres that cause "temporary and reversible irritation (itching, redness) of the skin and eyes," and the manufacturer is explicit that this is "a mechanical reaction to dust and coarse fibers… not damaging in the way chemical irritants may be." Wear gloves and long sleeves, keep dust down, clean up by vacuuming or dampening the surface rather than dry-sweeping, wash your hands in cold water afterwards, and never use compressed air to blow fibres off skin or clothing.6 On the question people actually worry about: IARC's evaluation of man-made vitreous fibres puts insulation glass wool, continuous glass filament, rock (stone) wool and slag wool in Group 3 — not classifiable as to their carcinogenicity to humans.7 Itchy, then, and worth gloves. Not the hazard the forums make of it.

How Each Medium Changes Your Week

The comparison that matters day to day is what your watering routine becomes.

  • In a potting mix: water when the medium says so rather than on a schedule — the top inch or two dry, or the pot noticeably light when you lift it. This page used to say "every 3-7 days"; no source supports a calendar interval, and the pot's weight is a better instrument than any of them. Feed lightly once the mix's own charge runs down.
  • In coco: nutrient solution at most waterings, roughly daily to every other day, and never let it go bone dry — dry coir is hard to rewet evenly and you end up with a channel down one side of the pot.
  • In rockwool: measured feeds on a schedule, pH-adjusted every time. Pair it with a drip system or use it only for propagation.

"Water until you get some runoff" is the standard advice and it is right, but the amount is not one number. Virginia Tech ties the target leaching fraction to the electrical conductivity of the water you're applying: 0.1 (10%) below EC 1.0 dS/m, 0.2 at 1.5, and 0.3 above 2.0 — saltier water in, more runoff needed to carry the salt back out.8 Grodan works the same problem from the crop's side and asks for 5-15% runoff in vegetative growth rising to 15-25% in flower.9 Both beat the flat "10-20%" this page used to print, because both tell you which way to move it.

The reason any of this matters more in an inert medium is pH. Purdue's soilless range is narrow: "all nutrients are readily available at a pH of 5.4 to 6.2," and each species has its own optimum inside that.10 A peat-based mix drifts through that window slowly. Rockwool has nothing to slow it down, which is the real content of "least forgiving."

I learned the coco version of this the annoying way: moved a pepper from a potting mix into coco and kept watering it like a potting mix, generously but twice a week. Three weeks later it had tip burn and uneven growth. The medium hadn't done anything to the calcium; I had simply stopped delivering it, because in coco the water is the feed.

What a Medium Actually Costs to Run

This section used to carry four retail prices. They are gone, because nobody publishes prices for hobby growing supplies and a figure that ages silently is worse than no figure. What doesn't decay is the shape:

  • A bagged potting mix is the cheapest to start and the only one of the three that might carry a plant with no nutrient bill at all for the first weeks.
  • A compressed coir brick is cheaper per litre of finished medium than bagged mix, and buys you a mandatory nutrient bill in exchange. Count the bottles as part of the price of coco, because they are.
  • Rockwool is the smallest line of the three by volume — you buy cubes, not cubic feet — and the only one that is strictly single-use.

Over a year, the gap between them is small enough that it should not decide anything. Pick the medium that matches the attention you will actually give it.

Perlite: The Common Add-In

Those white volcanic granules open a mix up and add air around the roots. How much to add is the question, and the honest answer is a range rather than a rule: across LSU's published container recipes, perlite runs from one part in three in a seed-starting mix to one part in eight in a mix for acid-loving plants, clustering around one in four to five.11 This page used to state 20-30% by volume as though it were settled; it isn't, and the recipes are more useful than an average of them. Buy horticultural perlite in a coarse grade and check the bag says 100% perlite with no fertilizer added. (We have dropped the line telling you to look for an OMRI listing "if you plan to eat it" — OMRI listing is an organic-compliance determination, not a food-safety one, and the two are not the same claim.)

Perlite dust is worth a sentence and not a paragraph. It is amorphous silica — volcanic glass — "with little to no crystalline silica content by volume," and it is regulated as a nuisance dust: OSHA's limits for it are the particulates-not-otherwise-regulated figures of 15 mg/m³ total and 5 mg/m³ respirable.12 That is a different category from the crystalline silica that causes silicosis, and getting that wrong in the alarming direction would be its own new error. Open the bag away from your face and don't work in a cloud of it.

You don't have to pick just one. Plenty of growers start seeds and cuttings in rockwool cubes for the even moisture, then transplant into a potting mix or coco for the rest of the plant's life. That gets you the propagation consistency without committing to a hydroponic feeding schedule.

Which One Should You Actually Pick?

New to indoor growing: a bagged soilless mix, and spend the attention you save on light instead. Comfortable with a routine and willing to feed every watering: coco, bought buffered, with the calcium and iron the substrate asks for. Already running or planning a drip or hydroponic system: rockwool, conditioned properly, with the pH meter calibrated. And whichever you pick, the answer to "when do I water?" is the medium and the weight of the pot, not the day of the week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which grow medium is easiest for beginners?

A bagged soilless potting mix. Its peat or coir base carries real cation exchange capacity, so it holds nutrients between feedings and absorbs a mistake instead of passing it straight to the roots. Rockwool is the opposite — it holds nothing, which is exactly why growers who want control choose it.

Does coco coir need different nutrients than soil?

Different amounts, not a different bottle. Purdue states that coir-based substrates require more calcium, sulfur, copper and iron, and less potassium, than peat-based ones. Coir that has not been properly soaked and rinsed can also arrive carrying enough potassium, sodium and chloride to push electrical conductivity above the recommended range, so buy it buffered or rinse it yourself.

Why does rockwool need to be conditioned before use?

Because it is completely inert and arrives with nothing in it, so the first solution you soak it in is the entire root-zone chemistry. Grodan specifies a balanced nutrient solution at EC 1.5 to 3.0 mS/cm, pH adjusted to 5.5 to 6.5, around 68 °F, and warns not to go below pH 5.2 — a lower pH dissolves the stone wool fibres and the block loses structural integrity.

Can I use more than one growing medium in the same setup?

Yes — a common approach is starting seeds or cuttings in rockwool cubes for even moisture, then transplanting into soil or coco coir for the plant's full life cycle.

Is coco coir more sustainable than peat?

It is genuinely contested, and the honest answer is that neither is cleanly ahead. The drawbacks documented for coir are land-use change during production, water pollution during processing, deforestation in coconut-growing regions, and a high CO2 footprint from shipping it out of Asia. Claims you may also have read about water consumption in salt-washing and about labour conditions are not established by that source.
