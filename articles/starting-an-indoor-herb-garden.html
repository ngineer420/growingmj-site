Starting an Indoor Herb Garden

Which herbs actually thrive indoors, light requirements, and how to avoid leggy, weak growth in a windowsill or counter herb garden.

An indoor herb garden is the cheapest way into indoor growing, and it is also the one most likely to be quietly disappointing, because the failure is slow. Nothing dies in a week. The plant just stretches, pales, and stops being worth walking over to. Almost always that is light, and the amount of it a kitchen window supplies is the one number worth getting straight before you buy anything.

Which Herbs Actually Do Well Indoors

Iowa State Extension divides culinary herbs into two useful groups. Its shortlist of the best herbs to grow indoors is parsley, mint, chives, basil, oregano, cilantro and thyme — the ones that tolerate the lower light, low humidity and cooler temperatures of a house. The next group down is rosemary, sage, lemon balm, marjoram, chervil and bay laurel, which it describes as good species to try next, since they "can be successful indoors but have higher than normal light requirements and/or significant pests (like spider mites) when grown indoors."1

Cilantro is on the easy list but comes with a caveat the list does not carry: it bolts. A cilantro plant that runs to flower is not sick, it is finished, and the answer is a fresh sowing every few weeks rather than a rescue.

Rosemary is not the beginner herb it gets sold as, and this page used to say it was. It sits in Iowa State's second tier, and both of the reasons it gives bite indoors specifically: it wants more light than a windowsill supplies, and the dry indoor air that suits it in principle is exactly what spider mites like.1 Our propagation guide rates rosemary Hard for an unrelated reason — cuttings are slow and often rot in water — and the two ratings now agree that it is not a first herb.

Eight Hours, and What a Window Gives You

Iowa State's number is the place to start: most herbs need around eight hours of direct light each day when grown indoors, some will tolerate as little as six, and most want eight or more.1 "Direct" is the word doing the work. A room that reads bright to your eye can be delivering an hour or two of actual sun on the leaf.

Hours alone still don't settle it, because a dim hour and a bright hour are not the same hour. The figure that combines the two is the daily light integral — total photons landing on a square metre over a day — and you get it from an intensity reading with one multiplication:

DLI = PPFD × hours × 0.0036

Run the herbs through it. A small LED bar holding 300 µmol/m²/s at leaf height for 16 hours gives 17.3 mol/m²/day, which lands inside basil's 15-25 band and well past parsley's 10-15. Drop that to 200 for 14 hours and you get 10.1 — enough for parsley, short for basil, nowhere near cilantro. Same fixture, same herbs, completely different outcomes, and the difference is not a mystery about "good light" but two numbers you can look up.2 Our DLI calculator does the multiplication if you'd rather not.

If you have no meter, UMN's intensity bands are a usable proxy for what a fixture needs to deliver: 50-150 µmol/m²/s is low light, 150-250 medium, 250-450 high.3 Basil is a high-light plant on that scale and a windowsill is not, which is the whole story of most dead indoor basil.

I killed three of them before I accepted it. All three sat on what I would have sworn was a bright kitchen windowsill, all three stretched and paled over about six weeks, and I responded by watering more, which is the wrong lever pulled harder. The window faced east. A clip-on LED bar fixed it so completely that the fourth plant became a pruning problem instead.

If your window isn't cutting it, the fix is small. Iowa State puts lights 6 to 12 inches from the leaves on a timer set for 12 to 16 hours a day.1 A small LED bar clipped under the shelf above a windowsill covers a row of pots at that height for very little. An all-in-one countertop herb garden does the same job with the timer and the watering built in, at the cost of a fixed number of plant sites; check before buying whether it accepts ordinary seed and plain nutrient solution or only the maker's own refill pods, because that one detail decides what it costs to run.

Pots, Drainage, and Soil

Drainage holes, and that is most of it. Use a bagged indoor potting mix rather than garden soil, which compacts in a container, and read the panel for a peat or coir base with perlite in it and no topsoil. What you should ignore is any claim on the bag about fungus gnats. Colorado State is blunt about which media the egg-laying females prefer: "moist-growing media containing high amounts of peat moss are particularly attractive to adult females."4 That describes almost every indoor potting mix sold, including the ones advertising gnat resistance. Gnats are a moisture problem, and the watering section below is the actual control.

Watering: Check the Pot, Not the Calendar

Clemson's test costs nothing and needs no equipment: push a finger into the mix up to the first joint, and if it is dry at your fingertip, water. Then water thoroughly — until it runs out of the bottom of the pot, which both wets the lower two-thirds where most of the roots are and washes out accumulated salts. Empty the saucer afterwards rather than letting the pot stand in what came through.5

Letting the top 1 to 2 inches dry between waterings is also the single most effective thing you can do about fungus gnats, because dry medium kills the eggs and larvae in it and makes the surface less attractive to females looking for somewhere to lay.4 One habit, two problems.

If you would rather read an instrument than a finger, buy a capacitive soil moisture meter and treat it as a cross-check rather than an oracle. Clemson's own caveat applies to all of them: accuracy varies widely, and "fertilizer and soil type can affect the reading."5 That is worst on the battery-free dial probes with two bare prongs, which respond to conductivity, so a fed pot reads wet whatever the soil is doing.

Starting From Seed vs. Buying Small Plants

Starter plants are the faster, more forgiving path for a first herb garden; seed is cheaper and gives you varieties no grocery store carries. If you go the seed route for slow starters like parsley, a heat mat with a tray and vented dome helps. Hydrofarm rates its windowsill mat at "10-20°F over ambient temperature," which is a manufacturer's figure for its own product rather than a horticultural finding, so treat it as the size of the bump and not as a soil temperature.6 Our seed starting guide has the per-crop soil temperatures that actually matter.

Harvesting Is the Pruning

Cutting an herb is what keeps it compact, and Iowa State gives the amounts. On annuals — basil, chervil, parsley — cut just above a leaf or pair of leaves and leave roughly 4 to 6 inches of stem standing. On perennials like chives, mint, oregano and thyme, take the top third of the growth.1

Basil gets its own paragraph, because the near-universal advice about it appears to be wrong. Every guide, including an older version of this one, tells you to pinch off flower buds to push the plant back into leaf. Utah State Extension says flatly that "pinching off the flowers as they form does not stimulate new foliage; in fact it encourages flowers to form in the axils of the leaves thus reducing the yield of the plant." What works instead is harvesting before flowers form at all: once a plant has 6-8 leaves, cut back to leave only 2-4, and rotate which plants you cut.7 Same scissors, different timing, and the timing is the part that matters.

A small pair of pruning snips makes cleaner cuts than pulling leaves off by hand. Spring-action handles are the feature worth having if you are working several small pots at once.

Feeding: Half Strength, and Not in Winter

Iowa State's regime is short enough to quote in full: herbs "do not require as much fertilizer as other container plants but they do benefit from periodic light fertilization with an all-purpose fertilizer solution at half or quarter strength," applied from early spring through summer, avoiding the winter months entirely.1 A general-purpose liquid fertilizer diluted that far lasts a long time.

This page used to explain that overfeeding dulls flavour because the aromatic oils get diluted. We cut that, because the research on nitrogen and basil oil genuinely disagrees with itself — nitrogen has been found to raise leaf oil concentration, to lower it at high rates, and to change nothing. The practical advice survives without the mechanism: herbs need less fertilizer than other container plants, and there is no version of this where feeding harder gets you a better-tasting plant.

The November Problem

Day length collapses, the sun drops in the sky, and the windowsill becomes the coldest, draughtiest place in the house. Indoor air gets very dry at the same time: Iowa State puts many houseplants' preference at 40 to 50 percent relative humidity, and notes that homes in winter "may be only 10 to 20 percent."8 An older version of this page said radiators dry the air to 30 percent, which was understating it by half.

Basil is the one to move. Rutgers describes it as susceptible to chilling injury — blackened leaves, stem discoloration, loss of aroma — and puts its optimal storage temperature at 55°F, well above the fridge temperature that suits most cut herbs.9 That is a postharvest figure rather than a growing threshold, so don't read it as a hard line, but a pane of glass on a January night gets colder than anything in that paper and the plant leaning against it is the one that goes black. Move it a foot back from the glass. A timer-driven light removes the day-length half of the problem entirely, which is why winter is when most people finally buy one.

When You Have More Than You Can Eat

Two directions for the surplus: dry it (see harvesting and drying herbs — oregano and thyme dry well, basil is better frozen) or root cuttings into new plants for other people, which takes a glass of water and a windowsill and is covered in the propagation guide.

A Realistic First Setup

Two or three 4-6 inch pots with drainage holes, a bag of indoor potting mix, small parsley, mint and chive plants from a nursery, and whichever is true of your kitchen: your brightest window, or a cheap LED bar on a timer above it. Add basil once the first three are alive and you know what your light actually does, because basil is the one that will tell you the truth about it. We are not going to quote a total — nobody publishes prices for this and the figure this page used to give was invented — but the light is the only line on that list that costs real money.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which herbs are easiest to grow indoors?

Iowa State Extension's shortlist for indoor growing is parsley, mint, chives, basil, oregano, cilantro and thyme. Rosemary and sage sit in its next group down — workable, but they want more light than a window usually gives, and rosemary attracts spider mites in dry indoor air.

Why does my indoor basil keep getting leggy?

Legginess is a light problem, not a watering problem. Iowa State puts most culinary herbs at about eight hours of direct light a day indoors, and basil wants the most light of any herb on its beginner list — Virginia Tech's target for it is 15 to 25 mol per square metre per day, which a winter windowsill does not reach.

How often should I water indoor herbs?

Check the pot, not the calendar. Clemson's test is a finger pushed into the mix to the first joint: if it is dry at your fingertip, water, and water until it runs out of the bottom. Letting the top inch or two dry between waterings also makes the surface much less attractive to egg-laying fungus gnats.

Do I need a grow light for an indoor herb garden?

For parsley, chives and mint, a genuinely bright window is often enough. For basil and cilantro it usually isn't, because their published daily light targets are two to three times a parsley's. A small LED bar on a 12 to 16 hour timer, 6 to 12 inches above the leaves, is the cheap fix.

Should I start herbs from seed or buy small plants?

For a first indoor herb garden, buying small starter plants is faster and more forgiving. Seeds are cheaper and offer more variety, but add weeks of waiting and more chances for something to go wrong.
