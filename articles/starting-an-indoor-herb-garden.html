Starting an Indoor Herb Garden

Indoor Herbs & Kitchen Gardens

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Photo by futureshape, licensed under CC BY 2.0

An indoor herb garden is the easiest, cheapest, and most immediately rewarding way to get into indoor growing. Fresh basil, mint, or chives a few steps from the stove is genuinely useful, and unlike a full tent setup, you can start with nothing more than a sunny windowsill and a few pots.

Which Herbs Actually Do Well Indoors

Not every herb is equally suited to indoor life. Some good starting choices:

  • Basil — fast-growing and forgiving, but wants a lot of light; one of the first to get leggy if light is weak.
  • Mint — nearly impossible to kill indoors, and actually benefits from being contained in a pot rather than let loose in a garden bed where it spreads aggressively.
  • Chives — tolerant of lower light than basil, and regrows quickly after cutting.
  • Thyme — slower growing but tolerant of drying out between waterings, which suits inconsistent indoor watering schedules.
  • Parsley — reliable, but slow to germinate from seed; starting from a small nursery plant saves weeks.

Cilantro and dill are trickier indoors — they tend to bolt (flower and go to seed early) under indoor conditions, so treat them as more of an intermediate project once you're comfortable with the basics.

Rosemary is not the beginner herb it gets sold as, and this page used to say it was. Iowa State Extension's list of the best herbs to start with indoors is parsley, mint, chives, basil, oregano, cilantro and thyme. Rosemary is in the next group down — "good species to try next", which "can be successful indoors but have higher than normal light requirements and/or significant pests (like spider mites) when grown indoors." Both of those bite indoors specifically: it wants more light than a windowsill gives, and the dry indoor air that suits it in principle is exactly what spider mites like. Iowa State also notes it wants to dry only slightly between waterings, not the hard dry spells the "drought-tolerant" label implies. Our propagation guide rates rosemary Hard for a separate reason — cuttings are slow and often fail in water — and the two ratings now agree that it is not a first herb.

Light Is the Limiting Factor

The single biggest reason indoor herb gardens fail isn't watering — it's light. Iowa State Extension puts it at about eight hours of direct light a day indoors: some herbs tolerate as little as six, but most want eight or more. A "bright" windowsill often delivers far less than growers assume, especially in winter or in north-facing rooms. The telltale sign of insufficient light is legginess: long, thin, weak stems stretching toward the nearest light source, with sparse leaves.

If your windowsill isn't cutting it, a grow light solves the problem completely and removes the guesswork. An all-in-one countertop herb garden with a timer-driven LED and an aerating pump handles the two things beginners get wrong most, inconsistent light and inconsistent watering, at the cost of a small fixed number of plant sites and a consumable you top up every week or two. Check before buying whether the one you're looking at accepts ordinary seeds and plain nutrient solution or only the maker's own refill pods; that single detail decides what it costs to run. If you'd rather use your own pots, a small LED bar clipped above a windowsill shelf works just as well for less money.

I killed three basil plants before I accepted this. All three sat on what I'd have sworn was a bright kitchen windowsill. All three stretched, paled, dropped leaves, and died over about six weeks — while I responded by watering them more, which is exactly the wrong lever. The window faced east and got maybe three hours of real sun. A $30 clip-on LED fixed the problem so completely that the fourth basil plant became a pruning problem instead.

To put numbers on it: full summer sun is roughly 100,000 lux. A bright south windowsill in winter might deliver 5,000-10,000 for a few hours. Basil wants the equivalent of 6+ hours of direct sun; under a grow light, that translates to a PPFD around 200-400 for 14-16 hours a day, which any small LED herb light can manage from 6-12 inches away. Your eyes adapt so well to dim light that they're useless as a meter — a "bright" room is routinely a tenth as bright as it feels. The stretch of the plants is the honest measurement.

Pots, Drainage, and Soil

Whatever container you use, drainage is non-negotiable — herbs in a pot with no drainage hole are far more likely to develop root rot from water sitting at the bottom. Use a well-draining indoor potting mix rather than garden soil, which compacts too densely for a container and drains poorly indoors. Check the ingredient panel for a peat or coir base with perlite in it and no topsoil. Don't pick a mix on a fungus gnat claim, though — gnats lay in damp surfaces, so letting the top inch dry between waterings does more than any bag will.

Watering: Less Often Than You Think

Overwatering is the second most common herb-killer after insufficient light. Most herbs prefer the top inch of soil to dry out between waterings rather than staying constantly moist. Stick a finger into the soil before watering — if it's still damp an inch down, wait another day. That test is free and, for a kitchen herb pot, honestly enough. If you'd rather have an instrument, buy a capacitive soil moisture meter and skip the battery-free dial probes: they read conductivity rather than water, so once you start feeding, they drift toward "wet" regardless.

Starting From Seed vs. Buying Small Plants

Seeds are cheaper and give you more variety, but they add weeks of waiting and a step where a lot can go wrong (damping off, uneven germination, seedlings that get leggy immediately if light is weak). For a first indoor herb garden, buying small starter plants from a nursery or grocery store and transplanting them into a slightly bigger pot is the faster, more forgiving path. Once you're comfortable with watering and light, move on to starting basil or parsley from seed over a heat mat with a tray and vented dome, which keeps young seedlings warm and humid while they establish roots — the gentle bump in soil temperature noticeably speeds up germination for slow starters like parsley.

Pruning Keeps Herbs Productive

Regular light harvesting is good for most herbs — cutting the top inch or two of a basil stem, for example, encourages it to branch out and produce more leaves rather than growing one tall, single stalk. A small pair of pruning snips makes clean cuts that heal faster than tearing leaves off by hand. Spring-action handles and precision tips are what to look for if you're pruning several small pots regularly: noticeably less hand fatigue than a full-size pair of shears.

Feeding: Herbs Are Light Eaters

Herbs in fresh potting mix don't need feeding for the first couple of months — the mix carries them. After that, a general-purpose liquid fertilizer at half the label strength every two to four weeks is plenty. Resist the urge to feed harder for bigger harvests: overfed herbs grow lush, watery leaves with noticeably less flavor, because the aromatic oils get diluted into all that fast growth. Slightly hungry basil tastes better than pampered basil. This is one hobby where laziness is rewarded.

Keeping Herbs Alive Through Winter

Every indoor herb garden hits a wall in November. Day length drops, the sun sits lower, radiators dry the air to 30% humidity, and windowsills — the classic herb spot — become the coldest, draftiest real estate in the house. Basil in particular sulks below about 55°F and a cold pane of glass at night can be well below that. If your herbs live on a windowsill, either move them a foot back from the glass in winter or accept the seasonal slowdown. A grow light on a 14-hour timer erases the day-length problem entirely, which is why winter is when most people finally buy one.

When You Have More Than You Can Eat

A healthy herb garden eventually overproduces — that's the good problem. Two directions to take the surplus: dry it (see harvesting and drying herbs for the full method — oregano and thyme dry brilliantly, basil is better frozen) or turn cuttings into new plants for friends, which takes a glass of water and a windowsill (the propagation guide covers it). Regular harvesting isn't just allowed, it's what keeps the plants compact and productive.

A Realistic First Setup

If you want the simplest possible starting point: two or three 4-6 inch pots with drainage holes, a well-draining potting mix, small basil, mint, and chive starter plants, and either your brightest window or a basic grow light if that window isn't delivering enough light. That's a complete, functional indoor herb garden — everything beyond that (hydroponic herb systems, larger setups, seed starting) is an upgrade you can add once the basics are working. All-in cost if you buy everything new, light included: around $60-80. Skip the light and it's under $30.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which herbs are easiest to grow indoors for beginners?

Mint, chives, and thyme are the most forgiving — mint is nearly impossible to kill, and chives and thyme tolerate lower light and inconsistent watering better than basil does.

Why does my indoor basil keep getting leggy?

Legginess (long, thin stems reaching for light) is almost always a light problem, not a watering problem. Iowa State Extension puts most culinary herbs at about eight hours of direct light a day indoors — some tolerate as little as six — and a "bright" windowsill often delivers well under that.

How often should I water indoor herbs?

Less often than most people assume. Let the top inch of soil dry out between waterings — overwatering is the second most common way indoor herbs die, right behind insufficient light.

Do I need a grow light for an indoor herb garden?

Not always, but if your windowsill isn't bright enough (common in winter or north-facing rooms), a small dedicated grow light removes the guesswork and is often the single highest-impact upgrade you can make.

Should I start herbs from seed or buy small plants?

For a first indoor herb garden, buying small starter plants is faster and more forgiving. Seeds are cheaper and offer more variety, but add weeks of waiting and more chances for something to go wrong.
