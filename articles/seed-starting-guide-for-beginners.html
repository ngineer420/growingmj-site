Seed Starting Guide for Beginners

A step-by-step seed starting guide covering timing, depth, humidity, and light so your seedlings germinate strong instead of leggy and weak.

Seeds that never come up, and seedlings that come up thin and pale and then fall over, are usually the same story told at different speeds: the tray was the wrong temperature, or it was too dark once things emerged. Neither costs anything to fix, and both are easy to get wrong because most guides give one number where the sources give a range per crop.

Start With the Right Medium — Not Potting Soil

Minnesota's description of a seed starting mix is the shopping list: peat and vermiculite, no true soil, "sterile, lightweight and free from weed seeds, with a texture and porosity especially suited to germinating seeds and tiny seedlings."1 Buy something sold as a starting mix rather than a potting mix, and look for that fine texture with no visible bark. A seed starting mix is doing one job: staying open enough for a first root to push through while holding water without going swampy.

Sowing Depth and Spacing

Two extension rules of thumb, and they don't quite agree, which is useful because it tells you how much precision is actually needed. Minnesota: plant a seed twice as deep as its width, with a check anyone can apply — deep enough that one more seed could sit directly on top of it.1 NC State: cover to about two to four times the minimum diameter of the seed.2 Anywhere in that span works. Sowing an eighth of an inch deep when the packet said a quarter is not what killed your tray.

The seeds that need care are the very small ones, and the reason is not always the one people give. NC State handles them two different ways for two different reasons. Some are simply too fine to cover — "extremely fine seeds such as carrot, petunia, and snapdragon should not be covered, but simply dusted on the surface of the germinating medium and watered with a fine mist." Separately, a short list of species genuinely requires light to germinate: ageratum, begonia, browallia, impatiens, lettuce and petunia, which are sown on the surface for that reason.2 This page used to say "fine seeds like lettuce or basil need light." Lettuce does. Basil does not appear anywhere in NC State's propagation chapter, and the usual instruction for it is to bury it shallowly — so we have stopped implying otherwise.

There Is No Single Germination Temperature

This page used to say most vegetable and herb seeds germinate best around 70-80 °F. There is no such band, and treating one as universal is how people cook their lettuce and chill their peppers on the same shelf. NC State will give you a single number if you insist — "for most plants, 65 to 75 °F is best" — but it prints that immediately after showing how far one crop's minimum, optimum and maximum sit apart.2 Harrington's soil-temperature data, published by Washington State Extension, splits the two groups cleanly. Read down the minimum column and the split does the arguing for you: the warm-season crops will not start below 60 °F at all, while spinach and lettuce germinate at 35 °F and top out around where peppers are only getting comfortable.

Bottom heat, and who should not get it. A heat mat under the tray is the right call for peppers, tomatoes, cucumbers, squash, melons and beans — the crops whose optimum sits between 80 and 95 °F. It is the wrong call for lettuce and spinach. Lettuce is the textbook case of thermoinhibition: a 2024 review puts the optimal germination temperature for most lettuce varieties at 15 to 22 °C (59-72 °F) and is explicit that this is inhibition, not dormancy — "when environmental conditions return to normal, the seeds are able to germinate rapidly again."4 The seed does not die, it refuses. So a warm shelf does not make lettuce faster; past a point it stops it outright. Run the mat under the warm-season tray only and leave the greens on the cool end.

Two things to know before you buy a mat. It is sold on a claim about ambient temperature, not about your soil: Hydrofarm rates its windowsill mat at "10-20 °F over ambient temperature," which is a manufacturer's specification for its own product.5 And the mix is colder than the room to begin with — Minnesota measures potting mix in indoor containers running up to 5 °F below indoor air temperature.1 Put a probe in the mix and aim at the numbers in the table above rather than reading the room thermostat. Don't wire the mat to the light timer either; the seed wants warmth around the clock and the light is a separate schedule.

Humidity, and the Problem the Dome Creates

Germinating seeds want a surface that never dries out, and a heat mat, tray and humidity dome kit bundles the three things that get you there. Check that the dome vents and the tray has no drainage holes, so you can bottom-water.

Then be honest about what the dome is also doing. Damping off — the fungal collapse of a seedling at the soil line — is caused by Rhizoctonia, Fusarium and the water mould Pythium, and Minnesota is clear about the conditions it likes: "The damping off pathogens thrive in cool wet conditions. And any condition that slows plant growth will increase damping off. Low light, overwatering, high salts from over fertilizing and cool soil temperatures are all associated with increased damping off."6 A sealed dome over a cold windowsill tray is most of that list at once.

UMass gives the two controls that matter, and one of them is the one people skip: "Avoid overcrowding plants and use fans to improve airflow and dry out soil more quickly." It also notes that a lighter mix with less peat or compost supports fewer fungus gnats, "as they are attracted to moist soil where fungi thrive."7 So: vent the dome as soon as the first sprouts show, take it off entirely within a few days of most seeds emerging, and put any small fan in the room. It doesn't need to point at the tray. For the trays themselves, Minnesota's sanitation step is a 30-minute soak in 10% household bleach for anything reused, and new mix every time.6

Watering Without Disturbing Tiny Seeds

Pouring water onto a tray of just-planted seeds moves them before they've rooted. Bottom watering — setting the tray in a shallow pool and letting the mix draw up what it wants — leaves the surface undisturbed. Use warm water: Minnesota specifies 68-77 °F for young seedlings and notes that cool water around 50 °F slows growth and increases the opportunity for infection.6 Once seedlings have a couple of true leaves, top watering is safe again.

Light, and the Second Half of the Legginess Fix

Minnesota puts it flatly: "Lack of light is the major cause of elongated, skinny stems," and a windowsill in winter is rarely enough on its own. Give seedlings 12 to 16 hours a day, and not more — "don't leave the lights on continuously, as many plants need some dark period each night to develop properly."1

Distance is where the two sources diverge, and it is worth knowing why. Minnesota says keep lights no more than four inches above the tops of the seedlings, with two inches ideal; NC State positions plants six inches below the light source.12 Both figures are written around fluorescent tubes, which are far dimmer than a modern LED panel, so treat them as a floor rather than a target: raise the fixture until new growth stops stretching and stops bleaching. A seedling light stand with adjustable height rungs makes that a two-second job rather than something you rig with a stack of books.

The half of the legginess fix almost nobody mentions is temperature after emergence. NC State: once the seeds have germinated, move the flats somewhere well lit and run them at 65 to 70 °F during the day and 55 to 60 °F at night, which "prevents soft, leggy growth and minimizes disease problems."2 That is also the cleanest answer to the heat-mat question: the mat comes off when the seedlings are up, because the conditions that germinate a seed are not the conditions that build a stocky one.

Old Seed: Test It

Half of slow-germination mysteries are just old seed. Stored cool and dry — Illinois says below 45 °F — packets last for surprisingly different lengths of time.8

3 yearsAsparagus, bean, broccoli, carrot, celery, Chinese cabbage, kohlrabi, pea, spinach
4 yearsBeet, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, Swiss chard, fennel, kale, mustard, pumpkin, rutabaga, squash, tomato, turnip, watermelon
5 yearsCollards, cucumber, endive, muskmelon, radish
6 yearsLettuce

Every figure in this table is from one source — Illinois Extension's "Seed Viability and Germination" sheet — because the published tables genuinely disagree.8 Iowa State, equally current, puts spinach at 5 years against Illinois' 3 and broccoli at 5 against 3. Mixing them produces a table that is nobody's. The version this page used to print had spinach at 1-2 years and parsley at 2-3; parsley is the shortest lived thing on the list at one year, and spinach is not close to the bottom.

Before writing off a packet, run the paper towel test, and use the published decision rule rather than a remembered one. Illinois: ten seeds folded into a damp paper towel in a bag somewhere warm, checked after the number of days the packet gives for germination. All ten sprout, sow as normal; seven to nine, increase the number you plant; six or fewer, buy new seed.8 The version on this page before had gaps at two, five and six — counts it simply didn't cover.

Iowa State runs the same test bigger and stricter, and it is worth knowing which one you are following: at least 20 seeds, 50 better and 100 best, held at 70-80 °F, with 90% or better counted as very good and 50% or lower not worth using.9 Ten seeds is a coarse instrument — one seed is ten percentage points — so if the packet is expensive or the crop is slow, test more of them.

What the First Three Weeks Should Look Like

Seedlings look alarming even when they are fine, so it helps to know what normal is. The first stretch is nothing visible, and how long that lasts is a temperature question rather than a calendar one. Harrington's emergence data for tomato: 6 days at 77 °F, 8 days at 68 °F, 14 days at 59 °F, and 43 days at 50 °F.3 A cold tray is not a failed tray; it is the same tray running at a quarter speed. Don't dig around to check.

Then the surface humps and cracks, and a bent stem unfolds two smooth oval leaves. Those are cotyledons, they look nothing like the adult plant, and they panic everyone exactly once. The first true leaves appear between them a week or so later, and that is the moment the dome comes off, the light gets serious, and the day/night temperatures above start doing their work. Cotyledons yellowing and dropping around week three or four is the plant retiring its booster rocket, not a deficiency.

Hardening Off Takes Longer Than You Think

If any of your seedlings are headed outdoors, this step is longer than the seven-to-ten days this page used to give. Both sources say at least two weeks. NC State: start at least two weeks before planting out, increase the exposure gradually, "do not put tender seedlings outdoors on windy days or when temperatures are below 45 °F," and note that "even cold-hardy plants will be injured if exposed to freezing temperatures before they are hardened."2

Minnesota's version is the same fortnight described as a routine: a few hours in the shade during the warmth of the afternoon, sheltered from wind, back inside before the temperature drops, a little longer and a little more sun each day.1 Seedlings staying in an indoor setup permanently skip this entirely.

Transplanting Up

Two or three sets of true leaves and roots showing at the drainage holes, and they're ready for a larger container of ordinary potting mix. Handle seedlings by their leaves, never their stems — a damaged leaf grows back, a crushed stem usually doesn't.

Get the temperature, the moisture and the light right and most common vegetable and herb seed does the rest unsupervised. And seed is not the only route — for a plant you already own and like, a cutting is faster and copies it exactly (see propagating plants from cuttings).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why aren't my seeds germinating?

Check temperature first, because there is no single right one. Harrington's soil-temperature data has peppers, cucumbers and beans refusing to start below 60 °F, while lettuce and spinach germinate at 35 °F and give up where the warm-season crops are only getting going. After temperature: sown too deep, a surface that dried out between waterings, or regular potting soil in place of a seed starting mix.

Should I water seed trays from the top or bottom?

Bottom water whenever possible. Pouring water directly on a tray of just-planted seeds easily displaces them before they've rooted; setting the tray in a shallow pool lets the mix absorb moisture without disturbing the surface.

Why are my seedlings tall, thin, and falling over?

Light, almost always — Minnesota calls lack of light the major cause of elongated, skinny stems. Temperature is the second half of the fix: NC State runs seedlings at 65 to 70 °F during the day and 55 to 60 °F at night after they emerge, specifically to prevent soft, leggy growth.

Do seedlings need light 24 hours a day?

No. Minnesota gives 12 to 16 hours a day and says not to leave the lights on continuously, because many plants need some dark period each night to develop properly.

When are seedlings ready to transplant into a bigger pot?

Once they have two or three sets of true leaves and roots are visible at the drainage holes. Handle them by their leaves, never their stems, since a damaged leaf grows back but a crushed stem usually doesn't.
