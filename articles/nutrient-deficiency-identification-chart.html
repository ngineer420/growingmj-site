Nutrient Deficiency Identification Chart

Plant Health & Troubleshooting

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Yellowing leaves get blamed on "not enough light" or "too much water" more often than anything else, but a huge share of discolored, spotted, or curling leaves are actually a nutrient problem — either a deficiency or, just as often, an excess. The single most useful trick for diagnosing which nutrient is involved is to look at where on the plant the symptom shows up first, not just what it looks like.

Mobile vs. Immobile Nutrients: The Key Diagnostic Rule

Some nutrients can be moved by the plant from older leaves to new growth when supply is short; others can't. That single fact explains most of what you're looking at:

  • Mobile nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) — when these run short, the plant pulls them from older, lower leaves to feed new growth. Symptoms show up on the bottom/older leaves first.
  • Immobile nutrients (calcium, iron, sulfur, manganese, zinc) — the plant can't relocate these once they're used, so deficiencies show up on the newest growth first, at the top of the plant.

Before you reach for any chart, check that one detail — top of the plant or bottom of the plant — and you've already cut the list of likely suspects in half.

Don't Rule Out Excess

Nutrient burn — too much fertilizer rather than too little — can mimic a deficiency. Classic signs are dark, crispy brown leaf tips and edges on otherwise dark green, healthy-looking leaves, usually appearing suddenly after a feeding rather than developing gradually. If you've been feeding on a regular schedule and see sudden tip burn, cut your fertilizer strength in half before assuming you need to add more.

Two Tools That Make This Much Easier

Guessing which nutrient is off is a lot harder than measuring it. If you're feeding regularly and still seeing symptoms, two inexpensive tools take most of the guesswork out:

  • A pH meter — pH that's too high or too low "locks out" certain nutrients even when they're present in the soil or solution, so the plant shows deficiency symptoms despite adequate feeding. Most houseplants and vegetables want a soil pH in the 6.0-7.0 range; hydroponic setups typically run slightly lower, around 5.5-6.5. Look for one that holds its calibration for weeks (the Apera Instruments PH20 pH meter is a good example), since a meter you have to recalibrate constantly is a meter people stop using.
  • A PPM/EC meter — confirms whether your nutrient solution is actually as strong (or weak) as you think it is, rather than relying on the measuring cup and hoping (the Apera Instruments EC20 meter is a solid pick here).

The pH Lockout Problem, With Numbers

This deserves more than the one-line warning it usually gets, because it's the most common way growers end up chasing a deficiency that isn't one. Each nutrient is only chemically available to roots within a pH window, and the windows don't all overlap:

NutrientGets locked out when...
Iron, manganese, zincpH climbs above ~6.5-7 (very common in hard-water areas)
PhosphoruspH drifts below ~5.5 or above ~7
Calcium, magnesiumpH drops below ~5.5-6
Nitrogen, potassiumFairly tolerant — available across most of the growing range

Notice the pattern: iron deficiency symptoms in a plant watered with alkaline tap water usually mean the pH is high, not that the soil lacks iron. The nutrients are sitting there; they are just chemically unavailable. So the meter comes before the supplement.

But "iron won't work above pH 7.5" is only half the story, and the missing half matters if alkaline water is your situation. Colorado State's GardenNotes #223 is specific about it: in soils above pH 7.5, only chelates formulated for high pH are effective — EDDHA and EDDHMA. The cheap chelates that fill garden-centre shelves, EDTA and DTPA, "lose effectiveness quickly as the pH rises above 7.2 to 7.5." Ordinary ferrous sulphate applied to the soil does little for the same reason: above pH 7, applied iron is rapidly fixed into insoluble forms roots cannot take up. So if a soil test puts you above 7.5, the answer is Fe-EDDHA (or a foliar iron spray for a within-season fix), not a bigger dose of the same product that isn't working.

Lowering the pH is the other route, and it is slower than it sounds. CSU notes that where the soil contains free lime — check by moistening a spoonful with vinegar and watching for fizzing — lowering the pH is "impractical to impossible." A reservoir is the opposite case: adjust the solution and the problem is gone the same day.

A Worked Example

Here's the diagnostic chain on a real case — my container tomato, midsummer, in coco coir. Symptom: older leaves going yellow between the veins while the veins stayed green, working from the bottom of the plant upward. Bottom-first means a mobile nutrient, which rules out iron despite the identical interveinal pattern (iron shows on new growth — top of the plant). Mobile plus interveinal yellowing points at magnesium, and cal-mag is a routine addition in coco anyway. One dose of cal-mag supplement in the feed, and the next two weeks of new growth came in clean. Total diagnosis time: five minutes of looking at where before what. The same symptom on the top of the plant would have sent me down a completely different path — that's how much the location rule carries.

Fixing It Once You Know

For most general deficiencies, a complete fertilizer with all the micronutrients as well as the core NPK — one that skips the urea some cheaper fertilizers use, which is gentler on sensitive plants (the Dyna-Gro Foliage-Pro 9-3-6 fits this description) — applied at half the label strength, then repeated at the normal interval, corrects the problem within a week or two without the risk of overcorrecting. Calcium and magnesium deficiencies are the exception — they're common in coco coir and hydroponic setups specifically, though not for the reason usually given. Coir is not binding them: Handreck's 1993 measurements put coir's cation exchange capacity at 21–30 mmol(+)/L against 78 for a peat, which he described as a low ability to retain cations. The real reason is what coir arrives with — high in potassium and sodium, very low in calcium — which is why Purdue's substrate guide (HO-255-W) advises that coir-based substrates require more calcium, sulfur, copper and iron, and less potassium. A dedicated cal-mag supplement added to your regular feeding schedule solves this far more reliably than a generic all-purpose fertilizer (the General Hydroponics CALiMAGic is a popular choice) — it's become close to a default recommendation in the hydroponic community specifically for preventing the tip burn and blossom end rot that cal-mag shortages cause.

Give it time before changing course again: nutrient corrections aren't instant. New growth typically looks normal within a week or two of correcting the problem, but leaves that are already yellowed or spotted usually don't recover their color — the plant's next round of growth is where you'll actually see the fix.

When It's Not a Nutrient Problem At All

Before treating for a deficiency, rule out the two things most often mistaken for one: overwatering (which causes yellowing by suffocating roots, not by starving the plant of nutrients) and insufficient light (which causes pale, weak, stretched growth rather than the specific patterns above). If your watering and light are already dialed in and the symptom pattern matches the chart, a nutrient issue is the most likely explanation.

Two other imposters worth a glance before you reach for a bottle: spider mite stippling reads as "pale, sick leaves" from three feet away but resolves into hundreds of pinprick dots up close (check the undersides — our pest guide covers the follow-up), and plain old age — a plant's lowest one or two leaves yellowing and dropping occasionally, with everything above them healthy, is normal turnover, not a nitrogen crisis. A deficiency is a progressing pattern, not a single sacrificed leaf.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if yellow leaves are a nutrient problem or overwatering?

Check the pattern and location first. Overwatering tends to cause overall soft, yellow, wilting leaves regardless of position; true nutrient deficiencies follow a specific top-of-plant or bottom-of-plant pattern depending on which nutrient is short.

Why do some deficiencies show up on old leaves and others on new leaves?

Mobile nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) can be pulled from older leaves to feed new growth, so symptoms appear on lower leaves first. Immobile nutrients (calcium, iron, sulfur) can't be relocated, so symptoms hit new growth first.

Can too much fertilizer cause the same symptoms as a deficiency?

Yes — nutrient burn from overfeeding often shows up as sudden, dark, crispy leaf tips on otherwise healthy-looking leaves. If symptoms appear right after a feeding, cut fertilizer strength in half before adding more.

How long does it take for a plant to recover from a nutrient deficiency?

New growth typically looks normal within a week or two of correcting the problem, but leaves that are already yellowed or spotted usually won't regain their color — recovery shows up in the next round of growth.

Why are calcium and magnesium deficiencies so common in coco coir and hydroponics?

Not for the reason usually given. Coir is not binding them — Handreck's 1993 measurements put coir's cation exchange capacity at 21–30 mmol(+)/L against 78 for a peat, which is a low ability to retain cations, not a high one. The real reason is compositional: coir arrives high in potassium and sodium and very low in calcium, so Purdue's HO-255-W advises that coir-based substrates require more calcium, sulfur, copper and iron and less potassium. So yes, add cal-mag in coco — but because there was little calcium there to begin with, not because the coir is holding it hostage.
