Nutrient Deficiency Identification Chart

A practical chart for reading yellowing, spotting, and curling leaves so you can identify and fix common nutrient deficiencies in indoor plants.

Plant Health & Troubleshooting Updated August 20, 2026 9 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

How can I tell if yellow leaves are a nutrient problem or overwatering?

Check the pattern and location first. Overwatering tends to cause overall soft, yellow, wilting leaves regardless of position; true nutrient deficiencies follow a specific top-of-plant or bottom-of-plant pattern depending on which nutrient is short.

Yellowing leaves get blamed on "not enough light" or "too much water" more often than anything else, but a huge share of discolored, spotted, or curling leaves are actually a nutrient problem — either a deficiency or, just as often, an excess. The single most useful trick for diagnosing which nutrient is involved is to look at where on the plant the symptom shows up first, not just what it looks like.

Would rather be asked than scan a table? The deficiency diagnosis tool walks the same reasoning as a few questions and returns a shortlist with the pH check gated first — including what it has ruled out and why.

Mobile vs. Immobile Nutrients: The Key Diagnostic Rule

Some nutrients can be moved by the plant from older leaves to new growth when supply is short; others can't. That single fact explains most of what you're looking at:

  • Mobile nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) — when these run short, the plant pulls them from older, lower leaves to feed new growth. Symptoms show up on the bottom/older leaves first.
  • Immobile nutrients (calcium, iron, sulfur, manganese, zinc) — the plant can't relocate these once they're used, so deficiencies show up on the newest growth first, at the top of the plant.

Before you reach for any chart, check that one detail — top of the plant or bottom of the plant — and you've already cut the list of likely suspects in half.

Sulfur is the one that catches people out, and it is worth knowing before you look at anything else. A sulfur-short plant goes uniformly pale all over, which on any single leaf is indistinguishable from nitrogen. The tell is position and nothing else: nitrogen starts in the lowest leaves and works up, while sulfur is worst toward the top.1

Symptom descriptions from e-GRO's nutrient disorder primer, which is written for greenhouse crops1, cross-checked against Cockson and colleagues, who induced and photographed each disorder in this crop.2 Where the two differ, the second one wins here — it is the only source we have that is not extrapolating from another plant.

Rows two and four are deliberately the same sentence. Interveinal chlorosis is one symptom, not two, and the only thing that separates iron and manganese from magnesium here is which leaves it landed on — magnesium is mobile, so the plant strips it out of old leaves first; iron and manganese are immobile, so they show on new growth. Whipker's group at NC State puts it in exactly those terms in an e-GRO alert: “lower leaf interveinal chlorosis is the most commonly observed initial magnesium deficiency symptom. An upper foliage interveinal chlorosis situation would initially point to an iron deficiency.”3 The nutrient magnesium is most often confused with is not iron but potassium, which also goes for the older leaves — the tell there is that potassium takes the margins while magnesium takes the tissue between the veins.

Separating iron from manganese is harder, and if you only ever learn one thing about it, learn that fixing the pH usually fixes both. Where the two do come apart in this crop, manganese is a bright yellow netted chlorosis that starts at the midrib and spreads outward, on upper and middle foliage, later picking up small tan flecks between the veins; iron is a generally lighter leaf, concentrated around the growing tip and the newest leaves.2 That is a distinction drawn under single-nutrient starvation with a control plant standing next to it. You have neither.

The zinc row was rewritten in August 2026. It previously read “small, thin, pale new leaves”, which was wrong twice over: “thin” is not supported by any source we could find, and “pale” described iron's symptom rather than zinc's. Zinc goes for the leaf margin on new growth — “marginal yellowing on the newer foliage and expanding leaves… concentrated mostly in the margin of the leaf and along the toothed portions of the leaflets”2 — alongside the stunting and short internodes that give the shoot tip a rosetted look.1

The purple stem problem

The phosphorus row used to say “dark purple or reddish stems and leaf undersides”, which is what almost every chart on the internet says, and it is the single least reliable line in the whole genre. Purple is an anthocyanin, and anthocyanins accumulate in response to light quality, temperature, carbohydrate status, root rot, overwatering and drought as readily as they do to a phosphorus shortage.4 Indoors, under blue-heavy LEDs and over a cold or saturated root zone, you have collected several of those causes at once.

The stronger finding runs the other way. When Whipker and Veazie tried to induce phosphorus deficiency at warm growing temperatures, across more than forty species, they mostly could not produce the purpling at all — the lower leaves went pale yellow with an olive-green spotting pattern instead.5 Cockson's group, starving the actual crop this site's readers grow, described the same thing: olive-green irregular spots on the lower leaves, deepening into sunken, almost wet-looking patches with marginal necrosis. No purple in the description.2 So the chart now leads on the spotting and treats purpling as the cold-weather variant it appears to be — and if your plant is purple and warm, look at the root zone before you look at the feed.

Don't Rule Out Excess

Nutrient burn — too much fertilizer rather than too little — can mimic a deficiency. Classic signs are dark, crispy brown leaf tips and edges on otherwise dark green, healthy-looking leaves, usually appearing suddenly after a feeding rather than developing gradually.

The useful thing here is that UGA publishes a feedback rule rather than a dose, which means you can steer without a meter. Start at about one-quarter of the label rate, applied monthly. If the plant overall goes lighter green, feed every two weeks instead. If new growth is dark green but the leaves are small and the internodes longer than on the older growth, reduce the rate.6 That last symptom is the one worth memorising, because it is what overfeeding looks like before the tips go crispy.

Two Tools That Make This Much Easier

Guessing which nutrient is off is a lot harder than measuring it. If you're feeding regularly and still seeing symptoms, two inexpensive tools take most of the guesswork out:

  • A pH meter — pH that's too high or too low "locks out" certain nutrients even when they're present in the soil or solution, so the plant shows deficiency symptoms despite adequate feeding. There is no single target, and merging them is the mistake this chart used to make: garden soil 6.0-6.5 for most vegetables7, soilless container mix 5.4-6.28, and for a recirculating solution, whatever the crop asks for — Oklahoma State's per-crop table runs from 5.0-5.5 for cucumber to 6.0-7.0 for lettuce, with tomato at 6.0-6.5 and basil at 5.5-6.0.9 Buy a meter with a replaceable probe and two-point calibration, and keep solution on hand — a meter you have to fight to recalibrate is a meter people stop using.
  • A PPM/EC meter — confirms whether your nutrient solution is actually as strong (or as weak) as you think it is, rather than relying on the measuring cup and hoping. Automatic temperature compensation is worth having, and so is knowing whether the meter you bought reports raw EC or a converted PPM number, and on which conversion factor.

The pH Lockout Problem, With Numbers

This deserves more than the one-line warning it usually gets, because it's the most common way growers end up chasing a deficiency that isn't one. Each nutrient is only chemically available to roots within a pH window, and the windows don't all overlap:

Reading on a soilless mixWhat is actually happening
Above about 6.4Iron goes unavailable first, and boron next. Copper, manganese and zinc can follow but rarely do. Phosphorus availability falls off across the same climb.
5.4 to 6.2Everything is available. This is the band to aim for.
Below 5.5The opposite problem, and the one nobody warns you about: boron, copper, iron, manganese and zinc all become more available. Iron and manganese are the two that commonly reach toxic levels.
Below 5.0Whatever symptom you are looking at, this is probably the cause of it.

e-GRO Alert 9(8)1 and Purdue HO-237-W8, with the phosphorus and micronutrient trend measured across a pH gradient by Altland and Buamscha.10

Two things follow from that table. The first is that iron chlorosis in a plant watered with alkaline tap water usually means the pH is high, not that the substrate lacks iron — the nutrient is sitting right there, chemically out of reach. The meter comes before the supplement.

The second is that this table describes a soilless mix and does not transfer to garden soil. The offset is not a rounding error: the ideal range for organic and soilless substrates has been documented as roughly one to one and a half pH units below what is considered ideal for mineral soils, a finding that goes back to Lucas and Davis in 1961 and has been reproduced since.10 A chart that gives you one lockout range for both — as this page used to — is off by more than the width of the band it is describing.

But "iron won't work above pH 7.5" is only half the story, and the missing half matters if alkaline water is your situation. Colorado State's GardenNotes #223 is specific about it: in soils above pH 7.5, only chelates formulated for high pH are effective — EDDHA and EDDHMA. The cheap chelates that fill garden-centre shelves, EDTA and DTPA, "lose effectiveness quickly as the pH rises above 7.2 to 7.5."11 So if a soil test puts you above 7.5, the answer is Fe-EDDHA, or a foliar iron spray for a within-season fix, not a bigger dose of the same product that isn't working.

Lowering the pH is the other route, and it is slower than it sounds. CSU notes that where the soil contains free lime — check by moistening a spoonful with vinegar and watching for fizzing — lowering the pH is "impractical to impossible."11 A reservoir is the opposite case: adjust the solution and the problem is gone the same day. That gap, between a substrate you have to negotiate with and a solution you can simply change, is most of why hydroponic growers get away with chasing pH and soil growers do not.

A Worked Example

Here's the diagnostic chain on a real case — my container tomato, midsummer, in coco coir. Symptom: older leaves going yellow between the veins while the veins stayed green, working from the bottom of the plant upward. Bottom-first means a mobile nutrient, which rules out iron despite the identical interveinal pattern (iron shows on new growth — top of the plant). Mobile plus interveinal yellowing points at magnesium, and cal-mag is a routine addition in coco anyway. One dose of cal-mag supplement in the feed, and the next two weeks of new growth came in clean. Total diagnosis time: five minutes of looking at where before what. The same symptom on the top of the plant would have sent me down a completely different path — that's how much the location rule carries.

Fixing It Once You Know

For most general deficiencies, a complete fertilizer — all the micronutrients as well as the core NPK — at UGA's quarter-strength starting point, then adjusted by what the next flush of growth looks like, corrects the problem without the risk of overcorrecting.6 Read the guaranteed analysis rather than the front of the bottle, and check the nitrogen sources while you are back there, since urea-heavy formulas are harder on sensitive plants.

Calcium and magnesium are the exception, and they come up in coco coir and hydroponics specifically — though not for the reason usually given. The vendor explanation is that coir binds calcium and magnesium and holds them away from the plant. We looked for a measurement of that and could not find one we could reach: the cation exchange figures this page previously quoted trace to a 1993 paper that is paywalled, and no accessible source reproduces them. What is well documented is only the direction — coir's exchange capacity is lower than peat's, not higher12 — which is the opposite of a substrate that hoards cations.

The explanation that does survive checking is compositional. Coir arrives high in potassium and sodium and low in calcium, which is why Purdue's substrate guide states plainly that coir-based substrates "require more Ca, sulfur (S), Cu, and Fe; they also require less K."13 So yes, add cal-mag in coco — because there was little calcium in there to begin with, not because the coir is holding it hostage. Look for one that states both calcium and magnesium as a percentage and gives a dose per gallon.

Judge the fix by the next flush, not by the calendar: we have no sourced figure for how long recovery takes, and the honest answer is that it depends on how fast the plant is growing. What does hold is the shape of it: leaves that are already yellowed or spotted will not regain their colour, so the only place a correction is visible is in growth that has not happened yet. Watch the new leaves, and give the plant enough time to make some before you change course again.

When It's Not a Nutrient Problem At All

Before treating for a deficiency, rule out the two things most often mistaken for one: overwatering (which causes yellowing by suffocating roots, not by starving the plant of nutrients) and insufficient light (which causes pale, weak, stretched growth rather than the specific patterns above). If your watering and light are already dialed in and the symptom pattern matches the chart, a nutrient issue is the most likely explanation.

Two other imposters worth a glance before you reach for a bottle: spider mite stippling reads as "pale, sick leaves" from three feet away but resolves into hundreds of pinprick dots up close (check the undersides — our pest guide covers the follow-up), and plain old age — a plant's lowest one or two leaves yellowing and dropping occasionally, with everything above them healthy, is normal turnover, not a nitrogen crisis. A deficiency is a progressing pattern, not a single sacrificed leaf.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if yellow leaves are a nutrient problem or overwatering?

Check the pattern and location first. Overwatering tends to cause overall soft, yellow, wilting leaves regardless of position; true nutrient deficiencies follow a specific top-of-plant or bottom-of-plant pattern depending on which nutrient is short.

Why do some deficiencies show up on old leaves and others on new leaves?

Mobile nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) can be pulled from older leaves to feed new growth, so symptoms appear on lower leaves first. Immobile nutrients (calcium, iron, sulfur, manganese, zinc) can't be relocated, so symptoms hit new growth first. This is why the same interveinal yellowing means magnesium at the bottom of the plant and iron or manganese at the top.

Can too much fertilizer cause the same symptoms as a deficiency?

Yes — nutrient burn from overfeeding often shows up as sudden, dark, crispy leaf tips on otherwise healthy-looking leaves. Watch for the earlier warning too: dark green new growth with small leaves and stretched internodes means the rate is too high. UGA's starting point for indoor plants is about one-quarter of the label rate, monthly, adjusted up or down by what the next growth looks like.

How long does it take for a plant to recover from a nutrient deficiency?

Judge it by the next flush of growth rather than by a date. We could not source a recovery time, and it depends on how fast the plant is growing anyway. What does hold is that leaves already yellowed or spotted will not regain their colour, so the correction is only ever visible in growth that has not happened yet.

Why are calcium and magnesium deficiencies so common in coco coir and hydroponics?

Not for the reason usually given. The vendor story is that coir binds calcium and magnesium, and we could not find a reachable measurement supporting it — what is documented is that coir's cation exchange capacity is lower than peat's, which is the opposite of a substrate that hoards cations. The real reason is compositional: coir arrives high in potassium and sodium and low in calcium, so Purdue's HO-255-W states that coir-based substrates require more calcium, sulfur, copper and iron and less potassium. So yes, add cal-mag in coco — but because there was little calcium there to begin with, not because the coir is holding it hostage.
