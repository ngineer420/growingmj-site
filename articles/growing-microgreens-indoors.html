Growing Microgreens Indoors

Indoor Vegetable Gardening

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Photo by photofarmer, licensed under CC BY 2.0

Microgreens are the indoor crop with the best effort-to-payoff ratio, and it isn't close. Seed to harvest in under two weeks. No transplanting, no fertilizing, no pest pressure worth mentioning, no grow tent. A tray of radish microgreens costs maybe a dollar in seed and produces the same volume of greens that sells for four dollars at the store.

They're also the crop where I tell people to skip the beginner kit entirely. The kits are fine; they're just three trays and a bag of soil marked up 300%. Here's the whole system.

What Microgreens Actually Are

Microgreens are ordinary vegetable seedlings — radish, broccoli, peas, sunflower — sown absurdly densely and harvested at the first-leaf stage. Height varies too much between varieties to define them by, which is why the harvest cue further down this page is a leaf stage rather than an inch mark. You're eating the seedling: stem, seed leaves, and the first hints of true leaves. Because harvest happens so early, the plant runs almost entirely on energy stored in the seed, which is why microgreens need no fertilizer and barely any soil depth.

Don't confuse them with sprouts, which are germinated in jars of water and eaten roots and all. Sprouts have a real food-safety reputation problem because they grow warm and wet. Microgreens grow in a medium, in air, under light, and you only eat what's above the soil line.

The Equipment List Is Short

  • Two 1020 trays (the standard 10x20 inch nursery footprint) — one with drainage holes for growing, one solid tray underneath for bottom watering. Thin dollar-store trays crack in a month; heavier-gauge 1020 trays bought as a matched pair last years. Half-size 10x10 trays are plenty for a household.
  • An inch of seed starting mix. Regular potting soil works, but a seed starting mix — fine-milled, with no visible bark in it — makes for cleaner harvesting. Coco coir also works well here.
  • Seed in bulk, and buy seed sold for eating. This is the one place microgreens differ from normal gardening: seeding rates run from 10 g of arugula to 150 g of peas per 1020 tray, so packet seed is uneconomical. The bigger reason is safety, not cost. Garden-centre vegetable seed is routinely sold pre-treated with fungicides — thiram and captan are the common ones — and a treated-seed label carries the instruction "do not use treated seed for food, feed, or oil purposes." A microgreen is eaten whole, seed hull and all, a week or so after sowing, so treated seed is disqualified outright rather than merely expensive. Buy seed sold specifically for microgreens or sprouting, by the ounce or pound, and check any packet you already own for a treatment statement or a bright coloured seed coating before you sow it.
  • Any decent light. A south windowsill, an LED shop light, or a spare corner of the grow tent all work.

Sowing: Dense on Purpose

Fill the holed tray with about an inch of mix, press it flat with another tray or a piece of cardboard, and mist it thoroughly. Then sow far thicker than instinct allows — seeds nearly touching, a solid carpet. Weigh it rather than eyeballing it; the rate is variety-specific and the spread is much wider than it looks. Utah State Extension publishes per-variety rates for a full 1020 tray: 10 g arugula, 28 g broccoli, kale, basil, cilantro or rutabaga, 42 g radish, 48 g sunflower, 150 g peas. Presoak only the two big seeds, and not overnight by default — USU gives 6 hours for peas and 12 hours for sunflower. Press the seed into contact with the medium. Most varieties don't need covering with mix at all.

The Blackout Period

Here's the step that separates leggy, patchy trays from thick ones. It is not one length for everything: USU's covered times run from 1-2 days for arugula, kale, radish and rutabaga through 2-4 for broccoli, 3 for peas and sunflower, 4-7 for basil, up to 7-14 for cilantro. Use the figure for what you sowed.

The procedure has two halves, and skipping the second is the usual mistake. Cover the seeded tray with a second 1020 tray resting directly on the medium and put something flat and heavy on it — 2 to 5 pounds, on every variety, not just the big seeds. That keeps the seed pressed into contact so the roots are forced down into the medium instead of pushing the seed up. Lift it off for the second half of the covered time — the cue is roots visibly pushing into the medium — and invert the top tray so it goes on as a cover that doesn't touch the seedlings. They stretch in the dark under that.

When you uncover for good they're pale yellow and look terrible. Under light they green up completely within 24 hours — watching a yellow tray turn green in an afternoon is honestly one of the more satisfying things in indoor growing.

Water From Below, Always

Overhead watering on a dense carpet of seedlings is how you get matted greens and damping off (the fungal collapse described in our seed starting guide — microgreens are its favorite victim). After germination, water by lifting the holed tray, pouring a cup or two of water into the solid tray, and setting the holed tray back in it. The soil wicks up what it needs. Once a day is typical; the tray feels noticeably light when it's due.

Airflow matters for the same reason. A small fan in the room — it doesn't need to point at the trays — cuts mold problems to nearly zero.

Harvest and What to Grow

Harvest by leaf stage, not by height: USU's cue is as soon as the first true leaves appear, or sooner, once the seed leaves are open. Height varies with variety and with how long the tray sat in the dark, so it's a poor trigger. Grab a handful gently, cut just above the soil with scissors or a sharp knife, and cut only what you'll eat in the next few days — a living tray keeps far better than a bag of cut greens. Cut greens keep about a week refrigerated in a container with a paper towel. Wash at eating time, not storage time.

Most varieties are one-and-done; compost the root mat and resow. A tray sown every week gives a continuous supply, and spent mats break down fast in a compost bin or even a corner of an outdoor bed.

Root hairs are not mold. Every new microgreens grower has the same panic on day three: fine white fuzz all over the seeds. If it's evenly distributed, clustered around roots, and vanishes when you water — root hairs, completely normal. Mold is grey-blue, patchy, sits on the soil surface rather than the roots, and smells musty. Root hairs are a sign of a healthy tray; mold is a sign to add airflow and water less.

Cost Reality Check

Startup: two good trays (~$15), a bag of starting mix (~$10), a seed sampler (~$20). Call it $45, less if you already garden. Per tray after that: about $1-2 of seed and a few cents of mix. If your household eats greens regularly, the setup pays for itself in the first month, which is a claim almost nothing else in this hobby can make.

Start with radish. It's the fastest, it's the most vigorous, and a first crop that works in eight days buys a lot of patience for everything else you'll grow.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do microgreens take from seed to harvest?

It depends on the variety and the range is wider than most guides admit. Utah State Extension gives 6-8 days for arugula, 8-12 for radish, kale, rutabaga and broccoli, 8-10 for peas, 9-10 for sunflower, 12-16 for basil and 21-28 for cilantro. The covered blackout period is part of that and is also variety-specific, from 1-2 days up to 7-14 for cilantro.

Do microgreens need a grow light?

A bright windowsill works, but even a cheap LED shop light gives thicker stems and darker leaves. Microgreens are harvested so young that light quality matters less than for any other indoor crop.

Why are my microgreens falling over or rotting at the base?

Usually damping off from overhead watering and poor airflow. Water from below by lifting the growing tray and pouring water into the solid tray underneath, and run a small fan nearby.

Do microgreens regrow after cutting?

Mostly no — you harvest the whole seedling. Pea shoots are the main exception and will usually give a second, smaller cutting if you cut above the lowest leaf.

Is the white fuzz on my microgreen seeds mold?

Usually not — fine white fuzz clustered around the seed and root is root hairs, which disappear after watering. Mold is bluish or grey, sits on top of the medium in patches, and smells musty.
