Growing Microgreens Indoors

A start-to-finish microgreens setup: trays, seeding density, the blackout period, watering, harvest timing, and the varieties that actually earn their shelf space.

Indoor Vegetable Gardening Updated August 20, 2026 7 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

How long do microgreens take from seed to harvest?

It depends on the variety and the range is wider than most guides admit. Utah State Extension gives 6-8 days for arugula, 8-12 for radish, kale, rutabaga and broccoli, 8-10 for peas, 9-10 for sunflower, 12-16 for basil and 21-28 for cilantro. The covered blackout period is part of that and is also variety-specific, from 1-2 days up to 7-14 for cilantro.

Microgreens ask less of you than anything else you can grow indoors. No transplanting, no feeding, no grow tent, and most varieties go from sowing to cut in eight to twelve days — a tray of radish on 88 cents of seed, at the prices Utah State published in January 2026.1

What they do ask for is air moving over a wet, crowded tray. Sowing a solid carpet of seedlings into damp medium is, from a fungus's point of view, an excellent idea, and damping off is the thing that takes a tray down overnight. Most of what follows — the weight, the bottom watering, the fan — is about that one risk. Skip the beginner kits, too. They are three trays and a bag of soil in a printed box, and you can buy the parts better.

What Microgreens Actually Are

Microgreens are ordinary vegetable seedlings — radish, broccoli, peas, sunflower — sown absurdly densely and harvested at the first-leaf stage. Height varies too much between varieties to define them by, which is why the harvest cue further down this page is a leaf stage rather than an inch mark. You're eating the seedling: stem, seed leaves, and the first hints of true leaves. Because harvest happens so early, the plant runs almost entirely on energy stored in the seed, which is why microgreens need no fertilizer and barely any soil depth.

Don't confuse them with sprouts, which are germinated in jars of water and eaten roots and all. The FDA treats the two as different products and says plainly that its sprout guidance does not cover microgreens: sprouts are cut while the seed leaves are still undeveloped, whereas microgreens reach the true-leaf stage, grow in a medium, and are harvested above the medium line.2 The distinction matters because sprouts are raised in warm, humid conditions that also suit Salmonella, Listeria and E. coli, so a few bacteria on a seed can multiply to dangerous numbers during sprouting even in a clean kitchen.3

What the FDA does not do is declare microgreens safe, and it is worth reading the caveat rather than the headline. In the same passage it asks for more research into microgreens "grown using conditions similar to those of sprouts (i.e., warm, moist, and nutrient-rich media)" and encourages growers of those to adopt the sprout standards voluntarily.2 There is a real recall to point at: in 2018 Greenbelt Greenhouse pulled its entire microgreens line over possible Listeria monocytogenes contamination.4 Buy seed sold for eating, don't leave the tray standing in water, and wash at eating time. Low risk, not no risk.

The Equipment List Is Short

  • Two 1020 trays (the standard 10x20 inch nursery footprint) — one with drainage holes for growing, one solid tray underneath for bottom watering. Thin dollar-store trays crack in a month; heavier-gauge 1020 trays bought as a matched pair last years. Half-size 10x10 trays are plenty for a household.
  • About two quarts of medium per 1020 tray — a little over half an inch deep. Utah State's guide treats seed-starting mix, coconut coir and a hydroponic grow mat as equally workable, so use whichever you can get.1 A fine-milled seed starting mix with no visible bark in it makes for cleaner harvesting.
  • Seed in bulk, and buy seed sold for eating. This is the one place microgreens differ from normal gardening: seeding rates run from 10 g of arugula to 150 g of peas per 1020 tray, so packet seed is uneconomical. The bigger reason is safety, not cost. LSU's seed-treatment guide states that most commercially available vegetable seed comes treated with at least one fungicide or insecticide, thiram most often and captan close behind.5 Under the Federal Seed Act that seed is labelled "do not use for food, feed, or oil purposes" — and a microgreen is eaten whole, seed hull and all, a week or so after sowing.6 That takes treated seed off the table outright rather than merely making it expensive. Buy seed sold specifically for microgreens or sprouting, by the ounce or pound, and check any packet you already own for a treatment statement or a bright coloured seed coating before you sow it.
  • Any decent light. A south windowsill, an LED shop light, or a spare corner of the grow tent all work.

Sowing: Dense on Purpose

Utah State's setup step is worth copying exactly, because it wets the medium evenly without leaving the tray standing in water. Pre-moisten the medium to the feel of a wrung-out sponge — squeeze a handful and your hand should come away wet with nothing dripping out. Fill the holed tray, put two cups of water in the solid tray, stand one inside the other, and after thirty minutes pour off whatever is left underneath.1

Then sow far thicker than instinct allows — seeds nearly touching, a solid carpet. Weigh it rather than eyeballing it; the rate is variety-specific and the spread is wider than it looks. USU publishes per-variety rates for a full 1020 tray: 10 g arugula, 28 g broccoli, kale, basil, cilantro or rutabaga, 42 g radish, 48 g sunflower, 150 g peas. Only the two big seeds get a presoak, and not overnight by default — six hours for peas, twelve for sunflower.1 Press the seed into contact with the medium. Most varieties don't need covering with mix at all.

The Blackout Period

Here's the step that separates leggy, patchy trays from thick ones. It is not one length for everything: USU's covered times run from 1-2 days for arugula, kale, radish and rutabaga through 2-4 for broccoli, 3 for peas and sunflower, 4-7 for basil, up to 7-14 for cilantro.1 Use the figure for what you sowed.

The procedure has two halves, and skipping the second is the usual mistake. Cover the seeded tray with a second 1020 tray resting directly on the medium and put something flat and heavy on it — 2 to 5 pounds, on every variety, not just the big seeds. That keeps the seed pressed into contact so the roots are forced down into the medium instead of pushing the seed up. Lift it off for the second half of the covered time — the cue is roots visibly pushing into the medium — and invert the top tray so it goes on as a cover that doesn't touch the seedlings.1 They stretch in the dark under that.

When you uncover for good they're pale yellow and look terrible. Under light they green up completely within a day — watching a yellow tray turn green over an afternoon is one of the more satisfying things in indoor growing. From there USU runs the lights 18 hours on and 6 off, at 74 °F while the seed is germinating, then 72 °F with the lights on and 68 °F with them off.1 Ordinary room temperature is inside that band, which is most of why this crop needs no equipment.

Water From Below, Always

A microgreen tray is a dense stand of seedlings in permanently damp medium, which is the textbook setup for damping off — the fungal collapse described in our seed starting guide. UMass gives the two controls for it in one breath: don't overcrowd, and use fans to improve airflow and dry the surface faster.7 Overcrowding is not optional here — the whole crop depends on it — so the airflow has to do more of the work. Any small fan in the room will do; it doesn't need to point at the trays.

Watering goes underneath for the same reason. Lift the holed tray, pour a cup or two into the solid tray, and set the holed tray back in it so the medium draws up what it wants. Then check it: if water is still standing under there half an hour later, pour it off. Judge the interval by lifting the tray rather than by the calendar — it feels noticeably light when it's due, and on a warm dry day that will be sooner than on a cool one.

Harvest and What to Grow

Harvest by leaf stage, not by height: USU's cue is as soon as the first true leaves appear, or sooner, once the seed leaves are open.1 Height varies with variety and with how long the tray sat in the dark, so it's a poor trigger. Grab a handful gently, cut just above the medium with scissors or a sharp knife, and cut only what you'll eat in the next few days — a living tray keeps far better than a bag of cut greens.

How much better depends almost entirely on temperature. In the one controlled trial we could find, mustard microgreens bagged and held at 5 °C — fridge temperature — kept good sensory quality for 14 days. At 10 °C that fell to four days, at 15 °C to two, and at room temperature they were past eating inside a day.8 That is one crop in one study, so treat the exact numbers as indicative — but the shape of it is the useful part, and it is why cut greens go straight to the coldest shelf. Wash at eating time, not storage time.

Most varieties are one-and-done; compost the root mat and resow. A tray sown every week gives a continuous supply, and spent mats break down fast in a compost bin or even a corner of an outdoor bed.

Root hairs are not mold. Every new microgreens grower has the same panic on day three: fine white fuzz all over the seeds. If it's evenly distributed, clustered around roots, and vanishes when you water — root hairs, completely normal. Mold is grey-blue, patchy, sits on the soil surface rather than the roots, and smells musty. Root hairs are a sign of a healthy tray; mold is a sign to add airflow and water less.

Cost Reality Check

Running cost is the one number here with a real citation behind it, because USU prices its own table. Buying at the five-pound rate in January 2026, a full 1020 tray came to 17 cents of arugula seed, 35 cents of rutabaga, 88 cents of radish and $1.84 of basil.1 Add a few cents of medium and that is the whole per-tray cost. Seed prices move, so read that as a ratio rather than a quote: the cheapest varieties are five to ten times cheaper than the dearest, and every one of them is well under the price of a supermarket clamshell.

Startup is trays, a bag of medium and seed, and we are not going to invent a figure for it — nobody publishes prices for hobby growing equipment, and every number this page used to give for them was made up. What is fair to say is the shape: the trays are the only thing you buy once, the seed is the only thing that recurs, and there is no light, tent, pump or fertilizer in the list at all.

Start with radish. It's the fastest, it's the most vigorous, and a first crop that works in eight days buys a lot of patience for everything else you'll grow.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do microgreens take from seed to harvest?

It depends on the variety and the range is wider than most guides admit. Utah State Extension gives 6-8 days for arugula, 8-12 for radish, kale, rutabaga and broccoli, 8-10 for peas, 9-10 for sunflower, 12-16 for basil and 21-28 for cilantro. The covered blackout period is part of that and is also variety-specific, from 1-2 days up to 7-14 for cilantro.

Do microgreens need a grow light?

A south-facing window works; Utah State's guide says artificial light simply gives you more control, and runs it 18 hours on and 6 off. Microgreens are harvested so young that no fixture on this site is the wrong one — a cheap LED shop light is plenty.

Why are my microgreens falling over or rotting at the base?

Usually damping off from overhead watering and poor airflow. Water from below by lifting the growing tray and pouring water into the solid tray underneath, and run a small fan nearby.

Do microgreens regrow after cutting?

Mostly no — you harvest the whole seedling. Pea shoots are the main exception and will usually give a second, smaller cutting if you cut above the lowest leaf.

Is the white fuzz on my microgreen seeds mold?

Usually not — fine white fuzz clustered around the seed and root is root hairs, which disappear after watering. Mold is bluish or grey, sits on top of the medium in patches, and smells musty.
