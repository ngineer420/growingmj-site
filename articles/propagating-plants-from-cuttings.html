Propagating Plants From Cuttings

Propagation

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Photo by Dinesh Valke, licensed under CC BY-SA 2.0

Propagation is the closest thing indoor gardening has to free money. One healthy basil plant can become six by the end of the month. A single pothos vine, cut into pieces, will fill a new pot in a season. You're not buying anything, you're not waiting on seeds, and the new plant is an exact copy of one you already know does well in your conditions.

It's also weirdly under-taught. Most beginner guides stop at "put it in water," which works right up until it doesn't, and then nobody tells you why your rosemary cutting turned into brown mush.

What a Cutting Actually Needs

A stem cutting is a plant running on stored energy with no root system. Everything about propagation follows from that one fact. It can't pull up water, so it needs high humidity or a wet medium to keep from wilting. It can't feed itself much, so it doesn't want fertilizer yet. And the cut end is an open wound, so it's in a race: grow roots before rot moves in.

Your job is just to tilt that race in the cutting's favor. Clean cut, warm temperature (roughly 70-75°F / 21-24°C is the sweet spot for most species), moderate light, and no standing funk.

Taking a Cutting That Wants to Root

The quality of the cutting matters more than anything you do to it afterward. What I look for:

  • A stem from healthy, actively growing tissue — firm, green, not flowering. A stem that's already pushing flowers has its hormones pointed the wrong way; pinch buds off or pick a different stem.
  • 4-6 inches long with at least two or three nodes (the bumps where leaves attach). Roots emerge from nodes, not from smooth stem.
  • Cut just below a node with clean snips, not scissors that crush the stem. Sharp micro-tip bypass pruners make a cleaner wound, and a cleaner wound is less prone to rot than anything a pinching blade leaves.
  • Strip the leaves from the bottom half. Any leaf that would sit underwater or under the medium will rot, and that rot spreads up the stem.

Big top leaves can also be cut in half, crosswise. It looks brutal but it works — less leaf area means less water lost to evaporation while there are no roots to replace it.

Water Rooting: Easiest to Watch, Easiest to Learn From

A glass of water on a bright windowsill (bright but out of direct afternoon sun) is the beginner method for a reason: you can see exactly what's happening. Basil, mint, pothos, tradescantia, coleus, and most soft-stemmed houseplants root this way with almost no effort.

Two rules keep water propagation from going wrong. Change the water every two or three days — stagnant water runs out of oxygen and starts breeding the bacteria that rot stems. And use an opaque or dark glass if you can; roots prefer darkness, and algae prefers light.

Check the plant before you put a cutting where a pet can reach it. A rooting glass usually ends up on a windowsill or a kitchen counter, which is exactly where a cat gets to it. The ASPCA toxic plant database lists golden pothos and heartleaf philodendron — two of the easiest plants on this page to root — as toxic to dogs and cats on insoluble calcium oxalates, which cause oral irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. Tradescantia and coleus are both listed as toxic too, and so is garden mint (Mentha sp., essential oils — vomiting and diarrhea on large ingestions), which surprises people. Basil, sage, rosemary and spider plant are all recorded as non-toxic to dogs and cats. Look the plant up before the glass goes on the counter, and if a pet chews one, call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 or your vet.

Yes, tomato suckers. The side shoots you pinch out of a tomato plant's leaf joints root in about a week and become full plants. If you grow indoor cherry tomatoes under a light, you never have to start tomatoes from seed more than once a year.

Rooting in a Medium: Better Roots, Less Transplant Shock

Water roots are not hairless — this page said so until August 2026 and then contradicted itself two paragraphs later, which is a good sign a claim is wrong. Roots formed in water do produce root hairs. What differs is the build: they grow in a medium with no resistance, plenty of dissolved oxygen at the surface and no particles to grip, so they come out thinner, more brittle and less branched than roots the same plant would make in a medium. Move them into soil and a good share of that fragile growth breaks or dies back while the plant builds sturdier replacements, which is why water-rooted cuttings often sulk for a week or two after potting up.

Rooting directly in a moist medium skips that adjustment. The standard options are a seed starting mix kept evenly damp, plain perlite, or rockwool starter cubes — rockwool is my pick for anything fussy because it holds the air-to-water ratio without any judgment calls on my part. Pre-soak the cubes, since rockwool runs alkaline out of the bag, and buy a sheet whose cubes are all the same density so they don't dry at different rates. Poke the stem in, keep it humid, and resist the urge to tug on it to check for roots. Tugging tears off exactly the root hairs you're waiting for.

Humidity is the catch. A cutting in a medium can't drink, so the air around it needs to stay near saturation for the first week or two. A vented humidity dome over a tray solves this, and the heat mat, tray and dome kits sold for seed starting cover the dome and the gentle bottom heat in one box. Crack the dome vents daily for a few minutes so stale air doesn't turn into mold.

Rooting Hormone: When It's Worth It

For mint and pothos, rooting hormone is a waste of money. They root if you look at them funny.

For woody or semi-woody cuttings — rosemary, lavender, thyme that's gone woody at the base, fruiting shrubs, a lot of the fancier houseplants — a rooting gel genuinely changes the odds. My rosemary strike rate went from roughly one in five to better than half when I started dipping cuttings before sticking them in the medium. Gel beats powder for coverage; look for the auxin concentration printed on the label. Dip the bottom inch, tap off the excess, and don't double-dip back into the bottle — decant a little into a shot glass so you're not contaminating the whole jar.

When to Pot Up

Move a water-rooted cutting to soil when roots are 1-2 inches long and starting to branch. It feels early. Do it anyway — a cutting with six-inch water roots transplants worse, not better, because those long roots are fully water-adapted and half of them die off in soil.

For the first week after potting, keep the soil consistently moist rather than letting it dry out the way you would for an established plant. The roots are shallow and there aren't many of them yet. After that, ease back to normal watering. A medium-rooted cutting is ready when roots show at the edges of the cube or plug — pot the whole cube, don't unwrap it.

Don't fertilize a cutting. No nutrients in the rooting water, none in the rooting medium, nothing until the cutting is potted and pushing new leaves. Fertilizer at this stage burns the very root tips you're trying to grow. The first feeding, at half strength, comes 2-3 weeks after transplant.

A Good First Project

Buy one healthy basil plant from the grocery store. Cut it into four or five cuttings, root them in a glass on the windowsill, and pot them up as they're ready. Three weeks later you'll have a row of basil that cost you the price of one plant, and you'll have made every propagation mistake there is to make on a plant that forgives all of them. Then go take cuttings of the plants you actually care about.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take a cutting to root in water?

Basil and pothos usually show roots in 5-10 days. Mint is often faster. Woody herbs like rosemary can take 4-8 weeks in water and often do better rooted directly in a moist medium.

Do I need rooting hormone to propagate cuttings?

Not for easy plants like basil, mint, or pothos — they root readily in plain water. Rooting gel earns its keep on slower, woodier cuttings like rosemary, lavender, and many shrubby houseplants, where it meaningfully improves the strike rate.

Why do my cuttings keep rotting instead of rooting?

Usually one of three things: leaves left below the waterline, water that never gets changed, or a cutting taken from soft, floppy growth instead of a firm healthy stem. Strip the lower leaves, refresh the water every 2-3 days, and cut just below a node.

Should I root cuttings in water or soil?

Water is easier to monitor and works well for soft-stemmed plants. Soil or rockwool produces roots that transplant with less shock, and suits woody cuttings better. Both work — water is more beginner-friendly, medium-rooting is more robust.

When can I transplant a water-rooted cutting into soil?

Once the roots are 1-2 inches long with some branching. Longer than that and the roots get so adapted to water that the move into soil sets the plant back noticeably.
