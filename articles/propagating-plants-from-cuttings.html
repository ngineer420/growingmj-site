Propagating Plants From Cuttings

How to root cuttings from herbs, houseplants, and vegetables in water, soil, or rockwool — what actually roots, how long it takes, and why cuttings rot.

Propagation Updated August 20, 2026 9 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

How long does it take a cutting to root in water?

Iowa State's figure for houseplant stem cuttings is three to six weeks. In water on a windowsill the soft-stemmed ones are much faster than that — basil and pothos in about a week or two in our experience, mint sooner — while woody herbs like rosemary can take a month or two and often do better rooted straight into a moist medium. Only the three-to-six-week figure has a source behind it.

Propagation is the closest thing indoor gardening has to free money. One healthy basil plant can become six by the end of the month. A single pothos vine, cut into pieces, will fill a new pot in a season. You're not buying anything, you're not waiting on seeds, and the new plant is an exact copy of one you already know does well in your conditions.

It's also weirdly under-taught. Most beginner guides stop at "put it in water," which works right up until it doesn't, and then nobody tells you why your rosemary cutting turned into brown mush.

What a Cutting Actually Needs

A stem cutting is a plant running on stored energy with no root system. Everything else follows from that one fact. It can't pull up water, so it needs high humidity or a wet medium to keep from wilting. It can't feed itself much, so the medium it sits in should be, in NC State's words, "sterile, low in fertility, well drained to provide sufficient aeration, and moisture-retentive."1 Low, note — not none. And the cut end is an open wound, so the whole thing is a race: grow roots before rot moves in.

Your job is to tilt that race. Clean cut, moderate light, no standing funk, and warmth — but warmth in two different places, which is the part most guides flatten into a single number. Iowa State puts ideal air temperature at 65-75 °F and then suggests a heat mat to bring the rooting medium to 75-80 °F.2 Roots want the warmer end; leaves losing water they cannot replace do not. This page used to give a flat 70-75 °F for everything, which is the air figure wearing the medium's job.

Taking a Cutting That Wants to Root

The quality of the cutting matters more than anything you do to it afterward. What I look for:

  • Healthy, actively growing tissue — firm, green, not flowering. A flowering stem is a stem whose sugars are already committed elsewhere, so pinch the buds off or pick a different one. (This page used to say its "hormones are pointed the wrong way." The advice survives; the explanation was made up.)
  • Three to six inches, with at least two nodes — the bumps where leaves attach, which is where new roots come from.2 NC State splits it finer by tissue: 3-5 inches for herbaceous cuttings, 4-6 for woody ones.1
  • Cut just below a node with clean snips, not scissors that crush the stem. Sharp micro-tip bypass pruners make a cleaner wound, and a cleaner wound is less prone to rot than anything a pinching blade leaves. Dip the blade in rubbing alcohol or a one-part-bleach-to-nine-parts-water mix so you are not carrying disease from one plant into the next cut.1
  • Strip the leaves off the lower third to half of the stem.1 Any leaf sitting underwater or under the medium will rot, and that rot spreads up the stem.

Big top leaves can be cut in half across the midvein, which NC State recommends for exactly the reason it looks like it shouldn't: less leaf area means less water lost while there are no roots to replace it.1

One thing worth knowing about why any of this works. The textbook story is that the growing tip floods the stem with auxin and the buds below stay shut until you remove it. Measured in garden pea, that is not the order things happen in: buds were released up to 24 hours before auxin in the neighbouring stem changed at all, and the initial trigger turned out to be sugar. The shoot tip's demand for it starves the buds below; cut the tip off and the competition ends.3 Auxin still decides which of the released buds go on to become branches. Nothing you do with the snips changes. The sentence you tell yourself while doing it should.

Rooting in a Glass of Water

A glass of water on a bright windowsill (bright but out of direct afternoon sun) is the beginner method for a reason: you can see exactly what's happening. Basil, mint, pothos, tradescantia, coleus, and most soft-stemmed houseplants root this way with almost no effort.

Change the water often enough that it never goes stagnant, and never let the level drop far enough to leave new roots in air. How often is "often" is the one thing extension will not agree on: Iowa State says one to two times a week, Virginia Tech says weekly, others say every few days, and this page used to say every two or three with nobody behind it.24 Pick the interval you will actually keep. What everyone does agree on is the failure: water left alone goes oxygen-poor and, in Virginia Tech's phrase, "inhibitory to rooting." Use an opaque or dark glass if you have one, because roots prefer darkness and algae prefers light.

Check the plant before you put a cutting where a pet can reach it. A rooting glass usually ends up on a windowsill or a kitchen counter, which is exactly where a cat gets to it. The ASPCA toxic plant database lists golden pothos and heartleaf philodendron — two of the easiest plants on this page to root — as toxic to dogs, cats and horses on insoluble calcium oxalates, which cause oral irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. Tradescantia is listed as toxic as well, on dermatitis rather than oxalates. So is garden mint (Mentha sp., essential oils — vomiting and diarrhea on large ingestions), which surprises people. Basil, sage, rosemary and spider plant are all recorded as non-toxic.5 Ornamental coleus is the one to be careful looking up: the ASPCA entry filed under "coleus" is Coleus ampoinicus, Cuban oregano, a different plant, and there is no entry for the bedding coleus most people mean. Absence of a record is not a clearance. Check before the glass goes on the counter, and if a pet chews one, call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center on (888) 426-4435 or your vet.

Iowa State's figure for houseplant stem cuttings generally is three to six weeks to root.2 Nobody publishes a per-species table for a windowsill glass, so the one below is mine rather than anyone's data:

Yes, tomato suckers. The side shoots you pinch out of a tomato plant's leaf joints root in about a week and become full plants. If you grow indoor cherry tomatoes under a light, you never have to start tomatoes from seed more than once a year.

Rooting Straight Into a Medium

Water roots are not hairless — this page said so until August 2026 and then contradicted itself two paragraphs later, which is usually how you find out a claim was never checked. What the extension sources actually say is that the roots come out different, and they are careful not to say much more than that. Iowa State: roots formed in water "are more coarse in texture," which "means they are not well adapted to potting soil and once potted, may show signs of stress, such as wilting, leaf drop, browning of the leaves, or tip die-back."2 Virginia Tech puts it as "greater transplant shock with a greater incidence of death," and gives no number.4 Neither will tell you what fraction dies, so neither will we; this page used to say half, which was invented.

Rooting directly in a moist medium skips that adjustment. The standard options are a seed starting mix kept evenly damp, plain perlite — which Iowa State likes for cuttings precisely because it "holds a good balance of water and air" — or rockwool starter cubes.2 Pre-soak rockwool, since it runs alkaline out of the bag, and buy a sheet whose cubes are the same density so they don't dry at different rates.

This page used to call rockwool the fussproof option, on the grounds that it holds the air-to-water ratio with no judgment calls required. Its own manufacturer disagrees. Grodan's grower guide asks you to "allow excess water to drain away naturally," specifies "no standing water in the trays," warns that "overly wet conditions in the root zone can slow root initiation and facilitate the establishment of pathogens," and then puts a floor under it too: do not let the block dry back below about 25-30% water content.6 That is a window with a wall at each end, and a propagation tray sitting in its own runoff under a sealed dome is the classic way to fall out of the wet side of it. Rockwool is still the pick for a fussy cutting. It just wants a decision from you, not none.

Poke the stem in, keep it humid, and resist the urge to tug on it to check for roots. Tugging tears off exactly the root hairs you're waiting for.

Humidity is the catch. A cutting in a medium can't drink, so the air around it has to stay near saturation for the first week or two. A vented dome over a tray does it, and the heat mat, tray and dome kits sold for seed starting cover the dome and the bottom heat in one box — the heat mat being how you get that 75-80 °F medium under 65-75 °F air. Iowa State's instruction on the dome is one most people ignore: do not seal it tightly, because the cuttings still need air exchange.2

Rooting Hormone: When It's Worth It

For mint and pothos, rooting hormone is a waste of money. They root if you look at them funny.

Where a rooting gel earns its money is woody and semi-woody cuttings — rosemary, lavender, thyme gone woody at the base, fruiting shrubs, a lot of the fancier houseplants. That is the case NC State makes for it too, framing hormone treatment as a woody-cutting practice and "particularly for difficult-to-root species."1 The active ingredients are synthetic auxins, IBA or NAA, and the concentration you want is on the label rather than in a rule of thumb.2 Dip the bottom inch, tap off the excess, and decant into a shot glass rather than dipping into the bottle: NC State's instruction is that leftover product gets thrown away, never returned to the original container.1 For what it is worth from one windowsill, the rosemary cuttings I dip strike far more often than the ones I don't. That is an impression, not a trial.

When to Pot Up

Move a water-rooted cutting to soil when the roots are about an inch long.2 It feels early. Do it anyway. The longer a root system spends in water the more thoroughly it is built for water, and the transplant is what it has to survive.

For the first week after potting, keep the soil consistently moist rather than letting it dry out the way you would for an established plant. The roots are shallow and there aren't many of them yet. After that, ease back to normal watering. A medium-rooted cutting is ready when roots show at the edges of the cube or plug — pot the whole cube, don't unwrap it.

"Never fertilize a cutting" is close, but it is not what the sources say. NC State's requirement of the rooting medium is that it be "low in fertility" — low, not sterile of nutrients.1 Commercial propagators go further and feed under the mist from the day cuttings are stuck, but they hold it to 50-100 ppm nitrogen specifically to avoid salt burn, and that is a mist bench with constant leaching rather than a glass on a windowsill.7 At home the practical version is unchanged: nothing in the rooting water, nothing added to the rooting medium, and the first feed once the cutting is potted and pushing new leaves. The reason is just narrower than "fertilizer burns roots."

Troubleshooting

What you seeWhat went wrong
Stem turns brown/black and soft from the bottomRot — water went stale, a leaf was submerged, or the cutting was taken from weak growth. Start over with a fresh cutting and clean water.
Cutting wilts within hoursToo much leaf area or air too dry. Remove more lower leaves, halve the big ones, add a dome or bag.
Weeks pass, no roots, but the cutting looks fineToo cold or too dark, or simply not slow enough yet — three to six weeks is normal. Get the air to 65-75°F and the medium to 75-80°F, move it somewhere brighter, and wait. Woody cuttings are slower again.
Roots formed, then transplant killed itWater roots left too long, or soil allowed to dry out in week one. Transplant at about an inch of root and baby it briefly.
Mold on the medium under a domeDome never vented. Open the vents daily; a few minutes of fresh air is enough.

Practice on Supermarket Basil

Buy one healthy basil plant from the grocery store. Cut it into four or five cuttings, root them in a glass on the windowsill, and pot them up as they're ready. A few weeks later you have a row of basil that cost the price of one plant, and you have made every propagation mistake there is to make on a plant that forgives all of them. Then go take cuttings of the plants you actually care about.

With one thing checked first. A plant patent runs 20 years from filing and gives its holder the right to exclude others from "asexually reproducing the plant, and from using, offering for sale, or selling the plant so reproduced, or any of its parts" — and the USPTO lists rooting cuttings first among the methods that count as asexual reproduction.8 Taking the cutting is the excluded act. There is no carve-out for doing it at home, for keeping the result, or for giving it away. Most of what is on a windowsill is far out of patent or was never in it; the ones to look at twice are the recently branded ornamentals, which usually carry a tag saying "propagation prohibited" and mean it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take a cutting to root in water?

Iowa State's figure for houseplant stem cuttings is three to six weeks. In water on a windowsill the soft-stemmed ones are much faster than that — basil and pothos in about a week or two in our experience, mint sooner — while woody herbs like rosemary can take a month or two and often do better rooted straight into a moist medium. Only the three-to-six-week figure has a source behind it.

Do I need rooting hormone to propagate cuttings?

Not for easy plants like basil, mint, or pothos — they root readily in plain water. Rooting gel earns its keep on slower, woodier cuttings like rosemary, lavender, and many shrubby houseplants, where it meaningfully improves the strike rate.

Why do my cuttings keep rotting instead of rooting?

Usually one of three things: leaves left below the waterline, water that never gets changed, or a cutting taken from soft, floppy growth instead of a firm healthy stem. Strip the lower leaves, refresh the water at least weekly, and cut just below a node.

Should I root cuttings in water or soil?

Water is easier to monitor and works well for soft-stemmed plants. Soil or rockwool produces roots that transplant with less shock, and suits woody cuttings better. Both work — water is more beginner-friendly, medium-rooting is more robust.

When can I transplant a water-rooted cutting into soil?

Iowa State says transplant at about an inch of root. Longer than that and the root system is more thoroughly built for water, which is the thing it then has to stop being.
