Understanding Grow Light Schedules

How many hours of light indoor plants actually need — photoperiod basics, schedules for herbs, greens, and fruiting vegetables, and why plants need real darkness.

Lighting Updated August 20, 2026 7 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

How many hours a day should my grow light run?

It depends on the crop. Minnesota Extension gives 12-14 hours for hydroponic lettuce and herbs and for foliage houseplants, 14-16 for flowering houseplants, and 16-18 for seedlings. Fruiting vegetables like tomatoes and peppers want 14-16 hours. Spinach is the exception and should stay nearer 12, because it is a long-day plant that bolts. Nothing on that list needs 24 hours.

Buying the right light gets all the attention. How long you run it gets almost none, which is backwards — a mediocre light on a good schedule beats a great light on a bad one. And schedule mistakes are sneaky, because the plant doesn't complain today; it just quietly underperforms for a month while you blame the nutrients.

The good news: for most of what home growers grow, there are about three schedules that matter, and a $12 timer runs all of them.

The Numbers That Actually Matter

A light schedule controls two separate things, and untangling them clears up most of the confusion:

  • Total light delivered per day. Photosynthesis is roughly cumulative — a dimmer light run longer can deliver the same daily total as a stronger light run shorter. This is what drives growth speed. Growers measure it as DLI (daily light integral), but you don't need the math; you need the idea — and if you do want the number, the DLI calculator turns your PPFD and photoperiod into it, with target ranges per crop.
  • Day length as a signal. Separately from energy, some plants read the length of the night like a calendar and use it to decide when to flower.

Most schedule advice is really about the first one. The second only bites for a handful of species, covered below.

If you want one number to set a mixed shelf to and stop thinking about it: 14 hours suits almost everything on that table. The one thing that does resent it is spinach, which is a long-day plant and will bolt — keep spinach on its own timer nearer 12, or accept that you are growing it for a short window.

One honest caveat on the lettuce row, because the sources genuinely disagree. Twelve to fourteen hours is the home-scale extension figure. Controlled-environment research runs far longer at the same daily light integral — Jeong and colleagues grew lettuce at a constant 13 mol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ under 24 h at low intensity against 12 h at high intensity and found the long, dim treatment expanded more leaf area and captured more photons, most strongly at warm temperatures. So a long day is not itself the problem for lettuce. Which means the number that actually matters is the DLI, not the hours — our DLI calculator converts between them.

Darkness Is Not Wasted Time

The tempting beginner logic goes: light makes growth, so 24 hours of light makes maximum growth. It doesn't work, for two reasons. Plants run real processes in the dark — moving sugars out of leaves, respiration, in some species the actual cell expansion that reads as "growth." Deny them the dark period and several common crops do measurably worse; tomatoes are the famous case, developing mottled, damaged leaves under continuous light.

The second reason is your electric bill. Going from 16 to 24 hours is 50% more electricity for, at best, marginal gains. A 200-watt LED at 16 hours a day costs roughly $17.11 a month at 17.8¢/kWh — the US residential average for the Rolling 12 months ending May 2026, from EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.3 (0.2 kW × 16 h × 30 days × $0.1782). At 24 hours it's about $25.66. Over a year the "free" extra light costs about $103 for nothing. Your own rate is the one that matters. State averages are nothing like uniform: in May 2026 the residential average ran from 12.35¢ in Idaho to 52.00¢ in Hawaii (EIA Table 5.6.A).

Photoperiod Species: When Day Length Is a Calendar

A minority of plants use night length as their flowering trigger. Short-day plants flower when nights get long — that's why poinsettias and Christmas cactus bloom around Christmas, and why commercial growers black them out in autumn. Long-day plants (spinach is the one indoor growers meet) flower when days stretch, which is why spinach under 16-hour lights bolts to seed just when it should be leafing out. Keep spinach nearer 12 hours.

Almost everything else a home grower touches — tomatoes, peppers, basil, lettuce (mostly), beans — is day-neutral and flowers when it's mature, whatever the timer says. So if a day-neutral plant won't flower, the schedule isn't the suspect; light intensity, pot size, or age usually is.

Consistency Beats Everything

Plants track light with real internal clocks, and they respond poorly to a schedule that wanders. A light that runs 14 hours starting at 6 a.m. on weekdays and 10 a.m. on weekends is a worse schedule than a consistent 12 hours. This is why a timer isn't optional equipment. A 7-day digital outlet timer is the standard cheap answer, and battery backup is the feature to insist on — a power blip that silently shifts your schedule three hours is exactly the failure you won't notice for weeks. A wifi smart plug does the same job with logs and remote control if you'd rather have that; either way, check the wattage rating covers your fixture.

Light leaks during the dark period count. If something in your setup cares about darkness — or you just want plants to actually rest — the dark hours should be dark. A grow tent's zipped interior handles this; a shelf in the living room with a lamp on until midnight doesn't quite. It matters most for the photoperiod species above and for getting seedlings to grow stocky rather than confused.

Two Schedule Tricks Worth Stealing

Run the lights overnight in summer. The plant doesn't care whether its "day" starts at 8 p.m., but your tent does — lights-on during the coolest hours flattens the heat spike, and if you're on a time-of-use electricity plan, overnight rates can cut the running cost by a third.

And when a plant moves from your seedling shelf to the big light, keep the hours the same even though the intensity jumps. Change one variable at a time; a transplant that's also had its day length shuffled is a transplant you can't troubleshoot.

Reading the Plant's Feedback

Whatever schedule you set, the plants grade it within a couple of weeks, and the signals are readable. Long gaps between leaf pairs and a plant leaning hard toward the fixture mean it wants more light — more hours, a closer light, or a stronger one. Bleached, washed-out patches on the leaves nearest the light, or leaves cupping upward like taco shells, mean it's getting hammered — raise the fixture or dim it before you touch the timer, since intensity is usually the culprit there rather than hours. And leaf edges curling with crispy tips right under the lamp is often plain heat: put your hand at canopy height for thirty seconds. If it's uncomfortable for your hand, it's uncomfortable for the leaf. Adjust one thing, give it ten days, look again.

Setting It Up Once

Pick the column from the table that matches your crop, set the timer, write the on/off times on a piece of tape on the fixture, and stop adjusting it. The growers who get in trouble with schedules are almost never the ones who picked 14 instead of 16 — they're the ones who kept fiddling. Light schedule is the one part of this hobby where the right move is to make a decent choice and then leave it alone for the whole grow.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours a day should my grow light run?

It depends on the crop. Minnesota Extension gives 12-14 hours for hydroponic lettuce and herbs and for foliage houseplants, 14-16 for flowering houseplants, and 16-18 for seedlings. Fruiting vegetables like tomatoes and peppers want 14-16 hours. Spinach is the exception and should stay nearer 12, because it is a long-day plant that bolts. Nothing on that list needs 24 hours.

Can I leave a grow light on 24 hours a day?

You can, but you shouldn't. Most plants grow better with a real dark period — several important processes run at night, and continuous light stresses many species (tomatoes actually develop leaf damage under it). You also pay 50% more in electricity for little or no gain — 24 hours is one and a half times 16.

Does it matter what time of day the light runs?

Not to the plant — it only cares about consistency and total hours. Running lights overnight can save money on time-of-use electricity plans and helps keep tents cool in summer; just keep the same window every day.

Why is my plant flowering (or refusing to flower) indoors?

Some species use day length as their flowering trigger. Long nights push short-day plants (poinsettias, Christmas cactus) to bloom, while most common vegetables and herbs are day-neutral and flower based on age and size instead.

Do I need a timer for my grow light?

Yes — consistency is the whole game, and no one flips a switch at the same time every day for months. A basic digital outlet timer with battery backup costs about the same as a takeout lunch.
