Understanding Grow Light Schedules

How many hours of light indoor plants actually need — photoperiod basics, schedules for herbs, greens, and fruiting vegetables, and why plants need real darkness.

Lighting Updated August 20, 2026 7 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

How many hours a day should my grow light run?

It depends on the crop. Minnesota Extension gives 12-14 hours for hydroponic lettuce and herbs and for foliage houseplants, 14-16 for flowering houseplants, and 16-18 for seedlings. Fruiting vegetables like tomatoes and peppers want 14-16 hours. Spinach is the exception and should stay nearer 12, because it is a long-day plant that bolts. Nothing on that list needs 24 hours.

Almost every question about grow lights is about the fixture. Almost none of them are about the timer, which is odd, because the timer is the cheaper half of the same equation and the one you can change tonight.

Schedule mistakes are also quiet. The plant does not wilt or spot; it just grows slightly worse than it should for a month while you re-read the nutrient label. The good news is that for most of what home growers grow there are about three schedules worth knowing, and one timer runs all of them.

Two Things a Schedule Controls

A photoperiod does two unrelated jobs, and separating them clears up most of the confusion in this subject.

  • It sets the total light delivered per day. Photosynthesis is roughly cumulative, so a dimmer light run longer can deliver the same daily total as a stronger light run shorter. This is what drives growth rate, and it is what most schedule advice is really about.
  • It acts as a signal. Quite separately from energy, some species read the length of the night like a calendar and use it to decide when to flower. This only bites for a handful of plants, and they are named further down.

The Sum That Turns Hours Into Light

The unit that puts hours and brightness on the same axis is the daily light integral, DLI: the total number of photons landing on a square metre over a day. Virginia Tech's introductory guide gives the conversion, and it is one multiplication — PPFD × 3,600 × hours ÷ 1,000,000, or more conveniently PPFD × hours × 0.0036. A fixture reading 200 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ at the canopy, run 16 hours, delivers a DLI of 11.5 mol·m⁻²·d⁻¹. The same guide is blunt about the units you should not use for plants — candela, lumen, foot-candles, lux, watts and joules are all measures of something other than what a leaf eats.1

Targets, from the same publication, so you can see what the hours are for:

Read those against the sum and the whole hours-versus-brightness argument dissolves. Basil at 15 mol needs 260 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ for 16 hours, or 350 for 12. Either works. Our DLI calculator does the arithmetic both ways if you would rather not.

Hours from University of Minnesota Extension, “Lighting for indoor plants and starting seeds” — seedlings 16-18, hydroponic lettuce and herbs 12-14, foliage houseplants 12-14, flowering houseplants 14-16.2 The spinach row is from e-GRO's alert on bolting in hydroponic leafy greens.3

If you want one number to set a mixed shelf to and then stop thinking about it, 14 hours suits almost everything on that table. Spinach is the one that resents it. It is an obligate long-day plant with a critical day length of 13-14 hours depending on cultivar, which means a 14-hour shelf is sitting exactly on the trigger. Put spinach on its own timer nearer 12, or accept that you are growing it for a short window.

Worth knowing even so: day length is not the main reason greens bolt indoors. The same e-GRO alert is explicit that the most common cause of premature bolting in both lettuce and spinach is high temperature — typically multiple days above 80 °F, with the number of hot days mattering more than how hot they got — and that with moderate temperatures, spinach does not bolt even under long days.3 If your lettuce keeps running to seed, look at the thermometer before the timer.

One honest caveat on the lettuce row, because the sources genuinely disagree. Twelve to fourteen hours is the home-scale extension figure. Controlled-environment research runs far longer at the same daily light integral — Jeong and colleagues grew lettuce at a constant 13 mol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ under 24 h at 150 µmol against 12 h at 300, and the long, dim treatment expanded more leaf area and captured more photons — an effect that got stronger the warmer the room was.4 So a long day is not itself lettuce's problem. What that leaves is the conclusion the whole page is built on: the number that matters is the DLI, and the hours are just one of two ways to reach it.

Darkness Is Not Wasted Time

The tempting beginner logic goes: light makes growth, so 24 hours of light makes maximum growth. It does not work, for two reasons.

The first is that some species are injured by it, and tomato is the textbook case — it is the species researchers reach for when they need a photoperiodic-injury-sensitive plant. In one recent study, three weeks under continuous light left tomato leaves visibly unhealthy and chlorotic, damaged badly enough that the researchers dropped the treatment from part of their analysis. The nuance worth carrying: this is genotype-dependent rather than universal. Tolerant tomato lines exist, and even those are brought up to 24 hours over about seven weeks rather than switched, while cucumber shrugs the whole thing off.5 If you grow tomatoes indoors, 16 hours is the standard the research itself runs as its control, and there is no reason to push past it.

The second reason is the bill. Going from 16 hours to 24 is 50% more electricity — 24 divided by 16 is one and a half — for gains that range from marginal to negative. A 200-watt LED at 16 hours a day is 96 kWh a month, which at 17.8¢/kWh comes to $17.11. At 24 hours it is 144 kWh, or $25.66. Over a year the extra light costs about $103. That rate is the US residential average across the rolling 12 months ending May 2026, and it is worth checking against your own bill rather than trusting it: in May 2026 the state figures ran from 12.35¢ in Idaho to 52.00¢ in Hawaii.6

Photoperiod Species: When Day Length Is a Calendar

A minority of plants use night length as their flowering trigger. Short-day plants flower when nights get long — that's why poinsettias and Christmas cactus bloom around Christmas, and why commercial growers black them out in autumn. Long-day plants (spinach is the one indoor growers meet) flower when days stretch, which is why spinach under 16-hour lights bolts to seed just when it should be leafing out. Keep spinach nearer 12 hours.

Almost everything else a home grower touches — tomatoes, peppers, basil, lettuce (mostly), beans — is day-neutral and flowers when it's mature, whatever the timer says. So if a day-neutral plant won't flower, the schedule isn't the suspect; light intensity, pot size, or age usually is.

Why the Timer Is Not Optional

Plants keep real internal clocks, and a photoperiod that wanders by hours from day to day is a photoperiod they cannot entrain to. Nobody has published a controlled comparison of a sloppy 14-hour schedule against a strict 12-hour one, so we will not pretend to know which is worse. What is not in doubt is that nobody flips a switch at the same minute every day for four months, which makes the timer the cheapest piece of equipment on this site to justify. A 7-day digital outlet timer is the standard cheap answer, and battery backup is the feature to insist on — a power blip that silently shifts your schedule three hours is exactly the failure you won't notice for weeks. A wifi smart plug does the same job with logs and remote control if you'd rather have that; either way, check the wattage rating covers your fixture.

Light leaks during the dark period count. If something in your setup cares about darkness — or you just want plants to actually rest — the dark hours should be dark. A grow tent's zipped interior handles this; a shelf in the living room with a lamp on until midnight doesn't quite. It matters most for the photoperiod species above and for getting seedlings to grow stocky rather than confused.

Two Schedule Tricks Worth Stealing

Run the lights overnight in summer. The plant does not care whether its "day" starts at 8 p.m., but the tent does — lights-on during the coolest hours of the night flattens the heat spike your exhaust fan has to chase. If your utility bills on a time-of-use tariff, the same move shifts your largest load off peak. How much that saves depends entirely on your tariff, and we are not going to invent a percentage for it; the number is on your own rate schedule.

And when a plant moves from your seedling shelf to the big light, keep the hours the same even though the intensity jumps. Change one variable at a time; a transplant that's also had its day length shuffled is a transplant you can't troubleshoot.

Reading the Plant's Feedback

Whatever schedule you set, the plants grade it within a couple of weeks, and the signals are readable. Long gaps between leaf pairs and a plant leaning hard toward the fixture mean it wants more light — more hours, a closer light, or a stronger one. Bleached, washed-out patches on the leaves nearest the light, or leaves cupping upward like taco shells, mean it's getting hammered — raise the fixture or dim it before you touch the timer, since intensity is usually the culprit there rather than hours. And leaf edges curling with crispy tips right under the lamp is often plain heat: put your hand at canopy height for thirty seconds. If it's uncomfortable for your hand, it's uncomfortable for the leaf. Adjust one thing, give it ten days, look again.

Setting It Up Once

Pick the row that matches your crop, set the timer, write the on and off times on a piece of tape stuck to the fixture, and leave it alone. The growers who come unstuck here are almost never the ones who chose 14 hours instead of 16. They are the ones who changed it three times in a fortnight and then could not tell which change did what.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours a day should my grow light run?

It depends on the crop. Minnesota Extension gives 12-14 hours for hydroponic lettuce and herbs and for foliage houseplants, 14-16 for flowering houseplants, and 16-18 for seedlings. Fruiting vegetables like tomatoes and peppers want 14-16 hours. Spinach is the exception and should stay nearer 12, because it is a long-day plant that bolts. Nothing on that list needs 24 hours.

Can I leave a grow light on 24 hours a day?

You can, but you shouldn't. Most plants grow better with a real dark period — several important processes run at night, and continuous light stresses many species (tomatoes actually develop leaf damage under it). You also pay 50% more in electricity for little or no gain — 24 hours is one and a half times 16.

Does it matter what time of day the light runs?

Not to the plant — it only cares about consistency and total hours. Running lights overnight can save money on time-of-use electricity plans and helps keep tents cool in summer; just keep the same window every day.

Why is my plant flowering (or refusing to flower) indoors?

Some species use day length as their flowering trigger. Long nights push short-day plants (poinsettias, Christmas cactus) to bloom, while most common vegetables and herbs are day-neutral and flower based on age and size instead.

Do I need a timer for my grow light?

Yes. Nobody flips a switch at the same minute every day for four months, and a photoperiod that wanders is one the plant cannot settle into. Buy one with battery backup, so a brief power cut cannot silently shift the whole schedule.
