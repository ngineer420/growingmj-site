Grow Tent Odor Control and Ventilation, Explained

How to set up intake, exhaust, and carbon filtration in a grow tent so heat, humidity, and odor all stay under control.

Grow Tents & Setups Updated August 20, 2026 9 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

What's the correct order for airflow through a grow tent?

Pull air in low (intake), push it out high (exhaust), and filter it through a carbon filter before the fan, not after. The reason is pressure, not filtration: a fan puts everything upstream of it under suction and everything downstream under positive pressure, so a filter mounted after the fan leaves a pressurised run of unfiltered air that leaks out at every joint. The carbon works the same on either side.

A grow tent is a sealed box with a heater in it. That is not a criticism of tents — the seal is the whole point, and the heater is your light — but it does mean the air has to be given somewhere to go, and given it deliberately. Left alone, a tent with the lights on is hot and saturated within the hour, and mold, gnats and smell all follow the humidity.

The parts list is short: a fan, a carbon filter, some duct, and a hole at each end. Almost all of the difficulty is in sizing the fan, and almost all of the sizing advice online is a rule of thumb with its assumptions stripped off.

The Three Jobs Ventilation Has to Do

  • Remove heat generated by your lights and, to a lesser extent, your plants.
  • Manage humidity so it doesn't stay high enough, for long enough, to invite mold or fungus gnats.
  • Control odor, since many herbs, tomato plants, and flowering vegetables produce a noticeable smell that most people would rather not have filling the whole house.

All three are solved by the same basic setup: pull air in low, push air out high, and filter what leaves the tent.

The Airflow Path, Step by Step

Hot air rises, and that single fact drives the entire layout:

  1. Intake, near the bottom. Most tents have a mesh-covered lower port that lets cooler room air passively enter as air is pulled out the top. Some setups add a small intake fan here too, though passive intake is often enough if your exhaust fan is properly sized.
  2. Exhaust, near the top. An inline fan and carbon filter kit mounted at the top of the tent pulls hot, humid, odor-carrying air up and out through ducting. The fan does the pulling; the filter does the scrubbing. Shop on the published noise figure at the CFM you actually need rather than the fan's headline rating — two fans moving the same air can differ a lot in how loud they are about it, which matters if the tent lives anywhere near a bedroom or office.
  3. Carbon filter placement. The filter should sit inside the tent, hung from the top support bars, with air passing through the filter before it reaches the fan — not after. The reason is pressure sign, not filtration efficiency. A fan induces suction upstream of itself and positive pressure downstream, working "by inducing a negative pressure or suction in the ducts leading to the hoods and positive pressure in the system after the fan."1 Put the filter after the fan and every joint, seam and clamp between the fan and the filter is pressurised with unfiltered air and leaks it outward. Put it before the fan and that same run is under suction, so a leak pulls room air in rather than pushing tent air out. The carbon itself does not care which side it sits on — contact time is contact time — so the "it cuts filtration effectiveness" version of this advice, which this page used to give, is not the reason. The pressure one is, and it is the better argument anyway.

Sizing Your Exhaust Fan

The usual rule — "one air change every one to three minutes" — is a real number with its premise deleted. It comes from greenhouse practice, where the figure is a maximum: John Bartok's ventilation guidance sizes summer fan capacity at one volume change per minute and says plainly what that buys you, an 8 to 10 °F rise from the intake louver to the fan. Winter ventilation is sized at a quarter of that.2 So one-per-minute is not a target you aim at, it is the top of the range, and it comes with a temperature promise attached. Quote the air changes without the ΔT and you have quoted half a specification.

There are two other ways to get a number for a 4x4, and it is reassuring that they land in the same place. Purdue sizes greenhouse ventilation by floor area at about 8 cfm per square foot, which puts a 16 ft² tent at roughly 128 CFM.3 A 4x4x6.5 ft tent holds about 104 ft³, so Bartok's one-volume-per-minute comes out at about 104 CFM. Both are a long way below the 200-400 CFM this page used to recommend.

The third method is the one worth actually learning, because it is the only one that knows what light you own. Ventilation is there to move heat, so size it against heat. The standard sensible-heat relation is CFM = BTU/hr ÷ (1.08 × ΔT), where ΔT is the temperature rise you are willing to run over the room outside the tent. Essentially all the power your light draws ends up as heat inside a closed tent, and one watt is 3.412 BTU/hr, so a 480 W fixture is shedding about 1,638 BTU/hr:

  • Hold a 15 °F rise → 101 CFM
  • Hold a 10 °F rise → 152 CFM
  • Hold a 5 °F rise → 303 CFM

That one sum settles most of the arguments in this subject. Whether 150 CFM is "enough" depends entirely on how warm the room outside the tent runs, which is why two people can give opposite advice and both be right. It also explains where the 200-400 CFM folklore came from: that is roughly what it takes to hold a 5 °F rise under a big light — a real requirement in a warm room and overkill in a cool basement.

Then subtract the losses. A carbon filter costs airflow, and the manufacturer's own guidance is to allow a 10-25% drop for the resistance of pulling through the carbon.4 Duct bends and length come off on top of that, and you want headroom left to run the fan below full speed. A 4-inch fan will meet the numbers above on paper with nothing to spare; a 6-inch fan meets them at part speed, which is where you want to be for noise, for motor life, and for the week in August when the room is 8 °F warmer than it was when you sized everything.

You do not have to do that arithmetic by hand. It runs the heat-removal cross-check above at the same time, which is the sum that catches people out when a small tent has a big light in it.

Negative Pressure: The Free Diagnostic

With the exhaust working, the tent walls draw slightly inward. That is negative pressure, and it is what you want: air can only enter through the intake and only leave through the filter, so the smell has exactly one exit and it is the scrubbed one. Walls puffing outward mean air is being pushed out through every zip and seam unfiltered — usually an intake fan overpowering the exhaust, or an exhaust fan mounted to blow in rather than out, which happens more than anyone admits.

There is a second reading of the same wall, though, and it is the one that gets missed. Slightly inward is right; hard against the frame is not. Bartok's field test for greenhouses is exactly this — if the covering is being pulled tight against the frame when the fans run, or the door is difficult to open, the intake area is not adequate — and his sizing rule is that the intake opening should be at least 1.25 times the fan area.2 A tent sucked flat is a tent whose fan is fighting its own intake port, moving less air than its rating for more noise than it should make. Open the lower port fully, or open a second one. For scale, greenhouse practice aims at a pressure difference of about 0.05 inches of water across the structure — a very slight suction, not a vacuum.5

The Noise Problem, Honestly

Nobody warns you that ventilation is the loud part of indoor growing. Lights are silent; a cheap inline fan at full speed is a small vacuum cleaner, and it runs around the clock. Mine was audible two rooms away through a closed door.

The best single fix is to buy a bigger fan and run it slower, and it is worth seeing why, because this page used to claim the opposite — that an oversized fan "wastes power". It does not. The fan laws put airflow in proportion to speed, static pressure to the square of it, and shaft horsepower to the cube: a 25% increase in rpm costs 95% more horsepower.6 Run that backwards and a fan loafing at 40% of its speed draws something like 6% of the power it would at full tilt. Big and slow is the cheap option as well as the quiet one.

Three more, from the fan engineers rather than from growers. Sound output is proportional to fan static pressure, so anything that lowers resistance in the system — a straighter duct run, a bigger intake, a filter that is not clogged — makes the same fan quieter without touching it. Structure-borne noise is a separate problem with a separate fix: hang the fan on flexible connectors and isolators rather than bolting it to anything rigid, because a fan hard-coupled to a frame turns the frame into a speaker. And where there is a choice, a lower-rpm fan is the safer buy; past about 3,000 rpm the noise tends to couple into the structure, and people find high frequencies more annoying than low ones anyway.7

Insulated duct does genuinely absorb noise, and the measured numbers are better than the marketing at the top end and worse at the bottom. Insertion loss for a 10-foot run of 6-inch flexible duct against bare sheet metal is about 8 dB at 125 Hz, 35 dB at 500 Hz and 46 dB at 2 kHz.8 That is a great deal of whoosh removed and very little hum — the low rumble you hear through a wall is precisely the part duct lining does least about, which is why isolating the motor matters more than lagging the pipe. The figures are also for ten feet and do not extrapolate: twenty feet is not twice the attenuation.

One thing to get right while you are hanging all of it: pull the duct taut. Flexible duct left slack is the most expensive mistake in the run. Testing at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that the moderate compression typical of real installations raises pressure drop by a factor of four, and heavier compression by factors close to ten.9 Every bend costs something as well, but no bend costs what a lazy concertina of duct behind the tent costs.

Circulating Air Inside the Tent

Exhaust handles air leaving the tent, but you also want air moving within it. A small clip fan pointed across the canopy rather than at it strengthens stems, dries the leaf surface, and breaks up the still, humid pockets where fungal problems start. UMass gives the target as two numbers that are easy to hit and easy to overshoot: total circulating capacity of about 2 cfm per square foot of floor, moving air through the canopy at 50 to 100 feet per minute.10 For a 4x4 that is roughly 32 CFM — genuinely one small clip fan, which is the rare case where the cheap answer is also the correct one. Two of them on opposite corners beats one big one blasting a single unlucky plant.

Insulated ducting with clamps to connect your fan and filter to the tent's vent ports without air leaking out at the joints — the insulation also stops hot exhaust radiating back into the tent through the duct wall — in a diameter that matches both the fan and the ports. And a thermometer/hygrometer, so you can confirm the setup is holding a reasonable temperature and humidity range instead of guessing from how the tent "feels".

Where Unfiltered Air Gets Out

Odor and hot air take the path of least resistance, and if the tent is under proper suction there should not be one. In practice there usually is: an unclamped duct joint, a zip left an inch open, a cable pass-through with a cable in it and no cinch pulled. Clamp every duct connection, keep the flaps shut except when you are working inside, and run cables through the port that exists for them rather than propping the door. If the smell persists with all of that done, the leak is almost always downstream of the fan — the pressurised side — which is the practical reason the filter belongs before the fan rather than after it.

A Simple Troubleshooting Checklist

One number before the table, because it is the one this page got wrong by a factor of two. Carbon filters do not last "12 to 18 months" — that figure appeared here three times and we could not source it anywhere. Denver's environmental best-practice guide for cannabis facilities, which is the closest thing to a published figure for this exact application, gives most carbon filters 6 to 12 months depending on load, notes that high humidity hinders filter performance so a damp tent sits at the short end, and uses a smell at the filter outlet rather than a date as the replacement cue. A pre-filter extends the carbon's life and is itself a consumable, swapped about every six to eight months.11

Where the Exhaust Air Should Go

The last piece nobody plans: the ducted air has to end up somewhere. Best is out a window or into a different room — genuinely removing the heat and humidity from the growing space. Acceptable for a small tent in a big room is venting into the same room near the ceiling, as long as you accept that the room warms up a few degrees and the tent's intake air gets warmer with it (a feedback loop that bites in summer). What doesn't work: venting into a closet, a crawlspace, or any small sealed volume — you're just relocating a humidity problem to somewhere you can't see it, and attics and wall cavities grow mold on cheaper substrates than you do.

If you take one thing from this page, take the heat sum. Air changes per minute was never meant to travel without its temperature rise, and losing that half is why so many first tents end up with a fan that is either roaring or losing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the correct order for airflow through a grow tent?

Pull air in low (intake), push it out high (exhaust), and filter it through a carbon filter before the fan, not after. The reason is pressure, not filtration: a fan puts everything upstream of it under suction and everything downstream under positive pressure, so a filter mounted after the fan leaves a pressurised run of unfiltered air that leaks out at every joint. The carbon works the same on either side.

What size exhaust fan do I need for my tent?

Size it against heat, not volume. Take the wattage of your light, multiply by 3.412 to get BTU/hr, and divide by (1.08 × the temperature rise you can live with). A 480 W fixture needs about 101 CFM to hold a 15 °F rise and about 303 CFM to hold 5 °F. Then add 10-25% back on for the carbon filter. Extension figures for a 4x4 land near 104-128 CFM, well under the 200-400 CFM commonly quoted.

How long does a carbon filter actually last?

Denver's Cannabis Environmental Best Management Practices guide for air quality puts it at 6-12 months depending on filter load, replaced according to the manufacturer's recommendation. High humidity shortens it, since humidity hinders filter performance. Replace on the trigger rather than the calendar: the guide's own cue is a smell near the filter outlet. A pre-filter buys you time and should itself be swapped about every 6-8 months.

Do I need a separate fan for air circulation inside the tent?

Yes — your exhaust fan moves air out of the tent, but a small clip fan circulating air inside strengthens stems, discourages mold, and helps prevent the still, humid pockets where spider mites thrive.

Why does my tent still smell even with a carbon filter installed?

Check for unsealed duct joints, a cracked zipper, or an open cable pass-through first — odor takes the path of least resistance, and unfiltered leaks are the most common cause of persistent smell.
