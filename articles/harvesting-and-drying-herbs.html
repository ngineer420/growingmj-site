Harvesting and Drying Herbs at Home

When and how to harvest indoor herbs, and how to dry and store them so they keep their flavor — hang drying, rack drying, oven and dehydrator methods compared.

Sooner or later every indoor herb garden produces more than you can eat fresh. A single healthy basil plant, pinched regularly, will out-produce a household's pesto habit by midsummer. The difference between that surplus becoming a jar of genuinely good dried herbs or a jar of green dust that smells like hay comes down to a handful of decisions: when you cut, how fast you dry, and how you store.

Harvest Timing: Oils First, Volume Second

The flavor in herbs lives in volatile oils, and the advice to take your big harvest before the plant flowers is well supported. The reason usually given for it is not. This page used to say oil content "peaks just before flowering", as a general rule about herbs. It isn't one — it is species-dependent, and at least one species goes the other way: a study of aromatic Lamiaceae under cultivation found spearmint's essential oil content significantly higher in July at full bloom (5.40%) than in April (1.63%).

What the extension sources actually say is simpler and holds up. NC State: "Harvest herbs before flowering, otherwise, leaf production declines." Illinois says the same. So the timing advice survives, on the grounds that a flowering herb stops making the leaves you want and the ones it has turn bitter (basil especially; mint less so) — not on a general claim about peak oil that we could not source.

Time of day matters too. NC State's instruction is precise and needs no theory attached: "harvest early in the morning, after the dew dries, but before the heat of the day." Under a grow light, "morning" just means the first couple of hours after the light comes on. Basil is the exception worth knowing: it is chilling-sensitive and handles an afternoon cut better than most.

For the cut itself, never strip a plant bare — but "a third" is too cautious for annuals, which is what this page used to say for everything. Illinois splits it: annual herbs "can be cut back 50-75 percent and still recover", while perennials should give up about a third. NC State goes further on the season as a whole: "up to 75% of the current season's growth can be harvested at one time." So take a third off a rosemary or a thyme, and don't be shy with basil or cilantro. Cut stems just above a leaf pair so the plant branches and regrows — the same cut described in our pruning and training guide. A harvest done right is also a pruning session; the plant comes back bushier and you harvest again in a few weeks.

Know Which Kind of Herb You're Drying

Herbs split into two camps, and the split predicts almost everything about how they dry:

TypeExamplesHow they handle drying
Low-moisture, sturdyThyme, oregano, rosemary, sage, marjoram, bayDry easily by any method, keep most of their flavor
High-moisture, tenderBasil, mint, parsley, cilantro, chives, dillSlow to dry, prone to browning and mold; several are honestly better frozen

Thyme dried on a shelf tastes close to fresh thyme. Dried cilantro tastes like disappointment no matter what you do — freeze it instead. NC State Extension's method is the safe default: chop it coarsely, pack generous pinches into a water-filled ice cube tray, freeze, and move the cubes to a bag or airtight container. Basil is the borderline case: it dries acceptably if you dry it fast, but it also freezes beautifully as pesto or blanched whole leaves.

Herbs in oil are a botulism hazard the moment they leave the freezer. Oil creates the oxygen-free environment Clostridium botulinum needs, and fresh herbs carry the spores in from the soil. Oregon State Extension (SP 50-701, Herbs and Vegetables in Oil) is unambiguous: home-made herb-in-oil mixtures must be refrigerated and used within four days, or frozen for longer storage, and never held at room temperature — home acidification is not a safe substitute. If you do freeze herbs in oil, label the container with the date it was made and the four-day use-by date that starts the moment it thaws. Freezing in water instead sidesteps the whole problem.

Air Drying: Slow, Free, and Right for the Sturdy Herbs

This page used to claim air drying preserves more of the delicate oils than any heated method, then argued two sections later that tender herbs do better with heat. Both cannot be true, and we could not source the first. Treat it as a trade-off instead: air drying for the low-moisture, sturdy herbs, where slow and cool costs nothing; a low dehydrator run for the tender, high-moisture ones, where speed is the thing keeping mould out of the jar. What air drying needs is the right room: dark, dry, and with some air movement. Light bleaches leaves and degrades oils; humidity is the enemy of the whole project. We are not going to give you a target temperature and humidity for the room: no herb-drying publication we could find states one, and the 60-70°F / 45-55% figures this page used to carry would have had tender herbs sitting damp for days, which is the mould risk the rest of this section warns about. The requirement is dark, dry relative to the rest of the house, and moving air — a closet or spare room works, a steamy kitchen doesn't. A cheap digital hygrometer, the same instrument the grow tent articles here lean on, settles the question of whether your chosen spot qualifies.

Two ways to hold the herbs while they dry:

  • Hang small bundles. Five to eight stems per bundle, tied with a rubber band (string loosens as stems shrink; a rubber band tightens with them), hung upside down. Keep bundles small — the middle of a fat bundle stays damp and molds from the inside out.
  • Use a mesh drying rack. For loose leaves, short stems, or anything too floppy to bundle, a stack of mesh shelves gives every leaf airflow on both sides. Collapsible hanging racks take up almost no space when stored. Buy the layer count you'll actually fill — a hobby garden rarely gets past four, whatever the eight-layer listings suggest.

Expect 5-10 days (Oregon State). Sturdy herbs finish at the fast end; basil and mint take the full stretch. Check bundles every few days and pull out anything showing dark spots before it spreads.

Faster Methods: Dehydrator and Oven

A food dehydrator is the best option for high-moisture herbs like basil that tend to brown during slow air drying. Oregon State's figures: preheat to 90-100°F (32-38°C) and dry 1-3 hours, or until the herbs are dry and crumble easily. That is cooler and much faster than the 95-105°F for 2-6 hours this page used to give. If the trays have wide gaps, a sheet of parchment keeps small leaves from falling through.

The oven is the method of last resort — extension guidance is that oven drying is simply not recommended. A household oven's lowest setting is far above the 90-100°F a dehydrator holds, hot enough to cook off a good share of the flavor you're trying to save. (This page used to give 170°F as the floor of a typical oven; we could not source that figure, so the reason stands and the number is gone.) If it's what you have: lowest setting, door propped open a crack, leaves in a single layer on parchment, checked every 15 minutes. They can go from damp to toasted in the time it takes to answer a text.

Microwave drying works better than the oven for small amounts, but only with the published protocol. Oregon State Extension (SP 50-921, Drying Herbs): never dry more than 1-2 cups at a time, and layer the herbs in a single layer between two sheets of plain white paper towels — not recycled ones, which often contain scraps of metal that can arc and catch fire. Time by wattage rather than by habit: for one cup of herbs, 1-2 minutes on high in a 700-1,200 W oven, 2-4 minutes in a 650-700 W oven, 3-6 minutes in a 500-600 W oven. Stir every 30 seconds after the first minute until the leaves are almost dry, then let them finish at room temperature. Parsley, chives, thyme and sage are the herbs this method is usually used on.

The doneness test: a fully dried leaf crumbles when rubbed between your fingers, and a fully dried stem snaps with a clean crack instead of bending. The stem test is the one that counts — stems hold moisture longest, and one bendy stem sealed in a jar can mold the whole batch.

Storage: Whole Leaves, Airtight, Dark

Strip the dried leaves off their stems and store them whole in airtight glass jars. Every time a leaf is crumbled, it exposes more surface area and bleeds oils; herbs crumbled at storage time taste flat months before whole-leaf ones do. Crush them in your palm as you add them to the pan, not before.

Keep jars somewhere dark and cool — a cabinet, not the shelf above the stove, which is convenient and also the worst spot in the kitchen for both heat and light. Label with the herb and the month; dried herbs don't spoil, they just fade, and most are past their best after about a year.

For the first week after jarring, check the jar daily for condensation on the glass. Any fog means the batch wasn't fully dry — pull it out and give it another few days on the rack before it turns into mold. This one check has saved more of my batches than everything else combined.

A Reasonable First Run

Start with oregano or thyme if you have it — they're nearly impossible to ruin and the payoff over store-bought is obvious. Cut a third of the plant mid-morning, rubber-band it into two skinny bundles, hang them in a closet, and forget them for ten days. Snap a stem. If it cracks, jar it whole and cook with it that week while your confidence is high. Basil and the tender herbs can wait for round two.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time of day to harvest herbs?

Early morning, once the dew has dried but before the heat of the day — that is NC State's wording. Under a grow light it means the first couple of hours after the light comes on. Basil is the exception and takes an afternoon cut better than most.

How long does it take herbs to air dry?

Oregon State gives 5-10 days for air drying. Sturdy herbs like thyme and oregano finish at the fast end. Thick, moist leaves like basil and mint are the slow ones, and are also the ones most likely to mould on a shelf — a dehydrator at 90-100°F for 1-3 hours suits them better.

How do I know when herbs are fully dry?

Leaves should crumble when rubbed and stems should snap cleanly rather than bend. If a stem flexes without snapping, there's still moisture inside and jarring it now risks mold.

Should I wash herbs before drying them?

Indoor-grown herbs usually don't need it unless you've sprayed them with anything. If you do rinse, shake them out well and let them dry on a towel for a few hours first — bundling wet herbs is the fastest route to mold.

How long do dried herbs keep their flavor?

Stored whole (not crumbled) in airtight jars away from light and heat, most dried herbs hold good flavor for about a year. Crumble or grind them only when you use them.
