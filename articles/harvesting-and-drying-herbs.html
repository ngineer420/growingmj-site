Harvesting and Drying Herbs at Home

When and how to harvest indoor herbs, and how to dry and store them so they keep their flavor — hang drying, rack drying, oven and dehydrator methods compared.

Indoor Herbs & Kitchen Gardens Updated August 20, 2026 8 min read Contains affiliate links

A basil plant pinched every couple of weeks will out-produce a household's pesto habit by midsummer, and then keep going. What happens to the surplus is decided by three things: when you cut it, how fast it dries, and what you put it in. Get those wrong and you get a jar of green dust that smells like hay.

When to Cut, and How Much

Cut before the plant flowers. That part is well supported. The reason usually given for it is not, and this page used to repeat it: that oil content "peaks just before flowering." For the one family where extension actually publishes a harvest stage, the claim runs backwards. Purdue's mint page puts optimum oil yield and quality at "approximately 10% of a peppermint crop is in full bloom," and for spearmint says harvest "begins when all plants are in full bloom."1 Oregon State's peppermint guide is blunter still: "Oil concentration reaches its peak during bloom."2 A cultivated trial of five Lamiaceae species found the same shape — highest oil yields at full flowering, with spearmint's July inflorescences at 5.40 mL/100 g dry weight against 1.63 for April leaves.3

None of which changes what you should do, because the reason to cut early was never the oil. NC State: "Harvest herbs before flowering, otherwise, leaf production declines."4 Illinois says the same and adds that deadheading buds as they appear keeps new leaves coming.5 A flowering herb stops making the leaves you want. That is the whole argument, and it survives the oil chemistry pointing the other way. Peppermint growers cut at 10% bloom rather than full bloom for a third reason again — past that point the oil picks up menthofuran and the flavor goes off.2

Time of day is where the sources genuinely disagree, so here are both. NC State and Illinois want the morning: "harvest early in the morning, after the dew dries, but before the heat of the day."45 Under a grow light that means the first couple of hours after the light comes on. Rutgers, writing about basil specifically, goes the other way: morning is cooler but dewy, so "when possible, a late afternoon harvest can provide the cooler temperatures in addition to dry leaves."6 Both are chasing the same two conditions — cool leaves and dry ones — and indoors under a light you usually have both all day, which makes this the least important decision on the page.

How much to take is where this page used to be wrong. "Never more than a third" is a perennial rule that got applied to everything. Illinois splits it: annual herbs "can be cut back 50-75 percent and still recover", while with perennials you "remove about one-third of the growth at any one time."5 NC State puts a ceiling on the whole season's growth rather than the plant: "up to 75% of the current season's growth can be harvested at one time."4 So: a third off a rosemary or a thyme, and no timidity with basil or cilantro. Cut just above a leaf pair so the plant branches, which is the same cut described in our pruning and training guide. A harvest done right is a pruning session that happens to fill a bowl.

Know Which Kind of Herb You're Drying

Herbs split into two camps, and the split predicts almost everything about how they dry:

TypeExamplesHow they handle drying
Low-moisture, sturdyThyme, oregano, rosemary, sage, marjoram, bayDry easily by any method, keep most of their flavor
High-moisture, tenderBasil, mint, parsley, cilantro, chives, dillSlow to dry, prone to browning and mold; several are honestly better frozen

Thyme dried on a shelf tastes close to fresh thyme. Dried cilantro tastes like disappointment no matter what you do — freeze it instead. NC State Extension's method is the safe default: chop it coarsely, pack generous pinches into a water-filled ice cube tray, freeze, and move the cubes to a bag or airtight container.4 Basil is the borderline case: it dries acceptably if you dry it fast, but it also freezes beautifully as pesto or blanched whole leaves.

Herbs in oil are a botulism hazard the moment they leave the freezer. Oil creates the oxygen-free environment Clostridium botulinum needs, and fresh herbs carry the spores in from the soil. Oregon State Extension (SP 50-701, Herbs and Vegetables in Oil) is unambiguous: home-made herb-in-oil mixtures must be refrigerated and used within four days, or frozen for longer storage, and never held at room temperature — home acidification is not a safe substitute.7 If you do freeze herbs in oil, label the container with the date it was made and the four-day use-by that starts the moment it thaws. Freezing in water instead sidesteps the whole problem.

Air Drying Suits the Sturdy Herbs

This page used to claim air drying preserves more of the delicate oils than any heated method, then argued two sections later that tender herbs do better with heat. Both cannot be true and we could not source the first, so treat it as a trade-off rather than a ranking. Air dry the low-moisture, sturdy herbs, where slow and cool costs nothing. Run the tender, high-moisture ones through a low dehydrator, where speed is the thing keeping mould out of the jar.

What air drying needs is the right room: dark, dry, and with some air movement. Light bleaches leaves and degrades oils; humidity is the enemy of the whole project. What we are not going to give you is a target temperature and humidity for that room. No herb-drying publication we could find states one, and the 60-70°F / 45-55% figures this page used to carry would have had tender herbs sitting damp for days — the exact mould risk the rest of this section warns about. Dark, drier than the rest of the house, and moving air. A closet or spare room works; a steamy kitchen doesn't. A cheap digital hygrometer, the same instrument the grow tent articles here lean on, settles whether your chosen spot qualifies.

Two ways to hold the herbs while they dry:

  • Hang small, loose bundles. Tie them with a rubber band rather than string, which loosens as the stems shrink while a rubber band tightens with them, and hang them upside down. Sources give no stem count and there isn't one to give — Oregon State just says small bunches, "(large amounts will mold)".8 The requirement is that air can get between the stems. The middle of a fat bundle stays damp and molds from the inside out.
  • Use a mesh drying rack. For loose leaves, short stems, or anything too floppy to bundle, a stack of mesh shelves gives every leaf airflow on both sides. Collapsible hanging racks take up almost no space when stored. Buy the layer count you'll actually fill — a hobby garden rarely gets past four, whatever the eight-layer listings suggest.

How long it takes depends on which of those two you picked, and the sources are further apart than you would expect. Oregon State's 5-10 days is for herbs laid flat on a tray, not hung.8 Illinois, describing bunches hung in a garage or attic, says "it may take up to a month for herbs to dry completely."9 Both are right about their own method; a tray in a warm room beats a bundle in a cold one. Check every few days either way, and pull out anything showing dark spots before it spreads.

The Dehydrator, the Oven, and the Microwave

A dehydrator is the right tool for the high-moisture herbs that brown during slow air drying. Set the thermostat to 95-115°F, or as high as 125°F where the room itself is humid, spread the herbs one layer deep, and expect 1-4 hours. They are done when the leaves crumble and the stems break rather than bend.8 A correction to this page's own history is buried in those numbers. It used to give 90-100°F for 1-3 hours, citing Oregon State, which is what Oregon State said in the 2013 printing of SP 50-921. The January 2025 revision of the same publication raised the band and widened the time, and dropped the "below 100°F" ceiling entirely in favour of "keep the temperature low." Same publisher, same pub number, different numbers — check the revision date on anything you are about to trust. If the trays have wide gaps, a sheet of parchment keeps small leaves from falling through.

The oven is the awkward one, and this page previously misreported it: we said extension advises against oven drying, when what Oregon State advises against is sun drying.8 Oregon State simply does not cover conventional ovens. Two others do. Illinois puts the ideal oven band at 90-110°F for 3-4 hours, and says plainly that ovens "oftentimes cannot be set low enough", with the door left slightly open as the workaround; NC State just says the lowest setting possible.94 So the oven is not forbidden, it is badly suited: the target is a temperature most household ovens cannot hold. If it is what you have, lowest setting, door propped, single layer on parchment, checked every 15 minutes. They go from damp to toasted in the time it takes to answer a text. (The 170°F this page used to give as the floor of a typical oven is gone. We could not source it.)

The microwave beats the oven for small amounts, with the published protocol and not without it. Never more than 1-2 cups at a time; lay them one layer deep between two sheets of plain white paper towels — not recycled ones, which often contain scraps of metal that can arc and catch fire; about 1-2 minutes on high for a cup of herbs; stir every 30 seconds after the first minute, then let them finish at room temperature.8 Illinois adds the warning that goes with it — scorching, and the possibility of starting a fire.9 Parsley, celery leaves, chives, thyme and sage are the herbs Oregon State names for this method. The wattage table this page used to carry (1-2 minutes at 700-1,200 W, and so on) came from the 2013 printing and is not in the current one, so it has gone with it.

The doneness test: a fully dried leaf crumbles when rubbed between your fingers, and a fully dried stem snaps with a clean crack instead of bending. The stem test is the one that counts — stems hold moisture longest, and one bendy stem sealed in a jar can mold the whole batch.

Store Them Whole

Strip the dried leaves off their stems and store them whole in airtight glass jars.6 Every time a leaf is crumbled it exposes more surface area and bleeds oils, so herbs crumbled at storage time taste flat months before whole-leaf ones do. Crush them in your palm as you add them to the pan, not before.

Keep jars somewhere dark and cool — a cabinet, not the shelf above the stove, which is convenient and also the worst spot in the kitchen for both heat and light. Label with the herb and the month. Dried herbs don't spoil, they fade, and the sourced shelf life is six months to a year, not the flat "about a year" this page used to give.6

Then, for the first week after jarring, look at the glass every day. Any fog on the inside means the batch wasn't fully dry: pull it out and give it another few days on the rack before it turns into mold. It is the cheapest check on this page and the one people skip.

Ten Days in a Closet

Cut a third off an oregano or a thyme in the morning — they are the two hardest herbs on this page to ruin, and the payoff over the supermarket jar is not subtle. Rubber-band the stems into two skinny bundles, hang them in a closet, and forget about them for ten days. Then snap one. If it cracks, jar the batch whole and cook with it that week while your confidence is high. Basil and the rest of the tender herbs can wait for round two, when you know what dry feels like.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time of day to harvest herbs?

Early morning, once the dew has dried but before the heat of the day — that is NC State's wording, and Illinois agrees. Under a grow light it means the first couple of hours after the light comes on. Rutgers dissents for basil, preferring a late afternoon cut on the grounds that it gets you cool leaves and dry ones at the same time.

How long does it take herbs to air dry?

It depends on the method, and the sources are far apart. Oregon State gives 5-10 days for herbs dried flat on a tray; Illinois says bunches hung in a garage or attic may take up to a month. Thick, moist leaves like basil and mint are the slow ones and the ones most likely to mould on a shelf — a dehydrator at 95-115°F for 1-4 hours suits them better.

How do I know when herbs are fully dry?

Leaves should crumble when rubbed and stems should snap cleanly rather than bend. If a stem flexes without snapping, there's still moisture inside and jarring it now risks mold.

Should I wash herbs before drying them?

Indoor-grown herbs usually don't need it unless you've sprayed them with anything. If you do rinse, shake them out well and let them dry on a towel for a few hours first — bundling wet herbs is the fastest route to mold.

How long do dried herbs keep their flavor?

Six months to a year, stored whole (not crumbled) in airtight jars away from light and heat. That is Oregon State's range; the flat “about a year” this page used to give was the optimistic end of it. Crumble or grind them only when you use them.
