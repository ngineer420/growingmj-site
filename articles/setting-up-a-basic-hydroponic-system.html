Setting Up a Basic Hydroponic System

A beginner-friendly walkthrough of setting up a deep water culture hydroponic system, including nutrients, pH, and common pitfalls.

Hydroponics Updated August 20, 2026 10 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

What's the easiest hydroponic system for a first-time grower?

Deep water culture (DWC) is the simplest powered starting point — one bucket, one air pump, roots suspended directly in nutrient solution. It is not literally the fewest moving parts of any method: a Kratky-style non-circulating setup has none at all, because it dispenses with the air pump and lets a falling water level leave root above the solution. DWC is the simplest method that keeps the solution aerated for you.

Hydroponics sounds more complicated than it is. At its core, you're growing a plant's roots directly in a nutrient solution instead of soil, which — once it's dialed in — tends to produce faster growth and fewer soil-related pests. The easiest entry point for a beginner is a deep water culture (DWC) system, which is exactly what it sounds like: a plant suspended with its roots hanging into a reservoir of oxygenated, nutrient-rich water.

What You Actually Need

A basic single-bucket DWC setup requires surprisingly little equipment:

  • A light-proof bucket or reservoir (5-gallon buckets are the classic starting point)
  • A net pot that sits in a lid cut to fit, holding your plant and growing medium
  • A growing medium that isn't soil — expanded clay pellets or coco coir are both common, inexpensive choices. If you buy a compressed coir brick, read the expanded volume printed on the packet rather than trusting a rule of thumb — brands differ by nearly threefold on how much a brick makes
  • An air pump and air stone to oxygenate the water
  • Hydroponic nutrients formulated for the growth stage you're at
  • A pH meter and a PPM/EC meter to check nutrient strength

Many beginners skip building this from scratch and start instead with a pre-assembled hydroponic kit that bundles the reservoir, growing chamber, drip ring and air pump together. It's a reasonable way to skip the trial-and-error of sourcing every part yourself. The thing to check before you buy is whether the replacement parts — pump, drip ring, lid — are still sold separately, since that is what decides whether the kit outlives its first failure.

Step 1: Set Up the Reservoir

If you are on treated tap water, deal with it before you mix nutrients — but the "let it sit uncovered for a few hours" advice you will read everywhere, including in an earlier version of this page, fails in two ways.

First, free chlorine takes far longer than a few hours, and it wants agitation rather than patience. UF/IFAS puts it at 24 hours of vigorous aeration — an airstone on an aquarium pump, not a still bucket — and their protocol says that if chlorine is still measurable after 24 hours, wait another 24 and re-test.1 You already own the airstone for this system, so run it in the fill water overnight before the nutrients go in.

Second, and more important: most US municipal water is now treated with chloramine rather than free chlorine, and chloramine does not off-gas the same way. UF/IFAS is explicit that combining chlorine with ammonia "enables the chlorine to remain active and NOT de-gas as easily as chlorine alone", and that after 24 hours of vigorous aeration a chloraminated sample shows greatly reduced but "probably not zero" chlorine, with ammonia still measurable throughout.1 So aeration alone will not clear it, and the standing-bucket ritual quietly does nothing.

Ask your utility which one they use — it is on the annual water quality report every US supplier has to publish. If it is chloramine, a chlorine neutraliser (sodium thiosulfate, sold for aquariums) removes the chlorine portion immediately, and you plan around the residual ammonia rather than pretending it is not there. If it is free chlorine, 24 hours on the airstone does the job.

Cut or drill a hole in the lid sized to snugly hold your net pot, with the bottom of the pot sitting a couple of inches above the current water line so the roots have room to grow down into the reservoir over time.

Step 2: Get the Air Pump Running

Drop the air stone to the bottom of the bucket and connect it to an air pump through airline tubing. A dual-outlet design is worth the small price bump over a single-outlet aquarium pump, since it runs two buckets — or one bucket plus a backup stone — off a single unit. Run it continuously, 24/7, for the life of the system.

It helps to know what the pump is actually buying, because "roots suffocate without it" is vaguer than the literature. Cornell measures the thing directly: lettuce grows satisfactorily at a dissolved oxygen level of at least 4 ppm, and in a pond with no oxygen added at all, DO falls to nearly zero.2 Cornell's plant pathology people set the working target higher still: above 6 ppm, ideally close to saturation at 8-9 ppm, because low dissolved oxygen is one of the conditions that invites Pythium.3 So the air pump is not keeping the roots breathing in some binary sense — it is holding a number, and the number has a comfortable band rather than a cliff. Worth saying too: the Kratky method runs entirely unaerated by design, which is only possible because the falling water level leaves a section of root in air. Aeration is how this system solves the problem, not the only way it can be solved.

Step 3: Mix Your Nutrients

Hydroponic nutrients usually come as a two- or three-part liquid system (often labeled "grow," "bloom," and sometimes a micronutrient supplement). Follow the mixing ratios on the bottle for your plant's current growth stage — these ratios are concentration-specific, and eyeballing them is one of the fastest ways to stunt or burn a plant. Mix nutrients into the reservoir water gradually, stirring as you go.

Step 4: Check pH and Nutrient Strength

This is the step beginners skip most often, and it's the one that causes the most mystery problems later. Use a pH meter to check your reservoir. A hobbyist-grade meter rather than a lab instrument is plenty, as long as it holds calibration for weeks at a time and lets you replace the probe when it eventually drifts. If pH is outside range, small amounts of pH-up or pH-down solution will correct it. Then use an EC/TDS meter to confirm your nutrient concentration matches what the label recommends for your plant's growth stage — too weak and growth stalls, too strong and you risk nutrient burn. Check whether the meter reports raw EC or a converted PPM number, and on which factor: if you have a reading in one and a feed chart in the other, our EC to PPM converter shows all three scales — 500, 640 and 700 — side by side.

What is actually buffering your reservoir: this page used to say there is no buffer in hydroponics, and that is half right in a way that hides the useful part. Oklahoma State puts it that soil acts as a buffer and "this buffer is absent in soilless culture."4 But something is still resisting your pH-down, and it came in with the fill water. UMass names it: alkalinity — dissolved bicarbonate and carbonate — is the measure of water's ability to neutralise acidity, desirable between 0 and 100 ppm as calcium carbonate and optimum at 30-60.5 High pH and high alkalinity are not the same thing and are routinely confused. Practically: on RO or rainwater your pH will swing hard and correct easily, while on hard tap water it will resist your acid and then climb back over a day or two. Oklahoma State's threshold is above 75 ppm alkalinity, at which point the solution pH will keep rising and needs checking more often.4 Your water utility publishes this number annually, and it is worth five minutes to go and find it.

As for what the target range is: the extension sources do not agree on one, and a page that picks a winner is hiding something from you. Oklahoma State publishes it crop by crop: basil and peppers 5.5-6.0, tomato 6.0-6.5, lettuce 6.0-7.0.4 Ohio State gives a single band of 5.5-6.5 as the range where most nutrients are highly available.6 Florida puts lettuce at 6.0-7.0.7 New Hampshire, writing for home growers specifically, says anything between 5.5 and 7.0 is fine.8 Between about 5.5 and 6.5 you are inside every one of those; hitting a decimal point is not the skill being tested.

How often to check, then, is a question with two published answers and one of them is stricter than anything this page has said before. Oklahoma State's instruction is flat: check pH and EC daily, and at the same time of day.4 New Hampshire, writing for home-scale systems, gives no interval at all.8 The reconciling variable is alkalinity: high-alkalinity water pushes pH up faster and earns the daily check, soft water drifts more slowly. Start daily for the first fortnight of any new setup, because that is how you learn which of the two you have.

Two rows this page used to carry are gone: lettuce at 0.8-1.2, which was low against every source above, and a seedling row nobody publishes.

One qualifier matters more than any of those numbers, and almost no popular chart carries it. Cornell states its lettuce target as EC 1150-1250 µS/cm above the source water.2 If your tap water reads 0.6 mS/cm before anything goes in it — and well water often does — then mixing to a raw meter reading of 1.2 is feeding at 0.6, half what you think. Measure the fill water first, once, and write the number on the bucket.

When in doubt, start at the low end. A slightly hungry plant grows slower; an overfed one gets burned tips and a reservoir you have to dump and remix.

Reservoir Temperature, and Both Ways It Bites

The variable that took out my first summer lettuce run was not pH or EC. It was water temperature, and it is the one this page had most wrong.

The published targets cluster tightly. Cornell's plant pathologists give a root-zone target of 68-75 °F.3 Oklahoma State calls 72-75 °F optimal.4 Florida allows a wider 65-80 °F for a home lettuce system.7 This page previously said 65-72 °F, which sits below all three at the top end.

The more useful correction is that cold is not a safe direction. The rule of thumb — "above 75 °F you get pythium" — treats root rot as a single organism with a single threshold, and it is not. Pythium aphanidermatum is favoured by higher temperatures and Pythium dissotocum by lower ones, so chilling a reservoir trades one species for another rather than escaping the genus. Cornell found that chilling ponds to about 68 °F reduced but did not eliminate P. aphanidermatum in baby-leaf spinach.3 Warm water also holds less dissolved oxygen at exactly the moment the roots want more of it, which is the other half of why the warm end bites first.

If the grow space runs hot: keep the reservoir shaded and off warm floors, top off with cool water, and freeze a couple of water bottles to rotate into the bucket on hot afternoons. Low-tech, and much cheaper than a chiller. If it runs cold, the fix is the same thermometer and the opposite instinct — do not congratulate yourself on a 60 °F reservoir.

Step 5: Plant and Monitor

Place your seedling (started in a plug or small cube of growing medium) into the net pot, surrounded by your clay pellets or coco coir for support. For the first several days, keep the water level high enough that the medium stays moist, then gradually lower it as roots grow down toward the reservoir — this "dry period" encourages roots to chase the water down rather than staying shallow.

Ongoing Maintenance

  • Top off water level as it evaporates and gets taken up by the plant.
  • Check and adjust pH and EC, daily at first, at the same time of day.
  • Change the solution — on an interval the sources genuinely dispute. Oklahoma State advises replacing the nutrient solution completely every two weeks, on the reasoning that ratios drift and sodium chloride accumulates every time you top off.4 New Hampshire scales it to the system instead: a recirculating system might be changed once or twice a year, a very small raft system every couple of months, a very large one not for years.8 A five-gallon bucket with one thirsty plant is the small end of that spectrum, where you are replacing a large fraction of the volume with plain top-off water every few days, so lean toward Oklahoma State's fortnight.
  • Watch roots through the net pot or a clear section of reservoir — healthy roots are white or cream-colored; brown, slimy roots are a sign of a rot problem, usually from insufficient oxygen or water that's too warm.

When Something Goes Wrong

DWC problems announce themselves quickly, which is honestly one of its virtues — you find out in days, not weeks. The common ones:

SymptomLikely causeFix
Brown, slimy roots with a sour smellRoot rot — warm water, low oxygen, or light getting into the reservoirTrim the slimy roots, full reservoir change, add an air stone, cool the water, block every light leak
Plant wilting even though roots sit in waterRoots can't drink — almost always rot or oxygen starvation, not "thirst"Check root color first; treat as above
White crust climbing the net potNutrient salts wicking up and dryingCosmetic; rinse it off during reservoir changes
pH drops (or climbs) every single dayNormal in a small reservoir, and faster on high-alkalinity fill waterAdjust in small doses; a larger reservoir swings less for the same plant. There is no published swing-per-day threshold, so judge it by whether you can hold the range
Green film on reservoir wallsAlgae — light is reaching the nutrient waterCover any clear parts; algae competes for oxygen and feeds gnat larvae

The overall pattern: nearly every DWC failure is really an oxygen, temperature, or light-leak problem wearing a disguise. Keep the water cool, dark, and bubbling and it's hard to kill anything.

What This Costs, Start to Finish

We have taken the dollar figures off this section, because nobody publishes prices for hobby growing equipment and the ones that were here were invented. The proportion is the part worth knowing anyway, and it is the opposite of what people expect: on a single-bucket build, the two meters cost about as much as every other part combined. Bucket, lid, net pot, clay pellets, air pump, line, stones and a two-part nutrient set are all cheap; a pH meter and an EC meter are each in the same bracket as all of them together.

That is exactly why they get skipped, and skipping them is the classic first-timer mistake: it turns every problem into a guess. The pre-built kits do not include meters either, so the real comparison is kit convenience against a modest saving on the parts you would have assembled yourself.

Why Start With DWC Specifically

Other hydroponic methods — ebb and flow, nutrient film technique, aeroponics — offer advantages at larger scale, but they add moving parts: pumps on timers, more plumbing, more points of failure. A single-bucket DWC system has exactly one moving part — the air pump — and is forgiving enough to learn the fundamentals of pH, nutrient strength, and root health before scaling up to anything more complex. It is worth saying that this is not the same as having the fewest moving parts available: the Kratky method has zero, running unaerated by design and relying on the solution level falling away from the upper roots. It is simpler still, but it gives you nothing to adjust when something goes wrong, which is the opposite of what a first system should do. Note also that if you buy a kit rather than build a bucket, several popular ones add a drip ring fed by the same air pump, so "one moving part" describes the build described here rather than every product sold as DWC.

Grow lettuce or basil first. Both are forgiving of EC drift, both mature fast enough that you see a full cycle inside two months, and both show you clearly when something is off. Tomatoes in DWC work fine, but a full-size plant drinks gallons a day and turns the relaxed weekly reservoir routine into a daily one — which is a different hobby, and one worth choosing on purpose.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the easiest hydroponic system for a first-time grower?

Deep water culture (DWC) is the simplest powered starting point — one bucket, one air pump, roots suspended directly in nutrient solution. It is not literally the fewest moving parts of any method: a Kratky-style non-circulating setup has none at all, because it dispenses with the air pump and lets a falling water level leave root above the solution. DWC is the simplest method that keeps the solution aerated for you.

How often should I check pH in a hydroponic reservoir?

There is no single published interval. Oklahoma State says daily, at the same time of day. New Hampshire, writing for home growers, gives none. The variable that reconciles them is your water's alkalinity: above about 75 ppm, pH climbs faster and earns the daily check. Test daily for the first fortnight of a new setup and you will find out which case you are in.

What pH range should I target for hydroponics?

Between 5.5 and 6.5 satisfies every extension source we could find, though none of them agree exactly: Oklahoma State publishes it crop by crop (5.5-6.0 for basil and peppers, 6.0-7.0 for lettuce), Ohio State gives 5.5-6.5, and New Hampshire tells home growers anything from 5.5 to 7.0 is fine. Hitting a decimal point is not the skill being tested.

How do I know if my hydroponic roots are healthy?

Healthy roots are white or cream-colored. Brown, slimy roots signal a rot problem, almost always caused by insufficient oxygen or water that's running too warm.

How often should I fully change the nutrient reservoir?

The sources disagree, and the system size is why. Oklahoma State advises replacing it completely every two weeks, because ratios drift and salts build up every time you top off. New Hampshire scales it instead: a small raft system every couple of months, a large one potentially for years. A five-gallon bucket is the small, fast-drifting end of that range, so a fortnight is the right instinct.
