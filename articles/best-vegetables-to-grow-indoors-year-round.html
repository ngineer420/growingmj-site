Best Vegetables to Grow Indoors Year-Round

The vegetables that actually perform well indoors year-round, sorted by the one thing that decides it: how many hours of direct sun your window really gets.

Indoor Vegetable Gardening Updated August 20, 2026 8 min read Contains affiliate links

The short answer

Can I grow vegetables indoors without a grow light?

Only where the window gives real direct sun. Illinois Extension counts six hours or more of direct sunlight as full sun and four to six as partial shade, and Clemson puts leaf and root crops in the partial-shade group while fruiting vegetables need at least five hours of direct sun and do better on eight to ten. So lettuce, spinach, chard and radish are the honest windowsill crops. Tomatoes and peppers are not.

This page used to open by promising a windowsill and then, four sections later, recommend a fixture built to flower a 4x4 tent. Those are two different articles, and the reason they kept colliding is that the honest answer to "what can I grow indoors?" is not a list of vegetables at all. It's a number: how much light lands where you intend to put the pot. Everything else — container size, days to harvest, whether a tomato is realistic — follows from that.

Count the Hours of Direct Sun First

Extension uses a definition you can check with a kitchen timer. Illinois counts six hours or more of direct sunlight as full sun, and four to six hours as partial shade.1 Clemson splits the crops along the same line: root crops and leaf crops tolerate partial shade, while vegetables grown for their fruits "generally need at least five hours of full, direct sunlight each day, but perform better with eight to ten hours."2

Now go and watch the window. Not "is it bright in here" — direct sun on the spot the pot will sit, timed, on a clear day, in the season you actually plan to grow. Most people find a number they did not expect, because an east window stops delivering at eleven and a north window never starts. Three hours is a real answer and it rules out fruiting crops before you spend anything.

That single measurement sorts the whole article:

  • Four to six hours of direct sun. Partial shade. Leaf and root crops only — lettuce, spinach, chard, radish, green onion.
  • Six hours or more. Full sun by the Illinois definition, which puts fruiting crops just inside Clemson's five-hour floor and well below the eight to ten they prefer. Workable for a compact pepper, marginal for a tomato.
  • Under four hours, or any north-facing room. Nothing on this page performs there on daylight alone. This is where a grow light stops being an upgrade and becomes the premise.

The Windowsill Crops

Radish is the one to start with. It is ready three to five weeks after sowing, it needs soil loosened only about six inches deep, and seedlings are up within ten days, so you find out quickly whether your window is doing anything at all.4 Pull them as soon as they size up; left in the ground they get fibrous and turn strong-tasting.

Lettuce and spinach are the other honest windowsill crops, harvested cut-and-come-again from the outer leaves. The thing that spoils them indoors is heat rather than light: Minnesota puts bolting down to long days, high light intensity and heat, with multiple days above 75 °F enough to send a plant into flower, and describes the resulting flavour as "extreme bitter."5 A sunny sill above a radiator is exactly that condition. If your lettuce turns bitter, look at the thermometer before you look at the watering can.

Green onions are worth a correction. Standing the cut root ends in a glass of water does regrow shoots, and this page used to say it works indefinitely. It doesn't — the plant is spending what's stored in the bulb, not building anything new, and it thins out. Nobody publishes a number for how many rounds you get, so we are not going to invent one. Grown properly from seed, green onions take 60 to 70 days.3

Microgreens are the exception that proves the light point. They're harvested so young that they need very little — Virginia Tech's target is 9 to 12 mol per square metre per day against 20 to 30 for a tomato — but "very little" is not "none," and an earlier version of this page said no grow light was needed, which contradicted our own microgreens guide.6 A cheap LED on a timer is the right answer there, and the full method has its own page.

Under a Light: Buy a DLI, Not a Wattage

Once you're adding light, hours stop being the useful unit and the daily light integral takes over — total photons on a square metre over a day. You get it from an intensity reading with one multiplication:

DLI = PPFD × hours × 0.0036

Virginia Tech's targets make the whole tiering of this article fall out on their own:

CropTarget DLI (mol/m²/day)
Seedlings and cuttings5-10
Microgreens9-12
Lettuce12-17
Spinach14-20
Tomato, cucumber, zucchini20-30

Virginia Cooperative Extension SPES-720, Table 3.6 The page's own caveat applies: the correct DLI "is somewhat subjective and not a fixed value." It also states plainly that candela, lumen, foot-candles, lux, watts and joules are not the units to measure plant light in — which is why the lux comparison this page used to print has gone.

A fruiting crop wants roughly double a leafy one. That is the entire reason the two tiers need different hardware, and it means you can size a fixture instead of guessing at one. Greens at 250 µmol/m²/s for 14 hours come to 12.6 mol — inside lettuce's band, from a LED grow light bar mounted under the shelf above them, which is tens of watts over a two-foot run. Tomatoes at the same 250 for the same 14 hours are at half what they need, and no timer setting fixes that; you need real intensity over the canopy, which is what a full-spectrum LED panel is for. Buy that one on its published PPFD map over the footprint your pots actually occupy, not on headline wattage — and note that a couple of dwarf tomatoes occupy a corner of a shelf, not a 4x4 floor. Containers: Smaller Than You Have Been Told

Temperature Is the Second Gate

Get the light right and fruiting crops fail on temperature next, which is the trap in a warm room or a sealed tent. Clemson is precise about tomatoes: the optimum daytime range is 70 to 85 °F, and several days of nights above 70 °F or days above 85 °F "will cause the plants to abort flowers," because "the pollen becomes tacky and nonviable, preventing proper pollination from taking place." The cold end matters too — nights below 55 °F prevent pollination as well.7 An earlier version of this page said pollen "goes sterile above 90°F," which was both the wrong threshold and a stronger claim than the source makes.

Peppers have their own band and their own honest gap. Minnesota lists dry soil, temperatures above 90 °F, and night temperatures below 60 °F or above 70 °F as things that weaken plant growth, and separately notes that hot days combined with hot nights make pepper flowers drop. It does not offer a mechanism for the flower drop, so neither will we. It also gives the timing that catches people out indoors: start pepper seed about eight weeks before planting out, earlier than you would start tomatoes.8

Pollination itself is the easiest part, despite its reputation. Clemson: tomato flowers "are pollinated when shaken by movement such as wind. Insects are not necessary for tomato flowers to be pollinated."7 Indoors you are the wind. Flick each open cluster with a finger every day or two while flowers are out — some people hold a running electric toothbrush against the stem instead, which is the same trick with a battery in it. That is the whole technique. We cut the anecdote that used to sit here as proof of it, because one pepper plant is not evidence of anything.

Blossom End Rot, and What Actually Fixes It

A page recommending container tomatoes ought to mention the disorder container tomatoes get, and this one never did. Blossom-end rot is the sunken, leathery brown patch on the bottom of the fruit, and Maryland describes it as "a common nutritional disorder of tomato, pepper, eggplant, pumpkin, squash, and watermelon that is caused by a shortage of calcium in enlarging fruits."9

The useful part is what it lists as the things that cause that shortage: low soil pH, low calcium levels, inconsistent watering, shallow watering or drought, and heavy nitrogen feeding. Three of those five are watering, and the sentence to take away is Maryland's own: "Regular, deep watering will alleviate the problem if calcium levels in the soil are adequate." It also says spraying affected plants with calcium chloride offers only temporary relief, which is worth knowing before you buy a bottle of it.9 In a pot indoors, "inconsistent watering" is the default state unless you make a point of it, so this is a watering problem wearing a nutrient costume — but not, as some guides have it, proof that soil calcium is irrelevant. Maryland lists it as a factor and recommends gypsum mixed into the planting hole.

What Not to Bother With

An honest exclusion list saves more money than any recommendation. Zucchini and squash want the 20-30 mol/m²/day of a fruiting crop delivered across a plant that sprawls a metre wide.6 Lighting that footprint to that intensity, for one of the cheapest vegetables in the shop, is the worst yield-per-watt on this page. (The old version of this line claimed a zucchini produces "less than a $2 haul," which was made up; the footprint argument stands on its own.) Vining melons and cucumbers are doable under a real light with a trellis, but they are a project rather than a starter crop. Potatoes occupy a grow bag for months to produce something sold by the sack.

Carrots deserve a longer answer, because they nearly work. Minnesota attributes forked and split roots to rocky, heavy or compacted soil and to drought — which is a precise description of a shallow pot of dense mix that dries out between waterings.10 Grow the short, stump-rooted types rather than long ones, in loose mix, watered consistently, and accept 75 to 80 days for the trouble.3

Watering: A Container Is Not a Garden Bed

Clemson is more insistent about this than most beginners expect: because the soil volume is small, containers "can dry out very quickly," daily or even twice-daily watering may be necessary, and you should check them at least once a day and twice on hot, dry or windy days. Water until it runs out of the drainage holes; the soil "should never be soggy or have water standing on top of it."2

Consistency, not just volume, is what the crops on this page care about. Split carrot roots, bitter lettuce and blossom-end rot are three different symptoms of the same swing between bone dry and soaked. A capacitive soil moisture meter reads water content rather than conductivity, which the battery-free dial probes do not — those drift toward "wet" as fertiliser salts build up. A self-watering planter flattens the swing by design, which is worth more to a tomato than to almost anything else you can grow indoors.

Keeping It Going

"Year-round" comes from succession, not from any single long-lived plant. Sow a new tray of lettuce or radish every week or two and the next batch is halfway through its cycle when you cut the last one; a radish's three-to-five-week turnaround is short enough to keep that rhythm going without much planning.4 A multi-variety vegetable seed collection is a cheap way to run several of the partial-shade crops against each other in one cycle and find out what your particular window does. Check the packet for a packed-for date in the current year; we've dropped the claim this page used to make about those seeds being heirloom and open-pollinated, because the link is a search and whatever it returns changes weekly.

If you take one thing from this page, take the timer. Count the hours of direct sun on the spot where the pot will go, before you buy the pot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I grow vegetables indoors without a grow light?

Only where the window gives real direct sun. Illinois Extension counts six hours or more of direct sunlight as full sun and four to six as partial shade, and Clemson puts leaf and root crops in the partial-shade group while fruiting vegetables need at least five hours of direct sun and do better on eight to ten. So lettuce, spinach, chard and radish are the honest windowsill crops. Tomatoes and peppers are not.

Can you really grow tomatoes indoors year-round?

Yes, under a grow light rather than on a windowsill, and in less pot than people expect — Illinois Extension puts a cherry or patio tomato in a one-gallon container and a standard tomato in three. You will also have to shake the flowers: Clemson notes tomato flowers are pollinated by movement such as wind, and insects are not required, so a daily flick of each open cluster does the whole job.

How do I get a continuous vegetable harvest indoors instead of one big batch?

Succession planting — sow a new tray of lettuce or radish every week or two, so a new batch is partway through its cycle by the time you cut the last one. Radishes are ready three to five weeks after sowing, which makes them the easiest crop to keep a rotation going with.

Why aren't my indoor vegetables producing much?

Usually light, and the useful way to check is the daily light integral rather than hours. Virginia Tech puts lettuce at 12 to 17 mol per square metre per day and tomato, cucumber and zucchini at 20 to 30 — a fruiting crop wants roughly double a leafy one, and a plant that grows but never sets fruit is often sitting at a leafy-crop light level.

Do container vegetables need different watering than in-ground vegetables?

Yes, and much more often. Clemson says containers can dry out very quickly, that daily or even twice-daily watering may be necessary, and that you should check them at least once a day. Water until it runs out of the drainage holes, and don't let the surface stay soggy.
