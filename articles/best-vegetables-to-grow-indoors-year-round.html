Best Vegetables to Grow Indoors Year-Round

Indoor Vegetable Gardening

Last updated: August 20, 2026

Photo by cristina.sanvito, licensed under CC BY 2.0

Herbs get most of the attention in indoor gardening, but a surprising number of actual vegetables do well indoors too — some without any grow light at all, and others with nothing more than a basic LED setup. The trick is picking vegetables that are naturally compact, fast-maturing, or shallow-rooted, since those are the traits that make a plant tolerate a pot and a windowsill instead of a full garden bed.

The Easiest Tier: Start Here

These are the vegetables most likely to succeed on a first attempt, even with average light:

  • Leafy greens (spinach, lettuce, kale, arugula) — fast-growing, shallow-rooted, and tolerant of the lower light levels a windowsill provides. Many can be harvested "cut and come again," snipping outer leaves while the plant keeps producing.
  • Microgreens — technically vegetable seedlings harvested young, microgreens are about as close to foolproof as indoor growing gets. Almost no pot depth needed — an inch of mix is plenty. They do want a light on a timer, though: Utah State runs them at 18 hours on and 6 off, and this page previously said no grow light was needed, which contradicted our own microgreens guide. Harvest runs from 6 days for arugula to 21-28 for cilantro. (They're worth their own writeup — see growing microgreens indoors for the full tray-to-harvest method.)
  • Radishes — one of the few root vegetables that actually works well in a container, since they mature in as little as 25-30 days and don't need much depth.
  • Green onions and scallions — can be regrown indefinitely from kitchen scraps: place the root end in a shallow glass of water, and new green shoots appear within days.

The Next Tier: Needs a Real Grow Light

These vegetables can be grown indoors year-round, but they're more light-hungry and benefit noticeably from a dedicated grow light rather than a windowsill:

  • Cherry and dwarf tomato varieties — full-size tomato plants are impractical indoors, but compact or "patio" varieties bred specifically for containers can fruit indoors under a strong light. Expect to hand-pollinate by gently shaking the plant or brushing flowers with a small brush, since there are no bees indoors to do it for you.
  • Peppers (hot and sweet) — naturally compact, tolerant of container life, and often available in dwarf varieties bred for windowsill growing.
  • Bush-variety beans — compact, don't need a trellis the way pole beans do, and produce reasonably well in the 2-3 gallon container given in the depth table below, under strong light.

Light is the real limiting factor: almost every "vegetables don't grow well indoors" complaint traces back to insufficient light, not soil or watering. Match the fixture to the tier, though. Greens, radishes and scallions are served by a modest LED grow light bar mounted under the shelf above them — tens of watts over a two-foot run, not a tent fixture. Fruiting vegetables are the ones that need a real light, run 14-16 hours a day, and the number to shop on there is coverage rather than headline wattage: buy a full-spectrum LED panel only with a published PPFD map for the footprint your pots actually occupy, because a tomato lit strongly on one side grows lopsided and fruits unevenly.

Container Depth: The Spec That Decides Success

Indoors, the pot is the plant's entire world, and undersized containers are the quiet reason behind most "it grew but never produced" outcomes. Minimums that actually work, not the minimums on the seed packet:

CropMinimum containerDepth that matters
Lettuce, arugula, spinach1-2 quarts per plant6 inches
RadishesAny wide tray or pot6 inches
Bush beans2-3 gallons8-10 inches
Compact peppers3-5 gallons10-12 inches
Dwarf/patio tomatoes5 gallons, not negotiable12+ inches

The tomato line is the one people fight. A "patio" tomato in a 2-gallon pot will grow, flower, set a handful of fruit, and then stall while drying out twice a day. The same plant in a 5-gallon bucket is a different organism. When a fruiting plant disappoints indoors and the light is genuinely strong, the pot is the next suspect.

Hand-Pollination Takes Ten Seconds, Not a Paintbrush

The pollination warning scares people off fruiting crops unnecessarily. Tomatoes and peppers are self-fertile — each flower has everything it needs, and outdoors it's mostly wind and bee buzz that shakes the pollen loose. Indoors, you are the wind: flick each open flower cluster with a finger, or touch an electric toothbrush to the stem behind the flowers for a second, every day or two while flowers are open. That's the entire technique. My first indoor pepper plant dropped its first dozen flowers before I started doing this; it barely dropped one after. If flowers keep dropping despite pollination, look at temperature instead — pollen goes sterile in sustained heat above about 90°F and sets poorly below 60°F at night, both of which happen inside warm tents and cold windowsills respectively.

Not Worth Growing Indoors (Sorry)

An honest list saves money. Full-size tomatoes want more root volume and light than any hobby setup delivers. Corn and squash are space-for-yield disasters indoors — a zucchini plant fills a 4x4 tent to produce less than a $2 farmers-market haul. Vining melons and cucumbers can be done under strong light with a trellis, but they're a project, not a starter crop. Long carrots need 12+ inches of loose, rock-free depth; stick to stump-rooted varieties like Paris Market if you want carrots at all. Potatoes technically work in a grow bag and take months to yield what a grocery store sells for a dollar. Grow the things that are expensive, perishable, or better fresh: greens, herbs, cherry tomatoes, and hot peppers.

Getting Started

If you're new to growing vegetables indoors, a multi-variety vegetable seed collection is a low-risk way to try several of the easiest options — lettuce, radish, and a leafy green or two — in the same growing cycle, so you can see what does best in your space before committing pots and a grow light to a single crop. Two things to check on the packet: a packed-for date in the current year, and the words "open-pollinated" if you intend to save seed from whatever performs best.

Watering Consistency Matters More Indoors

Container-grown vegetables dry out faster than the same plant in garden soil, and they can't send roots deeper to find water the way an in-ground plant can. Inconsistent watering is one of the most common causes of bitter lettuce, split tomatoes, and stunted root vegetables, and the fix is a routine before it is a gadget: lift the pot, and water when it feels light. If you want an instrument, a capacitive soil moisture meter reads water content directly — skip the battery-free analog dials, which respond to fertilizer salts as much as to water. A self-watering planter smooths out the swings between bone-dry and soaked that vegetables tolerate far worse than most houseplants do, which is especially useful for tomatoes, since inconsistent water is also what causes fruit to split near harvest.

Rotating Crops for a True Year-Round Harvest

The "year-round" part of indoor vegetable gardening comes from succession planting rather than any single long-lived plant. Sow a new tray of lettuce or radish seeds every one to two weeks, and by the time your first tray is harvested, the next is already partway through its cycle. This staggered approach keeps a steady, small harvest coming in instead of one large harvest followed by empty pots for a month.

A Realistic Starting Combination

If you only try one combination to start, a windowsill tray of cut-and-come-again lettuce plus a regrowing jar of green onions requires no grow light, almost no maintenance, and gives you a genuine, repeatable sense of what "growing your own food indoors" actually feels like before you invest in lights and containers for anything more ambitious.

Frequently Asked Questions

What vegetables grow best indoors without a grow light?

Leafy greens (spinach, lettuce, kale, arugula), radishes, and green onions regrown from scraps all do reasonably well with just good windowsill light. Microgreens are the one on that list that really wants a light on a timer rather than a window — Utah State runs them at 18 hours on, 6 off.

Can you really grow tomatoes indoors year-round?

Yes, but stick to compact or "patio" dwarf varieties bred for containers, grown under a strong dedicated grow light, and be ready to hand-pollinate by gently shaking the plant since there are no bees indoors.

How do I get a continuous vegetable harvest indoors instead of one big batch?

Use succession planting — sow a new tray of lettuce or radish seeds every one to two weeks so a new batch is partway through its cycle by the time the previous one is harvested.

Why aren't my indoor vegetables producing much?

Almost always insufficient light. Fruiting vegetables like tomatoes and peppers need considerably more light than leafy greens or herbs, often close to 14-16 hours a day under a dedicated grow light.

Do container vegetables need different watering than in-ground vegetables?

Yes — container-grown vegetables dry out faster and can't send roots deeper for water the way in-ground plants can, so consistent watering matters more, not less, indoors.
