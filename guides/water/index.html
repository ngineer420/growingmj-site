Water Through

Water goes in at the roots on a schedule you choose. It leaves through the leaves at a rate the air chooses.

Watering is two problems that get treated as one. The half you control is how much water goes in and how often. The half you do not control — unless you measure it — is how fast the air pulls that water back out through the leaf. Vapour pressure deficit is the number for the second half, and it explains most of the cases where the same plant on the same schedule thrives in June and sulks in January.

Why a watering schedule is the wrong tool

A calendar cannot know that the room got warmer, that the light is running two hours longer, or that the pot is now full of roots instead of soil. Every reliable watering method is a feedback loop rather than a schedule: lift the pot and feel the weight, push a finger in, or let a wick or a ceramic stake meter it out at whatever rate the plant is actually drinking.

How this loop fails

Overwatering is the single most common way an indoor plant dies, and it does not look like drowning. Roots sitting in saturated soil suffocate, stop taking up water, and the plant wilts — which reads as thirst, so the usual response is more water. That feedback loop, running the wrong way, is why the first fix is always a drainage hole and never a bigger watering can.

Where VPD comes in

At low VPD the air is nearly saturated, transpiration slows, and the plant stops pulling calcium up with the water — which shows up as tip burn that looks like a feeding problem. At high VPD it transpires faster than the roots can supply and the stomata close, so growth stops while the light is still on. A tent can look and smell perfect at either end of that.
